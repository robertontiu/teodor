# The studio manual (studio v8)

This is the painter's reference to the studio: how a canvas is made, how the hand works, how to look, how to measure and how to finish. Each medium has its own page in [`docs/manual/`](manual/) with its tools, supports, behaviour and finishing steps. The current is configured in [`studio/styles.json`](../studio/styles.json) and described in [`docs/styles/`](styles/).

Run every command as `python3 <workdir>/studio.py <command>` from anywhere; `CANVAS=<name>` selects a canvas (default `main`). Files are written next to `studio.py` as `<canvas>_*`.

## The canvas

```
new "#RRGGBB" [W H] [--medium M] [--support S] [--style ID] [--px-per-mm N]
```
Starts a canvas (default 1200x1600 px, oil on canvas) toned to the colour given (the ground: white paper for watercolour, a toned imprimatura for oil, a mid-tone paper for pastel). `--style` records the current, so `report` knows which target to compare with. `new` clears the schema, so make the canvas first.

`--px-per-mm` sets the sheet's scale (written to `<canvas>_meta.json` before the medium sets up): media that work in millimetres read it (ink: a drawing for process at about 6 px/mm, a plate at 10, engraving at 16; see the medium's page).

`media` lists every medium with its tools, supports and finishing steps.

## The schema (your drawing)

- `ref <file.svg|file.png>`: set or revise the schema the painting is matched against (rendered to canvas size).
- `scene <scene.json> [overlay.svg] [draft|final]`: render a 3D blockout as the schema, with people built to human proportions, plane-built heads with expressions, articulated hands, drapery in wind, and element masks for `elements`/`report` (see "Scene blockouts" below). For flat styles, an SVG schema is still the better drawing.
- `transfer [strength] [#color]`: lay the schema's contours on the canvas as a light drawing, once, early (recorded as the `drawing` role).
- `focus FX FY`: declare the focal point (canvas pixels). `simplify` declares it too, and an episode can carry `"focus": [x, y]`. Stroke size, `report`'s focal checks and the role checks use it.
- `elements [file.json]`: declare the picture's elements (`{"elements": [{"name": "figure", "region": {...}}, ...]}`) for the clarity check in `report`; with no file it lists them. One element at a time: the intention `{"type": "element", "name", "region"}`. Use tight regions (a polygon around the figure, not a loose box): the surround is measured just outside the outline.

**Translate the schema, don't copy it.** A painter groups the values into a few masses, keeps full detail and the hardest edges at the focal point, and simplifies everywhere else. `simplify FX FY [groups] [radius]` does that to the current schema around a focal point (FX, FY in canvas pixels; groups 3-5; radius as a fraction of the diagonal, default 0.25) and sets the result as a new schema version. Use `faithful` below 1 away from the focus too.

### Scene blockouts

A scene is a JSON file: `camera`, `sun`, `sky`, `ambient`, `fog`, `ground`, `lights`, `exposure`, `wind`, `render_scale`, `masks` and `objects`. Units are meters, y is up. Read `docs/subjects/figure.md`, `face.md` and `hands-feet.md` before you pose anyone. Example scenes: `docs/gallery/figures_*.json`.

- **camera:** `pos`, `look_at`, `fov` (vertical, degrees) or `lens` (the focal length in mm on 35 mm film, the "equivalent focal length"), `roll` (degrees, tilts the picture), `up` (a hint). Looking straight down or up works (the top of the picture is then -z): `{"pos": [0, 2.6, 0], "look_at": [0, 0, 0]}` is an overhead view; a camera near the ground looking up is a worm's-eye view. **Defaults:** a scene with a person or a hand and no `fov` or `lens` gets a **100 mm** portrait lens; other scenes keep fov 40 (about 48 mm).
- **frame** (in `camera`): `{"on": "person0.head", "fill": 0.6, "distance": 1.5, "from": [x, y, z], "height": 0}` places the camera for you: it looks at the part's centre (`head`, `face`, `hands`, `torso`, or the whole object) from `distance` m (default **1.5 m** for a head, face or hand: the standard portrait distance) along `from` (default: the way the face looks), and picks the lens so the part fills `fill` of the frame's height. With `lens` or `fov` given and no `distance`, it picks the distance instead. A tight head (fill 0.7) comes out at about 140 mm, a head-and-hands picture (fill 0.4) at about 80 mm.
- **Perspective is the distance.** At 30 cm a camera magnifies the nose about 30% against the face; at 1.5 m there is "essentially no difference" (Ward et al. 2018, JAMA Facial Plastic Surgery). Every render with a face in it prints the lens, the camera's distance to each face, how much perspective magnifies the nose against the cheekbones (about 3% at 1.4 m, 15% at 0.3 m) and, when a hand is in the picture, its distance and projected length in face-lengths (canon about 1). It **warns** when a close-up (the face over 15% of the frame's height) is taken from nearer than 1.2 m, and when a hand and a face are more than 20% apart in distance (the hand reads small or large against the face). A wide lens close to a face makes it swell and a hand behind it shrink (round 19: `docs/research/hyperrealism-gaps.md`, G4). The study sheets (`figure_sheet.py`) now shoot heads from 1.5 m and hands from 1.2 m.
- **wind:** a vector in m/s, e.g. `[0, 0, -16]` for a gale blowing toward -z. Drapery and long hair stream with it.
- **render_scale:** multiplies the render resolution (draft 300x400, final 450x600). Faces and hands need 1.3-2 when they are small in the frame. Building a person takes 2-4 s on 2 vCPU; drawing the mesh costs well under a second at any size, so time now goes to the marched parts (hair, other objects): the gallery's figure scenes at 1.3 take 8-10 s, a study head 3 s (16 s with long hair). With the primitive rig (`"rig": "primitive"`) time grows with the square of the scale (a figure with dress and shawl at 1.3: 5-15 minutes).
- **objects:** `box`, `sphere`, `ellipsoid`, `capsule`, `cylinder`, `house`, `fence`, `tree`, the old `figure` mannequin (kept for old scenes), and:

**`person`** (one human). People are a sculpted mesh: the MakeHuman base mesh (CC0; `studio/assets/LICENSES.md`) shaped by its own targets, posed on its 163-bone skeleton and drawn by a z-buffer, inside the same lights, camera, fog and shadows as everything else (`studio/v8/human.py`, `raster.py`). The assets are fetched once with `python3 tools/fetch_human.py` (into `~/.cache/teodor/`); without them, and with `"rig": "primitive"`, the older rig of ellipsoids and planes is built instead (old scenes build either way).
- who: `age` (years), `sex` (`female`/`male`), `build` (`slim`, `average`, `sturdy`, `heavy`, `muscular`, `frail`; or MakeHuman's own sliders `muscle` and `weight`, 0-1, 0.5 average), optional `height` (m), `ancestry` (`{"african", "asian", "caucasian"}` weights; default an even mix). The body comes from MakeHuman's targets for sex, age, muscle and weight (sculpted from anatomical references, so proportions, the bust, the neck, the muscle masses and old age's sagging are the mesh's own), scaled to the stature for sex and age, with a stoop and a forward head after 55. Measured on the mesh: about 7.4 heads tall for adults, 6.4 at eight, 7.0-7.2 at 75; waist-to-hip ratio 0.70 for women (target 0.72), 0.82 for men (target 0.89: the mesh's average man is slimmer at the waist; `heavy` 0.88); biacromial breadth about 34 / 40 cm (targets 36.7 / 41.1). Before puberty the face and body are a child's for both sexes (sex is carried by hair and clothes). `dimorphism` (default 1) moves the sex slider toward neutral (below 1). Fitted clothing is the body's own surface.
- where: `pos`, `facing` (degrees about y), `anchor`: `ground` (the lowest point touches the ground; add `"float": true` to lift it by `pos[1]`, e.g. standing on a wall), `pelvis`, `chest` or `head` (that point goes to `pos`: use `head` for a swimmer or a portrait). `lie`: `back`, `front`, `left`, `right`; or `pitch`/`roll` in degrees.
- pose: `pose` is a preset or a list of presets: `stand`, `contrapposto`, `walk`, `lean_wind`, `arms_wide`, `reach`, `throw`, `throw_release`, `hand_on_head`, `kneel`, `kneel_up`, `sit`, `sit_ground`, `lie_back`, `lie_side`, `sleep_back`, `tread_water`, `slumped`, `bowed`, `look_up`. `joints` overrides any joint, in degrees: `spine`, `neck`, `head` [forward, side to the left, twist to the left]; `pelvis` [tilt, hip hike, twist]; `arm_l`/`arm_r` [forward, out, twist, elbow]; `wrist_l`/`wrist_r` [flex, deviation, pronation]; `leg_l`/`leg_r` [forward, out, twist, knee]; `ankle_l`/`ankle_r` [dorsiflex]; `shoulder_l`/`shoulder_r` [raise, forward] (in head units); `lean` [forward, side] tilts the whole body about the feet (into a wind).
- reaching: `reach_l`/`reach_r` put the palm on a target by inverse kinematics: `{"target": [x, y, z] | "head_top" | "head_back" | "forehead" | "nape" | "chest" | "belly" | "hip_l" | "hip_r" | "knee_l" | "knee_r" | "cheek_l" | "cheek_r", "offset": [..], "palm": [direction the palm faces], "fingers": [direction the fingers point], "pole": [where the elbow goes], "hand": preset, "settle": true}`. A hand on a cat: the cat's top as target, `"palm": [0, -1, 0]`.
- contact: hands touch what is there instead of passing through it. A palm inside an object (or the person's own head, torso or legs) is pushed out, the wrist moving with it; a reaching hand with `rest`, `hold`, `cup` or `grip` settles onto the surface under its palm (up to 6 cm; `"settle": false` keeps it where you put it); each finger joint bends just enough to rest on the surface, and with those grips curls down onto it. People are built after the other objects, so this works for any object in the scene.
- hands: `hand_l`/`hand_r` = `open`, `spread`, `relaxed`, `rest`, `hold`, `grip`, `push_hair`, `throw`, `point`, `cup`, `fist`, or your own `{"f": [[mcp, pip, dip] x 4 fingers], "spread": deg, "thumb": [out, palmar, opposition, mcp, ip], "wrist": [flex, dev]}`. The angles bend the mesh's finger bones (the same conventions as before).
- face: the head is the mesh's sculpted head, shaped by sex and age (a child's large cranium over a small low face; old age's lids, folds and jowls) with eyeballs (iris and pupil at their anatomical sizes; `iris` sets the colour), teeth and tongue. Facial contrast follows Russell: a darker lash line in women, redder lips over a lighter, less red skin; a man's skin darker and redder. Brows (`brows` colour, default the hair's, greying later) are a thinner, higher arch in women. `beard` (0-1; default 0.35 for adult men, 0 for women and children) tints the beard zone: up to 0.6 a cool shaved shadow, above it the beard's own colour (`beard_color`). `skin_sex` (default 1, 0 off) scales the sex tint of the skin; `skin_wrap` (default 0.25) lets the light wrap a little past the terminator on the skin. `expression` = `neutral`, `calm`, `smile`, `laugh`, `gasp`, `surprise`, `fear`, `scream`, `sorrow`, `sleep`, `anger`, `disgust`, or `{"preset": "laugh", "au": {"6": 0.6, "4": 0.3}}` with FACS action units 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 12, 15, 17, 20, 23, 25, 26, 27, 41, 43, 45 (0-1), each moving MakeHuman's expression targets (brows inner and outer up and down, lids open, slit and closed, nose wrinkle, the mouth's corners, lips, jaw, the neck's platysma; `docs/subjects/face.md` has the table and how a laugh differs from a scream). `gaze` [yaw, pitch] turns the eyes. `planes` (0-1, default 0 on the mesh) groups the head's light into light, two halftones and shadow over a smoothed form, the way a painter blocks in a head; `body_planes` (default 0) does the same for the body.
- surface: `skin`, `color` (fitted clothing: the body's own surface, smoothed so small forms don't print through), `bare` (parts in skin: `torso`, `arms`, `legs`, `feet`), `shoes`, `lips`, `skin_gloss` (wet skin), **photographic detail** (on by default; G1 of `docs/research/hyperrealism-gaps.md`): `skin_detail` (0-1, default 1: pores at about 36 per cm² on the face, the skin's fine furrows, scattered fine lines deeper with age, a soft swell and a mottled colour, all procedural on the mesh, so they stay on the skin through any pose or expression; features smaller than about a pixel fade out), `skin_oil` (0-1, default 0.5: the sharp oily sheen, strongest on the forehead, nose and lips, that breaks into glints over the pores; the broad sheen of skin is always there), `skin_seed`, `strands` (default true: hair drawn as strands along its growth, with gaps, locks and a highlight that breaks along each strand), `droplets` (`{"count": 40, "size_mm": [1, 4], "on": ["face"], "seed": 3}`: water drops on the skin, each a small lens with a darker rim, a white glint toward the light, a bright side away from it and a crescent of shadow), `hair`: `{"style": "short" | "crop" | "bob" | "long" | "bun" | "ponytail" | "slick" | "receding" | "bald", "color", "length", "gloss"}`. No openly licensed hair or clothes could be fetched (MakeHuman's asset library is not reachable from here), so hair and loose clothes are still the studio's own simulated masses, now resting on the mesh's skull and body, and hair is streaked along its growth so it reads as strands. Hair is a mass with thickness: a woman's (and a child's) hairline is rounded; a man's recedes at the temples with age (`recession` 0-1 overrides). `bob` and `long` hang as a curtain below the skull that covers the ears, frames the face and leans downwind; `long` continues in thick locks (`length` m, `locks`, `drag`) simulated in the wind; `bun` sits high on the back of the crown so it shows in the silhouette (`"bun_pos": "low"` for a nape bun); `ponytail` is a gathered, tapering mass from a tie. Also `thickness`, `volume` (multiplies it), `hairline`. Hair greys with age (none at 35, a little at 45, about a quarter at 55, three quarters at 75; Panhard et al. 2012 for 45-65, the rest judgement) unless `grey` (0-1) is given; brows grey half as much. A short-haired woman still reads female from her face, neck and body (the rig is measured that way).
- `clothes`: a list of `{"kind": "dress", "color", "length" (0-1 of the legs), "flare", "drag", "folds"}`, `{"kind": "shawl", "color", "length" (m), "width", "drag"}`, `{"kind": "shirt", "color", "sleeves": "long" | "short" | "none", "ease", "billow", "length"}`, `{"kind": "trousers", "color", "length": "long" | "short"}`. Shirts and trousers are the body's surface eased out by `ease` and bridging the hollows; the dress is simulated cloth hanging from the waist (it presses onto the legs on the windward side, held there by friction, and streams on the other); the shawl's ends are simulated in the wind; the shirt billows on the leeward side.

**`hand`** (a lone hand for studies): `pos` (the wrist), `preset`, `fingers`, `palm`, `side` (`l`/`r`), `length` (m), `forearm` (how much of it), `sex`, `age`. It is the mesh's hand and forearm, cut from a body. A lone mesh hand does not rest on other objects; for a hand resting on something use a `person` with `reach_l`/`reach_r`, or `"rig": "primitive"`.
**`animal`**: a cat, `pose` `curled` (asleep), `sit` or `lie`, `pos`, `facing`, `size`, `color`.
**`water`**: a surface at height `y` with `color`, `chop`, `ripples` (a list of [x, z] centres), `ripple_amp`, `gloss`. It hides what is under it, so a swimmer's body ends at the waterline.

**Element masks.** Every `scene` render also writes `<canvas>_elements.npz` and `<canvas>_elements.json` next to the canvas (outside the studio: `<image>_elements.*`; turn off with `"masks": false`). The npz holds one boolean mask (canvas height x width) per visible element, keyed `<object>.<element>`: a person's `head` (with the face), `face` (the face alone: from the hairline to the chin, ear to ear, with the eyes and mouth), `neck`, `torso`, `arms`, `hands`, `legs`, `feet`, `hair`, `drapery` (shirts, trousers, dresses and shawls), and `<object>.all`; `animal`, `water`; other objects as `<object>.object` (the old `figure` as `torso` and `skin`). Objects are named by their `id`, or `<type><n>`. Masks cover only what is visible from the camera. The JSON index (`"format": "teodor-elements/1"`) lists for each key: `object`, `type`, `element`, `pixels`, `share` of the canvas, `bbox` [x0, y0, x1, y1], `centroid` [x, y] and `depth_m` (median distance from the camera). Use them for `elements` regions, for `clip` regions around a face or a hand, and for checking that the focal element is where you meant it.

**Study sheets:** `python3 figure_sheet.py expressions out.jpg [sex] [age] [planes]`, `python3 figure_sheet.py variants out.jpg` (woman 20, 40 sturdy and 75, man 20, 40 and 75, girl 8, boy 8: each a neutral head and a full figure in clothes) and `python3 figure_sheet.py hands out.jpg` (every hand preset from the back, the thumb side and fingertips first) render the presets; `both` puts all three on one sheet (`docs/gallery/figures_sheet.jpg`, about 2.5 minutes on 2 vCPU: a head with long hair 16 s, other heads and figures 2-6 s). `human.body_stats(person)` measures the mesh standing: waist and hip girths as a tape takes them, their ratio, the biacromial breadth, stature and heads tall (`figure.body_stats` for the primitive rig).

**What the blockout still can't do** (paint these from knowledge, not from the render): faces are one sculpted face varied by sex, age and ancestry, not individuals (and never a real person's likeness); the skin's texture is procedural: at 1-2 mm it holds about half to two-thirds of what real close-ups hold (no age folds, stubble, moles or freckles yet), and up close it can read lumpy; catchlights appear only when the light sits where a cornea would reflect it toward the camera; hair is still a simulated mass under its strands (helmet-like for bobs, with a step where a curtain meets the face; a receding man's horseshoe is too small); a scream tears the mouth's corners a little; fitted shirts follow the body closely (no folds, the bust's underside still shows); a dress or shawl is coarse cloth (pipe folds, no fine creases); fingers rest on surfaces but don't squash them; small heads (under about 60 px tall) lose their features, so raise `render_scale` or crop closer.

## References (studies, casts and photographs)

Painters build faces, hands and figures from life and from references, not from memory alone (`docs/research/references.md`). Gather them in planning, for the hard subjects, by **feature** (what you study them for):

```
python3 <repo>/tools/find_refs.py <workdir> "hand study" "study of hands" --type study --feature "hand gripping cloth" --dry
python3 <repo>/tools/find_refs.py <workdir> --take met:655422 cma:162888 --feature "hand gripping cloth" --why "knuckles over a fold"
python3 <repo>/tools/find_refs.py <workdir> "Duchenne" --type photo --feature "laughing cheek"
python3 <repo>/tools/find_refs.py <workdir> --sheet
```
It searches The Met's and the Cleveland Museum of Art's open-access collections (public-domain / CC0 images only), downloads into `<workdir>/refs/`, records each reference in `refs/refs.json` (title, artist, date, museum, object and image URL, licence and its evidence, query, feature, reason) and draws `refs/sheet.jpg`, grouped by feature. `--type` is `photo`, `study` (drawings), `print`, `painting`, `sculpture` (casts and antique figures) or `any`; `--n` per query and museum (default 4); `--dry` lists candidates with their keys and downloads nothing; `--take` keeps chosen keys. It skips titles naming a sitter (`--allow-named` keeps them for a hand or drapery, never for a face), post-mortem and wound images, and more than `--per-artist` (default 2) works by one artist per feature, and warns until a feature has three references from different sources. Museum search is full-text: read every candidate, and replace each `reason` in `refs.json` with what you study in it.

The rules: study several references for one feature and invent the face; never take one person's face as the subject, never set a reference as the schema, trace, project or paste it; translate what they show through your current and your hand. `look refs [FEATURE]` puts them beside the canvas.

## The hand: episodes and intentions

```
act '{"felt": {...}, "do": [up to 4 intentions]}'      or      act @episode.json
```
One episode is up to four intentions, then you look. Long stroke lists go in a JSON file (`act @file`), because the shell limits argument length.

**Felt state** (`arousal` 0-1, `tension` 0-1, `valence` -1..1, `care` 0-1) drives the hand's movement: arousal makes faster, longer strokes that break into dry-brush; tension makes tremor, jagged corners and pressure spikes; valence makes curves smooth (warm) or angular (dark); care makes placement steadier with a slight hesitation at the start. Every stroke follows a human speed profile (accelerate, cruise, slow down), which viewers read as a hand (docs/research/emotion.md).

**The hand (v8).** A stroke is not one dab repeated. Pressure, width, load and dry-brush change along every stroke, and a slow pressure wave runs through it. Size, pressure, load, angle, colour and edge **drift** across a passage, so neighbouring strokes vary together instead of jittering one by one (correlated variation, as Hennig et al. 2011 found listeners prefer in human timing; docs/research/process.md 1.8). Strokes are smaller near the declared focus and broader away from it (×0.65 at the focus to ×1.35 at 0.4 of the diagonal), and smaller or broader by role (lay-ins broad, accents and lines small). `"focus_scale": false` and `"role_scale": false` switch that off for an intention. These numbers are judgements tuned by eye; the before/after measurements are in docs/proposals-v8.md. A medium can set its own defaults for a tool calibrated on the plain stroke: ink's line tools (nib, stipple, burin, needle, drypoint) default to the plain stroke at their own size (see manual/ink.md); what you ask for explicitly wins.

**Articulations** (`"articulation"` on `match`, `free`, `model`, `strokes`, or on one stroke of `strokes`; default `auto`):
- `drag`: a loaded stroke dragged until the paint runs out: lighter, narrower and breaking into dry-brush toward its end.
- `swell`: the calligraphic stroke: it lands on the tip, presses into the belly and lifts to a point.
- `scumble`: a thin, half-loaded, scrubbed layer that breaks over what is beneath (the National Gallery's definition: a thin layer lighter than the colour under it; a glaze is darker).
- `flick`: fast, full at the start, lifting off to a point.
- `plain`: the v7 stroke (constant intent, landing and lift only).
- `auto`: each stroke picks from a mix that suits its role (body: swell, drag and plain; dead colour: mostly drag; accents: swell and flick), drifting across the passage so neighbouring strokes share it.
Without `edge`, each stroke's edge also comes from its role (body strokes mix soft, hard and lost; accents are hard; scumbles lost).

**Intentions** (the `brush` is any tool of the canvas's medium; see `media` or the medium's page). Every intention may carry `"role"` (see "Layers with roles"):
- `match`: strokes toward the schema where the canvas differs most, following the form and stopping at its edges. `size` [min,max], `threshold` (lower = more coverage), `faithful` 0-1, `colors` (mixed in by 1-faithful), `color_shift` {`temp`, `sat`, `value`} (**multipliers**: `value` 0.9 darkens 10%; 0 turns black), `region`, `clip`, `max_strokes`, `adaptive` (small strokes where the drawing is detailed), `edge` hard|soft|lost, `pressure`, `load`, `articulation`. Direction: `follow` `form` (default: along the form, from the schema's structure; where the schema is flat the strokes continue the nearest form or follow a slow flow around one angle, never all one angle), `across` (cross-contour, wrapping around the form) or `direction` (use `direction` everywhere). `direction` {`angle`, `spread`} also sets the flow angle in flat passages. Strokes leave the form direction by a drifting angle of up to about 20°, so passages cross instead of stacking.
- `model`: model a form from a value schema. The `region`'s values (the schema's, or a value image `"values": "file.png"`) are cut into `steps` bands (default 4) and painted band by band, dark to light (`"order": "light_to_dark"` for watercolour), each band's strokes following the form's direction (`follow` `form` or `across`). Colours come from a ramp `colors` (dark to light) or from the schema. `size`, `length`, `density`, `max_strokes`, `articulation`, `clip` (default on). Use it for heads, hands, drapery and every rounded form, after a tonal study set as the schema.
- `free`: gestural strokes in a region. `colors` (with weights), `count`, `size`, `length` **as a range [min,max]**, `direction` ({`angle`,`spread`}, {`around`:[x,y]}, {`toward`}, {`away`}, {`field`:true}), `curve`, `pressure`, `load`, `edge`, `articulation`.
- `strokes`: exact marks: `strokes: [{"pts": [[x,y],...], "size", "color", "pressure", "articulation"}]`.
- `hatch`: tone built from lines, the way a draughtsman or tempera painter does: parallel strokes at `angle` with `spacing` across a `region`, in `layers` (value levels, default [0.75, 0.55, 0.35, 0.18]): each layer covers only where the schema (or a flat `value` 0-1) is darker than its level, turned by `cross` degrees (default 45), so darker passages get denser cross-hatching. `size`, `colors`, `length` (longest line segment), `pressure`, `max_strokes`. Lines follow the hand; spacing and angle drift slightly, and each line starts its pieces at its own offset so piece ends never line up. The defaults are coarse: use the measured settings in each medium's manual page (e.g. graphite 0.8-2 mm spacing). Pass `colors` with monochrome tools (the default #2a2a2a presses as hard as the tool can) and set `layers` below the paper's value on toned paper. Works with any medium (pencil, pen, tempera hatch brush, charcoal tip). Two systems for engraving and pen work: `"regular": true` rules the lines (no spacing or angle jitter, long unbroken lines, broken only where the region or the value level ends, a steady hand); `"follow": "form"` lays contour hatching, evenly spaced lines that follow the schema's form (the same direction field as `match`), each value layer turned by `cross` degrees from the form (`angle` turns the first; `length` caps a line). `max_strokes` defaults to 20000 lines (enough for a full engraved sheet at 16 px/mm), so a hatch no longer stops part-way across the sheet. **Feathered bands:** in the freehand hatch each layer's pressure falls off smoothly across its level instead of stopping dead, so a gradient laid in value bands doesn't step: full pressure below `level - feather/2`, none above `level + feather/2` (`feather` in the schema's value, 0-1; default 0.08, or 0.6 of the smallest gap between the layers if that is smaller; `"feather": 0` is the hard stop). On a graphite ramp hatched in seven bands the steepest step fell from 1.1 to 0.4 L* per px (docs/calibration/graphite-hyperrealism.md). Ruled (`regular`) and contour (`follow: form`) hatching keep the hard stop unless you give `feather`.
- `marks`: **designed fine marks planned from your schema's structure** (`marks.py`; AGENTS.md rule 2: a planner proposes paths from your own invented reference, you choose the families, regions and counts, and every mark is laid by the medium's tools through `strokes`). `{"type": "marks", "families": {FAMILY: {"region", "count", "brush", "grade", ...}}, "dry": false}`. Families:
  - `strands`: streamlines of the schema's local line direction through its darker threads (dark strands, tapered "bold to begin with, ended lightly") and its lighter ones (`"light"`: the share of light strands, lifted with `"lift"`: `electric_eraser` by default, or `stylus` to indent them before any tone). Without `count`, one strand every `spacing_mm` (default 0.35) of the sheet; `length_mm` [min, max] (default 3-9), `size_mm`.
  - `glints`: the schema's small bright blobs (at least 20 L* over their 2 mm surround, under 2 mm), lifted with the battery eraser, or `"brush": "stylus"` to indent them before any tone.
  - `pores`: its small dark blobs, each a light circling dab (`mech_05` HB at pressure 0.6), never planned more than 12 L* darker than the surround ("pores that are too dark will look like blackheads").
  - `edges`: its strong edges, a hard lead (`mech_03` HB) along the dark side.
  - `lashes`: its thin dark lines (lashes, creases, fine lines: dark ridges, not blobs), tapered strokes along them.
  - `droplets`: the scene's water drops (`<canvas>_structure.json`, written by `scene`), each as a unit: `parts` `rim` (a dark ellipse), `shade` (the side toward the light), `cast` (a crescent of shadow away from it), `glint` (white, toward the light) and `caustic` (the bright far side, a soft lift). Lay them in stages: the glint indented first (`"parts": ["glint"], "lift": "stylus"`), the caustic lifted before the fixative, rim, shade and cast after it.
  `"dry": true` plans without drawing: it prints the counts and writes `<canvas>_marks_plan.png` (dark marks red, lifts cyan). In a detail the same works at the detail's scale. **Order matters with graphite and charcoal:** erasers can't lift fixed tone, charcoal won't take on unfixed graphite, so: indents first, darks first, tone, lifts, workable fixative, then the dark fine marks (manual/graphite.md). Thresholds and sizes are judgements; measured results in `docs/research/hyperrealism-gaps.md`, G7.
- `blend`: soften (where the medium allows it; not in tempera or prints).
- `glaze`: the medium's thin layer over a region: an oil glaze, a watercolour wash, an ink wash, a pastel veil, a printed flat block. `color`, `opacity`, `region`, `feather`.
- **Revision: taking paint away** (painters revise on the canvas, very often by removal: docs/research/process.md 2.6). Each needs a `region`; `strength` 0-1 (default about 0.7), `angle` of the sweep, `passes`, `size`, `feather`; edges are broken by hand (`"ragged": false` for a clean edge). The medium decides what each means:
  - `scrape`: scrape back. Oil: the palette knife scrapes wet paint toward the layer beneath, and on dry paint the blade shaves the impasto and bares the ground on the tops of the weave. Watercolour: a blade takes paint off the paper's peaks. Gouache: back to the paper. Tempera: back to the gesso or the gold. Charcoal and graphite: the hard eraser; pastel: the scraper. Ink: a knife off the top fibres on paper; on a plate the scraper and burnisher take the lines out of the metal.
  - `wipe`: wipe out. Oil: the rag lifts wet paint (it warns when the passage is dry: scrape it instead). Watercolour: blotting and lifting (wet pigment comes up most, staining pigments least). Gouache: a damp brush re-dissolves it. Charcoal, graphite, pastel: the kneaded eraser. Tempera can't be wiped (it dries at once). Ink: blotting lifts wet ink only (dry ink is refused: scrape it); on a plate, the printer wipes that passage (plate tone).
  - `paintout`: paint a passage out with the colour its surroundings would have there (a blur of the colour around it), in the medium's broad brush (`brush`, `color` to choose). Watercolour lifts the passage first and then washes it, because it can't cover. Ink paints out only with Chinese white on bristol, illustration board and laid paper; on xuan, silk and plates it refuses. Charcoal and graphite paint out by erasing and redrawing the surrounding tone (refused where the passage is mostly fixed); pastel lays the surrounding colour as a dust veil. Wipe for charcoal and pastel is the chamois, then the kneaded eraser; for graphite the kneaded eraser.
  - Woodblock refuses all three with the reason: a print can't be taken back; recarve and print the block again. Media without their own way fall back to pulling the colour back toward the ground.
- Regions: `{"type":"canvas"}`, `{"type":"rect","x","y","w","h"}`, `{"type":"ellipse","cx","cy","rx","ry"}`, `{"type":"poly","pts":[[x,y],...]}`, and `{"type":"segment","at":[x,y]}`: a part of the schema, the connected area of similar value around the point (no polygon to write; a part with holes keeps its holes). `"at"` may be a list of points (their areas join); `"by": "colour"` groups by colour instead of value; `"tol"` is how similar (default 0.08 in value 0-1, or 10 ΔE by colour); `"blur"` smooths the schema first (default 2 px); `"grow"` widens (or, negative, shrinks) the area by px; `"holes": true` fills its holes. Needs a schema (`ref`); works wherever a region does (`clip`, hatch, match, revisions, `finish` regions, elements). Defaults are judgements: check the area with a cheap intention first, or `look ref`.

`dry [0-1]` lets time pass (what that means depends on the medium: oil gets tacky, a wash dries and its edges darken).

## Layers with roles

A passage in a real painting is several layers that each do a job (docs/research/process.md; docs/FINISHING.md; each current's process in docs/styles/). The studio records, per pixel, which roles built it (`<canvas>_roles.npy`, with the order of first use per 8 px cell in `<canvas>_rolefirst.npy`). The roles:

| Role | What it is |
|---|---|
| `underpainting` | the imprimatura, the monochrome lay-in, the abbozzo or verdaccio (aliases: imprimatura, ebauche, grisaille, verdaccio) |
| `drawing` | the underdrawing, the transfer, the first lines on bare ground |
| `dead_colour` | the first colour lay-in over the ground or underpainting (Dutch dead colouring, the academy's ébauche in colour) |
| `body` | body colour: the opaque working up, impasto (alias: impasto) |
| `glaze` | a transparent darker film over dry paint |
| `scumble` | a thin broken lighter layer over what is beneath |
| `wash` | a watercolour or ink wash |
| `hatch` | tone built from lines |
| `accent` | the last few touches: the darkest darks, the brightest lights, highlights |
| `contour` | lines restated over the colour at the end (a key line, dark contours) |
| `correction` | scraping, wiping, painting out, lifting, erasing |

Tag each intention with `"role"`; without it the studio infers one: `glaze` → glaze (wash in watercolour and ink, body in woodblock), `hatch` → hatch, revision tools and rags, lifts and erasers → correction, `articulation: scumble` or a starved brush → scumble, liners on bare ground → drawing and over paint → contour (dark) or accent, a few small `strokes` → accent, the first colour over bare ground → underpainting (one or two colours of one muted hue) or dead colour (charcoal, graphite and ink: drawing; watercolour: wash), later colour → body. Each current in `studio/styles.json` has a `layer_order` from its research doc, and `report` checks the canvas against it.

## Measuring: `report`

`report [style] [ref] [finish] [--crop X Y W H]` measures the canvas the way the studio measured the reference paintings of each current (`studio/targets.json`, from public-domain works in The Met's open-access collection; `tools/measure_targets.py`), and compares the two. Like Spectre's master report, it is a ruler, not a judge: read it, then decide.

- **value**: the key (mean lightness, L\* 0-100), contrast (spread), range, the shares of dark, middle and light, the *murky middle* (L\* 40-60), the notan balance, and the **value masses** as the eye counts them when squinting: the canvas is squinted (48 px on the long side), its L\* split into the best 2, 3, 4... groups, and a group counts only if it is a mass (at least 4% of the canvas with a connected piece of 2%), differs from its neighbour by about one step of a 7-step value scale (14 L\*, less in a narrow-range picture), and its boundaries are sharper than the picture's average gradient. Printed with each mass's L\* and share. (It counts 3 masses in round 16, where the old histogram measure said 2; public-domain Met oils hold 2-3.)
- **colour**: average and loudest chroma, the hue spread (0 = one hue, 1 = all hues), warm and cool shares.
- **edges**: edge density, the share of hard edges, and how many of the hard edges sit near the focal point.
- **texture**: fine detail energy and how much it varies between passages. Painters vary their marks by passage.
- **structure**: the spectral slope (steeper = smoother, flatter = more fine detail and noise), the focal point (where local contrast is strongest) and how concentrated contrast is around it.
- **marks**: orientation coherence (how strongly marks are oriented locally), direction spread (how much the stroke direction changes over the picture: 0 is all one angle), mark scale and its spread between passages, and edge variety (lost, soft, firm and hard edges all present). Flag under a direction spread of 0.25 (round 16: 0.14; Met oils 0.35-0.87).
- **layers**: each role's share, how much of the painted canvas holds one, two or three roles, the roles in the focal area, and the current's expected order. Flags: the focal area or a large region (a 3×3 grid) built from one role only (over 60% single-role pixels); roles out of the current's `layer_order` (a later role first used before an earlier one in over 15% of the cells holding both). Corrections are never ordered. Thresholds are judgements.
- **elements**: each declared element's contrast with its surround at thumbnail size (value difference, colour ΔE, the gradient along its outline) and the schema's contrast for comparison. *Masked*: contrast under 8 and a weak outline, the painting version of Spectre's masking measure: at a glance the element isn't in the picture. *Weakened*: under half the contrast the schema had. Thresholds are judgements.
- **fine** (at the sheet's real resolution, in millimetres; `eyes.measure_fine`): the measures above read the canvas at 512 px on its long side, where one pixel of a 48 × 64 cm sheet is 1.25 mm, so pores, strands, glints and the edges of the focus are invisible to them. The fine line reads every pixel (a sheet over 16 megapixels is halved, and says so), at the canvas's scale (`new --px-per-mm`, or the medium's: graphite 5 px/mm, charcoal and pastel 2.5; a detail multiplies it): p1, p5, p95; the shares below L\* 20 and 30 (near-black) and above 92 (bare paper); the share of **flat mid-tone** (L\* 35-65 with under 1 L\* of variation over 5 mm: fog); **texture by scale** (the RMS in L\* of the band between Gaussian blurs of s and 2s mm, s = 0.125 to 4 mm; a band finer than half a pixel is not measured); **speculars** (small bright blobs at least 20 L\* above their 2 mm surround and under 2 mm across, and how many reach 30 L\*); the **edge sharpness** distribution (the 10-90% rise of strong edges: p10, median, p90, the shares under 0.3 mm and over 2 mm). After a `scene`, the face and the hair are measured again at the subject's own scale (px per face-mm from the camera and the depth, written in the scene's element index), with the hair's strand coherence. `report --crop X Y W H` measures one crop at full resolution. Thresholds are judgements (`docs/research/hyperrealism-gaps.md`, G10).
- **against the close-up ruler**: a current that draws from close-up photographs has a second target, `<current>-closeup` in `studio/targets.json` (`graphite-hyperrealism-closeup`: built with `tools/measure_targets.py closeup` from openly licensed photographs; **provisional**: one photograph so far). Each of its keys is a bound, and every miss is a **flag** that names the fix: darks missing (share below L\* 20 or 30), no near-black (p1, p5), the lights too dark (p95), fog (flat mid-tone), no crisp highlights in the face (speculars ≥ 30 L\*), no crisp edges (p10 of the edge rise), skin or hair texture outside the ruler at each scale.
- **FLAGS**: one value mass; a murky middle; even texture everywhere; hard edges not gathered at the focus; strokes at one angle; one-role passages; roles out of order; masked or weakened elements; and the close-up ruler's misses.

`report ref` runs the same measures on the **schema** (the reference you match against) instead of the canvas, and writes `report_ref.json`: check that the reference holds the darks, the glints and the texture before the first mark, because you can't draw more than the reference holds.

`report finish` adds the **finishing check** (Spectre's post-master check; docs/FINISHING.md), run after the finishing stage: the finishing steps done; signed or not; the medium's own checks (oil: still wet, sunken matte passages over 3%, uneven gloss; watercolour: final darks reached, lifted or reserved lights, masking fluid removed, dry; gouache: dry; woodblock: a seal or signature, and a key line whose width varies like a carved brush line; charcoal: fixed, and no lifts after the final fixative; pastel and charcoal: how much the fixative darkened against the studio's modelled range; pastel: the last bright touches after the fixative and no final fixative over the whole sheet; graphite: transfer guide lines erased, sheen not overworked; smudges in margins the schema leaves bare; ink, by tradition: Chinese ink a seal or signature and the last dots; pen illustration printable as a line block (no grey wash, few values, greys that stay open in reduction, pencil erased); intaglio a platemark, the wipe and an even plate tone); and whether the key, contrast, hard edges and focal contrast are still in the current's range after finishing.
- **against the target**: each measure with the current's mean ± sd and whether the canvas is inside the range, and if not, which way to move.

A current without a target (Surrealism and Abstract Expressionism are still in copyright, so there is no public-domain sample) still gets the numbers and flags.

## Detail pass (a small region at higher resolution)

A head about 90 px wide on a 1200x1600 canvas is a handful of 2-6 px strokes, so the planes, soft eye sockets and an expression can't be built there: every cue shouts or vanishes (round 17: the face came out a mask in every round with a figure). The detail pass lets you lean in: paint the region on a sub-canvas at N times the resolution with the same tools, then bring it back down.

```
detail open X Y W H [--scale N] [--margin M]      open the region (canvas px) at N x (2, 3, 4, 5, 6 or 8; default 4)
<command> --detail ...                            any command on the open detail (or CANVAS=<canvas>_detail)
look detail                                       the detail at its own scale beside the region on the canvas after close
detail close [--resample area|sample]             bring it back down
detail cancel                                     discard it (the canvas is left exactly as it was)
detail                                            what is open
detail tiles X Y W H [--scale 4] [--tile 600] [--overlap 0.1]   plan a large passage as overlapping tiles (writes <canvas>_tiles.json)
detail open --tile K                              open tile K of that plan
```

**Relating a detail back.** `detail close` (and every `report` after it, for every closed detail) measures the detail against its surroundings and against the schema: its texture at 0.4-1.6 sheet-mm over a ring around it, divided by the same ratio in the schema (flag outside a factor of 1.5), and the step in mean value across each side of the rectangle beyond the schema's own step (flag over 0.5 L*). A flag reads "RELATE BACK": carry the detail's treatment into the passage around it, or soften it, and correct the whole passage, not the rectangle (round 19's fist carried 5-6× the texture of its arm and read as pasted on).

**Tiles (a head at 2× life).** `detail tiles` cuts a region into evenly spaced tiles of `--tile` detail px with `--overlap` between neighbours; open, draw and close them one at a time (`detail open --tile K`, `scene --detail` for each tile's reference). `marks` in a tile skips what earlier closed tiles already drew (up to the middle of their overlap), so the overlap isn't drawn twice. Measured (2 vCPU, a 48 × 64 cm sheet at 5 px/mm, a head at 2× life): one 4× tile of 600 × 600 detail px with 2,000 strands and 500 pores took about 2 minutes (open 4 s, its reference render 22 s, the marks 83 s, close 14 s) with 1.6 GB peak memory; results of the seams between tiles in `docs/research/hyperrealism-gaps.md`, G9.

- **What it carries.** Every array of the medium (colour, wetness, height, oil content, gloss, the layer beneath...) is resampled up, so you go on painting into the same wet paint. So are the support's texture (the weave keeps its size: a 4 px thread is 16 px at 4x), the schema, the element masks, the roles, their order and the passes. The focal point carries over in detail coordinates. Its own state lives in `<canvas>_detail_*`; its log continues the canvas's.
- **Units.** Inside the detail every coordinate and size is in detail px. **A 4 px brush on the canvas is a 16 px brush in a 4x detail**: give sizes as canvas px times N. The medium keeps a tool's physical features at their canvas size (oil: the bristle clumps, single hairs, edge widths, glaze pooling and the raking-light relief; charcoal, graphite and pastel: the paper's tooth, fibres and laid lines, lead and line widths, the edge rise, blender and eraser footprints, the dust haze, the sheen and the size caps), so a 16 px brush in a 4x detail makes the same mark as a 4 px brush on the canvas, only resolved. A mechanical pencil keeps its lead (0.3 mm is 6 px at 4x on a graphite sheet). Defaults (sizes, jitter, feather, hatch spacing and length) scale with N. Watercolour, ink, gouache, tempera and woodblock are not scale-aware yet: their grain, hairs and granulation come out finer than on the canvas, and `detail open` says so.
- **The schema.** `scene <scene.json> --detail [draft|final]` renders the canvas's scene for the region. The same camera is turned toward the region with a narrower field of view (a rotation about its centre, so there's no parallax) at the detail's resolution (draft half, final full), and warped onto the detail pixel for pixel, so it registers exactly with the canvas's schema. A 640x800 detail of a head took 167 s at `final` on 2 vCPU. `ref --detail file.png` sets any study. `simplify`, `focus`, `elements` and `report` work on the detail as on a canvas.
- **Closing.** Each array is brought back down. With `area` (the default) each canvas pixel is the mean of its N x N block: antialiased. With `sample` it takes the detail pixel at the canvas pixel's centre, the way the canvas itself samples paint. Where the detail changed nothing, the canvas keeps its own pixels exactly. The change fades out over the margin (default 8% of the region's shorter side; `--margin` in canvas px), so there's no seam, but strokes in the margin dissolve: keep the important marks inside it, and paint the margin with the handling of the surround. Roles are merged in, the detail's episodes are added to the canvas's log (tagged `in_detail`), and its own frames and log are kept in `<canvas>_details/detail_NN/`.
- **Texture after closing** (measured, `docs/gallery/detail_pass_after.jpg`; the same stroke recipe painted on the canvas and through a 4x detail). With `sample`, the fine-texture energy (|L* - blur|) is within 1% of the canvas's at blur radii 0.7, 1.5 and 3 px, and the height relief within 1%. With `area` it is 0.77, 0.87 and 0.93 of the canvas's, and the relief 0.90. The canvas's one-pixel speckle and stair-stepped stroke edges are its own sampling of sub-pixel bristles, and averaging smooths them. That is well inside the variation between passages in the measured oils (Romanticism's texture variation 0.44 ± 0.12, `studio/targets.json`), and the head test showed no seam (the gradient across the region's edge 0.97 of the gradient beside it). Use `sample` where the region must match its surround's handling exactly (a flat passage, a background); keep `area` for faces and fine lines, which alias with `sample` (a line under a canvas pixel wide breaks up, as it would on the canvas).
- **Dry media after closing** (measured, `docs/gallery/detail_pass_graphite.jpg`; hatched tone, blended tone with an eraser line, a 3 px and a 1.5 px line, drawn on the canvas and through a detail, for graphite on drawing paper and on vellum with the hyperrealist's kit, charcoal on drawing and Ingres paper, pastel on toned paper). At 4x the mean value is within 0.5 L* of the canvas's and each passage's within 0.4 L* (at 2x within 1.1 L*: the hatch's own randomness), line widths within 1% (`sample` 4%) and line darkness within 2%. With `sample` the fine-texture energy is 0.98-1.04 of the canvas's at blur radii 0.7, 1.5 and 3 px, the grain in hatched tone 0.96-0.99. With `area` they are 0.82-0.87, 0.88-0.92 and 0.92-0.96, the grain 0.86-0.89, and edges rise over up to 16% more (one canvas px of averaging). A line under 2 canvas px wide (a 1.5 px pencil line on the canvas) differs in darkness by up to 10%, because the canvas can't resolve it. Inside the detail a graphite line has its lead's width (0.35, 0.51 and 0.73 mm for 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7 mm leads) and the canvas's darkness. Time: a 4x detail costs 1.7-7 times the canvas's time for the same passage (16 times the pixels). The eye test (an 800 x 600 detail at 20 px/mm, docs/manual/graphite.md) took about 2 minutes on 2 vCPU. Closed, it is 3.1 L* from its reference at a 1 mm blur (a canvas block-in over the region was 6.8 L* off), with no step at the region's edge.
- **Still painting.** Every mark in a detail is a stroke of the medium's tools, and every detail command also adds a zoomed frame to the canvas's time-lapse, so the time-lapse shows the painter leaning in. The one step that isn't a stroke is the resampling at close. It is the same as stepping back from a passage painted with a small brush: nothing is pasted, traced or generated, and the region is the paint you laid, seen from the canvas's distance. Say in the journal that the head was painted in a detail.
- **What it doesn't do.** A detail doesn't dry or change with the rest of the canvas while it is open (`dry --detail` dries only the detail), and paint laid on the canvas inside the region while it is open is overwritten where the detail paints (the studio warns). `finish` and `sign` are refused in a detail. Details don't nest. oil-v7 has none.
- **How to use it for a head** (docs/subjects/face.md section 6): first block the head in on the canvas with the figure. Open a detail a little larger than the head and neck, so the margin falls on background or hair. Render the scene into it. Then: a lay-in that covers the face, the big planes with `model` (steps 4, brushes of 4-7 canvas px, so 16-28 at 4x), the smaller planes (steps 5-6, 2.5-4 canvas px), a light `blend` on the turning planes, and only then the features as small strokes in the planes' values (the socket's shadow before the eye, the cheek lifted under it for a laugh). Check with `look detail`: the right-hand panel is what the canvas will show.

- **Coordinates:** a canvas point p maps to `(p - X + 0.5) * N - 0.5` in detail pixels (region origin X, scale N); plain `(p - X) * N` sits 3/8 of a canvas pixel off.

## Finishing and signing

- `finish <op> ['<json>']`: the medium's finishing steps (varnish, retouch, fixative, lift, seal, impression...; see the medium's page and docs/FINISHING.md). The finishing stage also includes painter's moves: the last few accents, an edge pass at the focus, the unifying layer the current used. Then check `mirror` and `squint` once more.
- `sign [auto|br|bl]`: sign "Teodor", the very last mark (a medium can sign in its own way: a woodblock print cuts the signature in the keyblock; ink's `finish seal` and woodblock's `finish seal` count as the signature).
- `timelapse`: build the time-lapse video from the recorded frames.
