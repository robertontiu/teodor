# Watercolour (`watercolour`)

**What it is.** Finely ground pigment in gum arabic, thinned with water. It is transparent: every wash is a thin coloured film, and the white of the paper shining through it is the light. You cannot paint a light over a dark; you save the lights (leave the paper, or mask it) and work from light to dark, one glaze over another once the one below is dry. While a wash is wet it moves on its own: colour dropped into it bleeds into soft feathery shapes, water creeping into a half-dry wash pushes the pigment into a cauliflower ("back-run" or "bloom") with a dark, branching edge, and a wash that dries on dry paper leaves a darker line at its edge. Pigment settles into the hollows of the paper (granulation). Colours look darker wet and dry lighter. Code: `studio/v8/media/watercolour.py`, class `Watercolour`, a simplified version of Curtis et al. 1997 (`docs/MEDIA.md`, "Engine model (watercolour)").

**Supports.**
- `cold_press` (default): cotton paper with a felt tooth. Soft ragged edges, visible granulation, medium working time.
- `hot_press`: smooth. Paint stays on the surface longer, so edges are crisp and the dried edge line is the strongest; granulation is faint; lifting works best.
- `rough`: pronounced tooth. Water sinks in fastest (shortest wet-in-wet window), granulation is strongest, fast or dry strokes break up into the paper's pits, and details are hard.
- The ground colour you give `new` is the paper's white (for example `#f6f3eb`). The paper is kept one even white: there is no coloured noise on it.

**Tools** (`"brush": ...`).
- `round` (default): a pointed sable round. Light pressure gives a hairline from the tip; pressing lays the belly down into a broad stroke. It holds a lot of water, so long strokes stay wet; at the end of a long stroke it starts to break up into dry-brush.
- `flat`: a flat wash brush. Broad, even bands with straight edges.
- `mop` (aliases `hake`, `wash`): a big soft brush with lots of water and slightly thinner colour. Even washes and skies; into a wet wash it gives wide soft wet-in-wet shapes.
- `rigger` (alias `liner`): long, thin, even lines that taper (branches, rigging, grasses).
- `dry` (alias `fan`): dry-brush. Little water, splayed hairs: only the paper's peaks take colour, in broken, sparkling streaks. Lighter pressure catches fewer peaks. Nothing flows.
- `lift` (aliases `tissue`, `knife`, `smudge`): a damp clean brush or tissue. Picks a wet wash up almost completely, a dried wash partly, staining colours least. Soft-edged light shapes and lines.
- `sponge`: a sea sponge. A mottled, porous texture (foliage, stone, gravel). Dab rather than drag.
- `water` (alias `clean_damp_brush`): clean water. Wet the paper for wet-in-wet work; laid into a wash that has started drying (damp, no shine) it makes a back-run. Over dry paint it re-dissolves a little non-staining pigment.
- `mask` (alias `masking_fluid`): masking fluid. Reserves the paper with hard edges (shown as a pale yellow film) until `finish unmask`. Paint and washes do not reach the paper under it.
- `blend` (used by the `blend` intention): a damp brush that softens dried edges and wet colour and lifts a little.

**Pigments.** Every colour is painted as a real, measured watercolour pigment from `studio/pigments.json` (Bruce MacEvoy's handprint ratings of 750+ paints). Name one with `"pigment": "PB29"` (a Colour Index name) or a common name (`"ultramarine"`, `"burnt sienna"`, `"Prussian blue"`); otherwise the colour you give is matched to the nearest measured pigment by hue, chroma and value, preferring common paints. The pigment brings its own behaviour: how much it granulates, how firmly it stains, how transparent it is, the darkest value one wash of it can reach, how much lighter it dries, and whether its particles are fine (quinacridones, phthalos, madder: the dried edge line grows with concentration) or coarse (ultramarine, cobalts, earths: a faint, constant edge, more granulation). The colour itself stays the one you gave.

**Parameters that matter** (on any intention that makes strokes, and on `glaze`):
- `pigment`: the measured pigment, as above.
- `water` (or `load`; default 1, `match` uses 1.3): how much water the brush carries, in "drop loads": 1 is a glistening, puddled wash (the load of a measured 50 µL drop), 0.45 a normal stroke. More water: longer strokes, more flow, blooms, a longer wet-in-wet window. Below about 0.35 the brush is dry and the marks break up on the paper's peaks.
- `strength` (default 1): pigment concentration. The colour you give is how the paint looks at strength 1 over white; 0.3 is a pale tint. More pigment darkens less and less and stops at the pigment's darkest single wash (Prussian blue about L* 25, ultramarine 30, quinacridone violet 40, yellow ochre 65): darker than that takes a second glaze over the dry first one.
- `granulation`, `staining` (0 to 1), `opacity`: override the pigment's measured values (granulation and staining are its handprint ratings / 4; opacity is 0.6 × (1 − transparency / 4)). Staining colours (phthalo, alizarin, Prussian blue) barely lift; most paints stain (median rating 3 of 4).
- `fine` (0 to 1): override the particle size class (1 = colloidal).
- Felt state: fast, agitated strokes skip over the tooth (more on rough paper); pressure widens the round.
- `match`: the painter works like a watercolourist. At the start of each stroke the brush decides once whether the place needs a glaze at all (a difference below about 5% darkening is left alone, `"skip"` changes the threshold); once down, the stroke is continuous, carrying the transparent colour that, over what is already dry, gives the schema's colour (less pigment where less is needed). Strokes of 14 px and more carry a soft, wet margin of clean water, so neighbouring strokes of one episode join into one wash and their edges bleed out softly; smaller strokes stay crisp (accents). Match can only darken: plan the lights (mask, or leave them) before matching. For large areas use `glaze` or `match` with the `mop`, `dry` between rounds, and finish with small rounds for crisp accents.

**Glaze (a wash).** `glaze` lays a flat wash of water and pigment over the region: a transparent Kubelka-Munk film. `opacity` is the amount of paint (0.2 to 0.4 is a normal wash, 0.8 to 1 is the colour at full strength; more saturates toward the pigment's darkest), `water` the load (default 1, a glistening wash), `pigment` as above. The region's `feather` decides the edge: a small feather gives a hard edge, a large one fades out softly. A hard edge dries with a darker line only for fine pigments, and the more pigment the wider and darker it gets (quinacridone, alizarin); coarse pigments (ultramarine, earths) dry with an even, plain edge. A wash over a dry wash deepens it (yellow over blue gives green, layer by layer); a wash into a wet wash mixes with it wet-in-wet. Colour dropped into a wash laid earlier in the same episode blooms wet-in-wet; strokes laid side by side on dry paper in one episode stay crisp and join when the water moves at the episode's end.

**Drying and time.** One episode is about ten minutes of studio time. A wash of water 1 (glistening) loses its shine after about three episodes and is dry after about four (40 minutes, the measured drying time of a 50 µL drop on 300 g/m² cold-press paper); a normal stroke (water about 0.45) is dry after two; drying time grows in proportion to the water. While it shines, colour dropped in bleeds softly (wet-in-wet); in the damp episode after the shine has gone, wetter paint or water makes back-runs. Hot-press keeps the shine a little longer, rough paper drinks the water fastest (the shortest wet-in-wet window). Between episodes the water flows inside the wet area, pigment settles and granulates (coarse pigment settles fastest), and suspended fine pigment is carried to the edges where the wash meets dry paper. When a pixel dries its wash becomes a film over everything below, and its colour lightens by the pigment's drying shift (Prussian blue most, about 68%; most paints 10-20%; chromium oxide green darkens a little). `dry 0.5` lets about two episodes pass; `dry 1` dries everything. Dry before painting crisp details, or they will bleed.

**Finishing** (`finish <op> ['<json>']`):
- `darks '{"n": 30, "size": 8}'`: final darks and crisp accents, wet-on-dry, at most `n` short strokes where the schema is darkest and the painting is not yet dark enough (needs a schema). Dries the painting first.
- `lift '{"region": {...}, "strength": 0.6, "soften": 6}'`: lift soft lights from the dried paint (`rect`, `ellipse` or `poly`, as in intentions). Without a region it lifts where the schema is lightest and the painting has gone too dark. Staining colours lift less; hot-press lifts best.
- `unmask '{"soften": 2}'`: rub off the masking fluid, revealing the paper; `soften` (px) blurs the hard edges it leaves.
- `dry`: let the painting dry completely.
- `mount`: no varnish (watercolour goes under glass, with a mat); it dries the painting and changes nothing else.
- Then `sign`, the very last act.

**Order of work.** Light drawing (`transfer`, lightly). Mask the brightest whites. Large pale washes (sky, ground), often wet-in-wet: wet the area with `water` or lay a pale `glaze`, then drop colour in with the `mop` or `round` in the next episode. `dry 1`. Midtone glazes and shapes wet-on-dry. Darks and details with the `round` and `rigger`, dry-brush for texture. `finish darks`, `finish lift`, `finish unmask`, then `sign`.

**Limits.** The pigments of one wash are summed into one film (absorption per channel and one scattering value), so two pigments in one wash do not separate by granulation, and the order of dried layers is kept only in the composited colour (lifting treats all dried layers alike). The water flow is diffusion inside the wet area, not real fluid dynamics: no running downhill when the paper is tilted, and blooms grow from noise in the paper rather than from capillary flow. There is no salt, spattering or scratching out. The wet area moves only at episode ends. A colour is one pigment: a mixed colour (a green from blue and yellow, a grey) is matched to the single nearest pigment, which may be an unexpected one; name the pigment when its behaviour matters.

**Calibration** (Nov 25, 2026; the full report with all numbers is `docs/calibration/watercolour.md`).
- Set from data: per-pigment granulation, staining, transparency and darkest single-wash value from the handprint ratings in `studio/pigments.json` [P3]; the wet-to-dry shift per pigment from the 12 measured shifts (the others take their median as a density ratio); edge darkening that grows with concentration (in width and darkness) for fine pigments and stays constant for coarse ones, and the faster settling of coarse pigment, after González-Gutiérrez et al. 2019 [W1]; the saturating deposit vs concentration [W1]; drying time from their 50 µL drop on 300 g/m² cold-press paper (t_c = 2490 s), with drying proportional to the load.
- Fitted to the real references (The Met watercolours, `tools/calibrate_media.py`, compared at 8 px/mm): the size of the edge darkening (rim ratio of isolated washes: real 0.92-1.06, n = 3; now 0.96-1.00 for coarse pigments and 1.05-1.13 for fine ones, rising with load), the granulation and paper-texture amplitude on cold press (std L* in flat fields: paper 1.14 vs real 1.11; non-granulating wash 1.13 vs real 1.15-1.30; ultramarine 2.19 vs a granulating real wash 2.13), and the edges of a match painting (edge rise 1.9 mm and hard-edge share 0.26 at 4 px/mm, real median 1.9 mm and 0.24).
