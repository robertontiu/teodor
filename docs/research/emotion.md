# How paintings carry feeling: the research, and what it means for Teodor

This is Teodor's counterpart to Spectre's research base (Juslin & Västfjäll; Eerola, Friberg & Bresin; Gabrielsson & Lindström). It collects what peer-reviewed work says about how a picture carries emotion and turns each finding into cues a painter agent can use.

Two labels, as in Spectre's `docs/PRODUCTION.md`:
- **[S]** what a source found, with its evidence strength (sample size, replication).
- **[H]** what we infer for the painter. These are hypotheses for Teodor, not findings.

A distinction to keep throughout, borrowed from music research: an emotion a viewer **perceives** in a picture ("this painting is sad") is not the same as one the viewer **feels** ("this painting makes me sad"). Most of the studies below measure perceived emotion. Perceived emotion is what Teodor's critics report, and it is what the cues below can steer most reliably. Felt emotion depends far more on the viewer.

Every source named in the text is listed, with a link, under **Sources** at the end. Where a summary below rests on an abstract rather than the full paper, that is said.

---

## 1. Models of aesthetic and emotional experience

**[S] Findings.**
- **Leder et al. (2004)** describe looking at art as a sequence of five stages: perception (contrast, colour, symmetry, grouping), explicit classification (style, content), implicit classification (familiarity, prototypicality), cognitive mastering (making sense of it) and evaluation. It produces two outputs: an **aesthetic emotion** (pleasure, or not) and an **aesthetic judgement** (good, or not). The model's core claim is that much of the reward of art, especially modern and abstract art, comes from the **need to understand** being satisfied. Leder & Nadal (2014) reviewed ten years of work on the model and describe the "aesthetic episode" as unfolding over time within and across perceptual, cognitive and emotional components, rather than emotion arriving only at the end. Evidence: a descriptive framework supported by many separate experiments, not one decisive test.
- **Pelowski et al. (2017), VIMAP**, extends Leder's model with top-down processes: how viewers adapt or change during the experience. It is built around three processing "checks" that sort the possible outcomes of looking at art, from mundane to "moving, disturbing, transformative". The transformative outcome, as Pelowski describes it elsewhere (Pelowski 2015), starts from a discrepancy the viewer cannot resolve at first and ends with a change in their understanding. VIMAP is where chills, awe and the sublime are placed. Evidence: a theory paper that sets out hypotheses; it is not a tested law.
- **Chatterjee & Vartanian (2014), the aesthetic triad**: aesthetic experience emerges from the interaction of three brain systems, **sensory-motor** (what the marks and colours do to perception and the body), **emotion-valuation** (reward, liking) and **meaning-knowledge** (context, story, expertise). Evidence: a review of neuroimaging and lesion work; it organises evidence rather than predicting specific cues.
- **Menninghaus et al. (2019)** define aesthetic emotions as those that feed directly into aesthetic evaluation. Unlike everyday emotion lists, the aesthetic vocabulary is mostly positive, but many positive aesthetic emotions contain negative or mixed ingredients (being moved, the sublime, nostalgia). The appraisals that matter most are intrinsic pleasantness, familiarity and novelty. Their team built a questionnaire, the Aesthemos (Schindler et al. 2017), with 21 subscales covering, among others, being moved, awe, fascination, nostalgia, confusion and boredom. Evidence: a theory paper plus a psychometric tool validated on several samples; contested by Skov & Nadal (2020), who argue there are no special "aesthetic" emotions, only ordinary ones (a debate about labels, not about the cues below).
- **Silvia (2005, 2009)** applies appraisal theory: an emotion depends on how the viewer appraises the picture, not on the picture alone. **Interest** comes from two appraisals together: the picture is **new or complex** *and* the viewer feels **able to understand it** (coping potential). Without the second, the same complexity gives **confusion**. Silvia (2009) argues for taking the "knowledge emotions" (interest, confusion, surprise) and hostile ones (anger, disgust) seriously in art. Evidence: a series of experiments by Silvia and colleagues, mostly with US student samples; the reviews summarise this programme rather than one decisive study.

**[H] What it means for a painter agent.**
1. A painting is read in stages. **In the first second** the viewer gets only the perceptual layer (value pattern, colour, large shapes, whether there is a face or body). **Then** they classify (what is it, what style). **Then** they try to make sense of it. Each stage can carry or lose the feeling, and Teodor's critics follow the same order: the "three-second test" is the perceptual stage; "what is this a picture of" is classification; "what does it mean" is mastering.
2. The feeling must be **present in the perceptual layer**, not only in the meaning layer. Round 4's pride, which lived only in clues (height marks on a doorpost), is an example of a feeling placed only in the meaning layer, where many viewers never arrive.
3. **Give the viewer something to understand, and let them succeed.** Interest needs novelty plus coping potential (Silvia). A painting that is all novelty with no foothold reads as confusion (round 13's first critic: "an odd group portrait"). One clear, legible anchor, such as a readable gesture or a clear light, plus one thing that resists, is the recipe the evidence points to.
4. **Moving and transformative experiences start from a mismatch** that later resolves (Pelowski). In practice: something that at first seems wrong (a detail, a scale, a colour where it shouldn't be) that, on a second look, turns out to be the point. Round 14b's shadow that "kept both legs" worked this way for its final viewer.
5. Teodor's critics report aesthetic emotions (interest, tenderness, being moved) and depicted emotions (grief, dread) in the same breath. When reading critiques, separate "what emotion the picture shows" from "what the picture made me feel" and from "how good I found it".

## 2. Colour and emotion

**[S] Findings.**
- **Lightness and saturation matter more than hue.** Valdez & Mehrabian (1994; several experiments with student samples, controlled Munsell colour chips) found that brightness and saturation predicted emotional responses more strongly than hue. Pleasure rose with brightness and with saturation; arousal rose with saturation (with a U-shaped relation to brightness, and the highest arousal for green-yellow, as Wilms & Oberfeld 2018 summarise it); and, in the regression equations usually quoted from the paper, dominance (feeling of power) rose as colours got **darker** and more saturated. Wilms & Oberfeld note that Valdez & Mehrabian probably did not vary brightness and saturation fully independently.
- **Wilms & Oberfeld (2018)** varied hue (blue, green, red), saturation and brightness independently (N = 62, 30-second exposures to LED colours, with skin conductance and heart rate). **Saturated and bright colours raised arousal** and gave stronger skin-conductance responses; arousal rose from blue and green to red; valence was highest for saturated, bright colours. Interactions mattered: blue was the most pleasant hue only when it was highly saturated. This is a measure of felt emotion, and it is one of the better-controlled studies.
- **Cross-cultural agreement is high.** Jonauskaite et al. (2020) asked 4,598 people from 30 nations (22 languages) to link 20 emotions to 12 colour terms. The pattern of colour-emotion links was very similar across nations (average similarity r = .88), though nation still predicted local differences beyond the universal pattern, and similarity rose with linguistic and geographic closeness. The same group repeated the task with 7,393 people aged 16 to 88 from 31 countries (Jonauskaite et al. 2024): age groups agreed even more closely (similarity .97), older people giving fewer but more positive associations.
- **A systematic review gives the stable map.** Jonauskaite & Mohr (2025) reviewed 132 articles (42,266 participants, 64 countries, 1895 to 2022). Most links are many-to-many, driven by lightness, saturation and hue ("colour temperature"). The consistent ones: **light** colours with positive and **dark** with negative emotions; **red** with empowering, high-arousal emotions, both positive and negative (love *and* anger); **yellow and orange** with positive, high-arousal emotions (joy); **blue, green, green-blue and white** with positive, low-arousal emotions (calm, relief); **pink** with positive emotions (love, tenderness); **purple** with empowering emotions; **grey** with negative, low-arousal emotions (sadness, boredom); **black** with negative, high-arousal emotions (fear, anger) as well as sadness.
- **How robust, and the main caveat.** The review's own caveat: almost all of this was tested as *association* (which colour goes with which word), not as what people feel when they look at a coloured picture. Colour terms and colour patches give similar patterns (r = .82 in Jonauskaite et al. 2020, i-Perception; N = 132), except for purple, which did not match at all. So these links are robust as a shared *code*, which is what a painter needs to be read, but weaker as a cause of felt emotion.
- **In pictures, people use the same code.** Damiano et al. (2023; 81 artists and non-artists made 972 abstract colour and line drawings of six emotions; 242 viewers): **anger** was mostly red and the densest drawing; **fear** black, grey and dark blue; **sadness** dark blue, grey and black, with the fewest colours; **joy** yellow, pink and cyan; **wonder** yellow, pink and dark blue, with the most colours; **disgust** greens, browns and dark yellows. Viewers named the intended emotion at 40 to 43% for colour drawings, against 16.7% chance, and at about 32% for line drawings only. Colour carried more than line.

**[H] What it means for a painter agent.**
1. **Value (lightness) first, then saturation, then hue.** As Spectre has "mode and tempo first", Teodor has **key and intensity first**: the overall lightness of the picture sets valence (light = positive, dark = negative); overall saturation sets arousal (saturated = high, greyed = low). Hue refines inside that frame. A painting's feeling should be legible in a grayscale thumbnail (the studio's `look gray`) *and* in a blurred colour thumbnail.
2. **Use colour as a shared code, not as proof.** Red reads as strong and charged but does not say *which* charge (love or anger); the other cues decide. Black reads as fear or anger more than as sadness; grey and desaturated blue read as sadness and low energy.
3. **Number of colours is a cue.** Few colours read as concentrated negative states (anger, fear, sadness); many colours read as wonder, disgust or open joy (Damiano et al. 2023).
4. **Dark is power, not only gloom.** Valdez & Mehrabian found darker, saturated colours raise dominance. Dark, rich colours suit pride, menace and authority; dark greyed colours suit grief and despair.
5. **Don't rely on purple.** Its meaning was the least stable across languages and between word and patch.

## 3. Line, shape and contour

**[S] Findings.**
- **Lines carry feeling by imitating movement.** Lundholm (1921) asked 8 people (none of them artists) to draw pure lines for 48 feeling words. His conclusions, still the clearest statement of the idea: the feeling of a line "is probably dependent upon the suggestion of movement in the lines; that is, the lines appear to imitate in their movement the motor expression of emotional states". Long, low waves suggest slow, weak movement (sad, quiet, lazy); small waves and acute angles suggest fast, intense movement (merry, agitated, furious). Sad, lazy and quiet lines looked almost the same; so did furious and excited ones. **A jolly line and a furious line differed mainly in how often acute angles occurred.** Broad lines read as strong, thin ones as weak. Poffenberger & Barrows (1924) and Lundholm both found angular lines linked to "agitating, hard, furious" and curved lines to "gentle, sad, quiet, lazy" (as summarised by Bertamini et al. 2015). Evidence: tiny early samples, but the pattern has been reproduced for a century.
- **Curved is preferred, angular is threat.** Bar & Neta (2006) showed people like curved versions of everyday objects more than sharp-angled versions of the same objects; Bar & Neta (2007) found the amygdala more active for sharp objects. Leder, Tinio & Bar (2011) found the curvature preference only for neutral and positive objects, not negative ones (a curved bomb is not liked more). A meta-analysis of 61 studies, 106 samples (Chuquichambi et al. 2022) puts the effect at Hedges' g = 0.39, a medium effect, **"reliable but not universal"**: it weakens with longer viewing, varies with stimulus type, expertise and task. Bertamini et al. (2015) argue part of it is a dislike of angles rather than a love of curves. Friedenberg et al. (2023; two small experiments, N = 25 and 27): the sharper the angle, the more threatening it looked.
- **Downward V-shapes signal threat.** Aronoff and colleagues traced the geometry of threat in faces and art: threatening masks across cultures used angular and diagonal patterns (Aronoff, Barclay & Stevenson 1988); the angry face's key configuration is a downward-pointing V (brows), the happy face's a rounded one (Aronoff, Woike & Hyman 1992). The same split held in classical ballet postures and in 17th-century Dutch paintings (summarised in Aronoff 2006). Larson et al. (2009, fMRI) found a bare downward-pointing triangle activated the amygdala and related threat areas more than the same triangle pointing up.
- **In drawings made to express emotion, the same features appear.** Damiano et al. (2023): angry line drawings were the densest, with the most contours and with angular contours; negative emotions (anger, disgust, fear, sadness) had angular contours and shorter lines; **joy** had the lowest density, the fewest contours, no sharp angles and medium-to-long lines; **wonder** long lines and no sharp angles; **sadness** had the most vertical lines.
- **Arnheim (1954/1974)**, the classic art-theory source, describes the same dynamics: obliques and diagonals read as movement and tension, horizontals and verticals as rest and stability, and every shape as a pattern of "directed tensions". This is theory and close observation of artworks, not experiment; it agrees with the experiments above but should be treated as a guide, not a finding.

**[H] What it means for a painter agent.**
1. **Line = imagined movement.** Draw the contour or stroke the way a body in that feeling would move: slow, long, low waves for sadness and calm; small quick waves for excitement; acute angles for anger and fear. This matches Teodor's felt-state model (arousal → speed, tension → jagged corners, valence → smooth vs angular).
2. **Angles turn energy negative.** High arousal without angles reads as joy or exhilaration; the same arousal with frequent acute angles reads as fury or panic. Lundholm's "jolly vs furious = frequency of acute angles" is the single most usable line cue.
3. **Low arousal is ambiguous by line alone.** Sad, quiet and lazy lines look the same. Line cannot separate grief from serenity; value, colour and posture must (see section 10, "Nearest wrong feelings").
4. **Downward-pointing Vs and wedges threaten.** Use them (brows, rooflines, shards, shadows, crowd shapes) for anger, menace and dread; keep them out of tender pictures.
5. **Curves make a picture approachable, not happy.** Curvature raises liking but does not by itself add joy; in a negative scene it does little.
6. **Line weight is strength.** Broad, heavy lines for strength and pride; thin, faint lines for weakness, fragility, shame.

## 4. Composition, balance, gaze and viewing distance

**[S] Findings.**
- **The first glance is a gist, and it is structural.** Locher et al. (2007): after a single 100 ms glance at eight famous paintings, untrained viewers' written reactions overwhelmingly described a holistic impression of the **structural arrangement and meaning** of the picture. Eye-tracking then showed the familiar two phases: a quick global survey, followed by scrutiny of selected parts guided by what the viewer was trying to evaluate. Van Paasschen et al. (2014) note that emotional responses to images can start about 120 ms after onset, before objects are recognised.
- **Good compositions are perceptibly organised, but the sensitivity is coarse.** Locher (2003): untrained viewers told originals by renowned painters from versions with *major* disruptions of their structure better than chance, but not from *minor* ones. Gershoni & Hochstein (2011) found viewers agree with one another about balance even at first fixation, while a formula for balance based on Arnheim's symmetry axes matched their ratings poorly. Locher, Overbeeke & Stappers (2005) showed the perceived weight of a colour area in Mondrian adaptations depends on its size and its colour (red and yellow especially). Evidence: a handful of lab studies, small samples (tens of viewers), mostly with untrained viewers.
- **Where an object sits in the frame is read as meaning.** Palmer, Gardner & Wickens (2008): with a single object, people prefer it near the centre (**centre bias**), and a figure facing sideways is preferred **facing into the frame** rather than out of it (**inward bias**). Sammartino & Palmer (2012, JEP:HPP): objects that live above us (a flying eagle) are preferred high in the frame, objects below us (a bowl) low. But these defaults flip with meaning: with the title "Departing", people preferred a rear view of a plane, and with titles that implied it, off-centre and outward-facing compositions were preferred (Sammartino & Palmer 2012, *Perception*: "representational fit").
- **Left-to-right is a cultural direction.** Maass & Russo (2003): Italians imagine actions running left to right and place the agent on the left; Arabic readers reverse this, with an extra small left-to-right bias in both groups. Movement left-to-right reads as "forward" for Western viewers.
- **Content steers the eyes more than form when there are people.** Massaro et al. (2012; N = 42, eye-tracking): with paintings of people, gaze was driven by content (the figures); with landscapes, by low-level features (contrast, colour, dynamism). Vogt & Magnussen (2007; 9 artists, 9 laypeople): laypeople look at people and objects, artists at structure. Quiroga & Pedreira (2011): fixations go to salient regions, but with large individual variation on figurative paintings.
- **Detail guides the eye.** DiPaola, Riebe & Enns (2010) argue that Rembrandt varied detail selectively ("textural agency") to steer the gaze, and showed in the lab that viewers' eyes go to the regions of sharp detail and that they prefer portraits where detail sits around the eyes.
- **Viewing time and distance.** In a museum, visitors looked at each Gerhard Richter painting for 33 seconds on average (median 25 s; N = 225), and stood further back from larger canvases (1.5 to 2.1 m; r² = .93 between size and distance) (Carbon 2017). An older study of the Metropolitan Museum found a median of about 17 seconds per work (Smith & Smith 2001). The feeling must arrive within those seconds.
- **Tension and its resolution** in a picture (diagonals, forces pulling against the frame, an off-balance weight) is a central idea in art theory (Arnheim 1954/1974) but has little direct experimental support as a cause of *emotion*. Treat it as craft knowledge.

**[H] What it means for a painter agent.**
1. **The gist decides the feeling.** Teodor's critics give a first reading the way Locher's viewers did. Check the painting at thumbnail size and blurred: the value pattern and the big shapes must carry the feeling alone.
2. **Use the defaults, then break them for meaning.** Centred, inward-facing, at the "natural" height reads as stable and right. Breaking a default reads as meaning *only when the feeling supports it*: a figure low in the frame with empty space above (smallness, grief, being crushed); a figure facing out of the frame (leaving, longing, shame turning away); high in the frame (elation, pride, floating). Round 8's lesson (direction of travel decided the reading) is exactly this.
3. **Direction is read left-to-right.** For a European viewer, movement to the right reads as going forward, toward the future; movement to the left as going back, returning, retreating.
4. **Put the sharpest detail and strongest contrast where the feeling lives** (a face, a hand, the contact between two bodies). If there is a figure, the eye will go to it first and read its posture; if there is none, low-level contrast and colour lead the eye.
5. **Balance is coarse.** Viewers only feel large compositional disruptions. A deliberate imbalance must be big to be felt.
6. **Design for 2 m and 30 seconds.** The feeling has to read from a step back in half a minute; small clues (round 4's height marks) are bonuses for the few who lean in.

## 5. Brushstrokes and the artist's gesture

**[S] Findings.**
- **The theory.** Freedberg & Gallese (2007) propose that viewers respond to art partly by **embodied simulation**: the brain covertly simulates the actions, emotions and bodily sensations an image implies, including "the artist's gestures" visible in the marks (a slash in a canvas, the trace of a brushstroke). They present this as universal and as coming before interpretation. Evidence: a theory paper.
- **Motor areas respond to the traces of gestures.** Umiltà et al. (2012, EEG; Lucio Fontana's cut canvases): motor-related mu-rhythm suppression occurred when people looked at the original cuts, but not at graphically altered control images. Sbriscia-Fioretti et al. (2013, EEG; Franz Kline's black-and-white paintings) found premotor and motor activity, plus reward-related areas. Both are small EEG studies (about 10 to 20 participants), and the step from "motor cortex responds" to "the viewer feels the gesture" is an inference.
- **Viewers covertly re-enact strokes.** Taylor, Witt & Grimaldi (2012; five experiments): people moving their arm as if painting horizontal strokes responded faster when their movement matched the direction of visible brushstrokes in a painting, even though the painting was irrelevant to the task and outside their awareness.
- **Matching the artist's movement changes liking.** Leder, Bär & Topolinski (2012): people who made stippling movements while rating liked pointillist paintings more, and people who made sweeping strokes liked stroke-style paintings more. Ticini et al. (2014; N = 18) found the same direction with a primed hand grip, but the effect was tiny (about 0.1 on a 9-point scale).
- **Human movement dynamics are visible in the line and are preferred.** Chamberlain et al. (2022; four experiments): drawing movements with human-like bell-shaped velocity profiles (minimum-jerk or sigma-lognormal) were seen as more natural and more pleasant than constant-speed movements. In static line drawings, the visual traces of natural movement were preferred **only by art students**. Babcock & Freyd (1988) showed people can extract production information, such as stroke direction, from static handwriting.
- **Motion is perceived in abstract paintings, and is not only simulation.** Humphries et al. (2021; 43 people with Parkinson's disease, 40 controls): patients saw less motion in both Pollock (high motion) and Mondrian (low motion) paintings, though Mondrian's have no visible gesture. The authors conclude the motor system may integrate low-level visual features into a sense of movement, rather than simulate specific actions.
- **Can viewers read the *emotion* of a mark?** Partly. Lundholm (1921) and Damiano et al. (2023) show that lines made to express an emotion are recognised above chance (about 32% for line drawings against 17% chance in Damiano et al.), mainly through density, angularity, length and orientation, the visible traces of speed and force. No study we found shows viewers can recover the specific emotion *of a single brushstroke in a painting*; this is the weakest link in the chain.

**[H] What it means for a painter agent.**
1. **The mark is a signal of a body.** Viewers read the speed, force and steadiness of the hand from the trace, and they prefer traces that move like a human hand (accelerate, cruise, decelerate). Teodor's minimum-jerk speed profile is supported by this evidence; uniform, constant-speed dabs are not. This is the research reason behind the critics' repeated complaint that the surface "reads as a digital filter".
2. **Let the stroke vary inside itself.** Width, pressure and paint load should change along a stroke the way they do with a real hand: a landing, a swell, a lift. Uniform stamps carry no gesture to simulate.
3. **Match the gesture to the feeling, where the viewer will see it.** Large, visible, directional strokes for high-arousal feelings (anger, ecstasy, panic); small, careful, overlapping strokes for tenderness and care; slow, long, dragging strokes for grief and exhaustion; broken, scratchy, hesitant marks for anxiety. Where strokes stay visible, their direction is felt as movement (Taylor et al.).
4. **Don't expect a single stroke to name the feeling.** Gesture adds arousal and tension; colour, value and figure tell *which* feeling it is.

## 6. Faces, bodies, posture and gesture

**[S] Findings.**
- **Pictures with faces and bodies are read more alike.** In the WikiArt Emotions dataset (Mohammad & Kiritchenko 2018; 4,105 artworks, 10 annotators each, 308 people in total), agreement was "markedly higher for art with a body than with no body, and markedly higher again for art that depicts a face". Agreement was higher for Renaissance and post-Renaissance art than for modern and contemporary art, and higher for basic emotions (fear, happiness, sadness, anger, love) than for optimism, shame, guilt and regret. Overall agreement was modest (Fleiss' κ from close to 0 up to 0.31). Titles changed the emotion in a large share of works.
- **The body carries emotion on its own, fast.** De Gelder (2006) reviews evidence that emotional body language is perceived quickly and automatically, much like faces. Meeren, van Heijnsbergen & de Gelder (2005): when a face and a body disagree (fearful face on an angry body), judgements of the face are biased toward the body's emotion, with a brain response within about 115 ms.
- **At peak intensity the body, not the face, says whether it's joy or agony.** Aviezer, Trope & Todorov (2012): in photos of people at the peak of real wins and losses, viewers could tell positive from negative from the isolated body but not from the isolated face, and they "saw" the face's emotion according to the body it was put on.
- **Static postures are recognisable, unevenly.** Coulson (2004; 61 viewers, 176 mannequin postures, three viewpoints): some anger and sadness postures got 90% agreement or more; disgust was barely recognisable ("there may be no static body posture for disgust other than the act of retching"); **happiness and surprise were confused with each other**, the other emotions rarely. Features: **sadness** was the only emotion with a forward head bend, plus a forward chest bend and arms at the sides; **happiness** a head tilted back and arms raised above the shoulders with straight elbows; **anger** head back with arms raised forward; **fear** head back with raised forearms; **surprise** head and chest back with arms raised. **Front views gave the most agreement; sadness was recognised less often from behind.** Dael, Mortillaro & Scherer (2012; 10 actors, 12 emotions) found that some emotions have one typical body pattern but most are expressed by several.
- **Pride and shame have distinct, cross-cultural displays.** Pride: a small smile, head tilted slightly back, an expanded chest, arms raised or hands on hips (Tracy & Robins 2004, three experiments). Olympic and Paralympic athletes from more than 30 nations, including the congenitally blind, showed the pride display after winning and the shame display (head down, shoulders slumped, chest narrowed) after losing (Tracy & Matsumoto 2008). Keltner (1995) separated shame (gaze and head down) from embarrassment (gaze away, controlled smile, face touch).
- **Faces are less reliable than once thought.** A large review (Barrett et al. 2019) concludes that people do not reliably make one fixed facial configuration per emotion and that context shapes how faces are read. In paintings, the context *is* the picture.
- **Art context mutes joy, not pain.** Gerger, Leder & Kremer (2014): labelling an image "artwork" rather than "press photograph" reduced joy (and smile-muscle activity) but left anger, disgust, fear, sadness and shame unchanged; negative artworks were *liked* more in the art context. The authors relate this to emotional distancing (see Menninghaus et al. 2017 in section 7).

**[H] What it means for a painter agent.**
1. **A figure is the strongest emotion cue a painting has, and the posture is its loudest part.** If a feeling can be carried by a body, give the body a readable posture first, then the face. This is why Teodor's figurative rounds (from round 3) read more clearly than the abstract ones.
2. **Stop painting the back of the figure when the feeling lives in the figure.** Front and three-quarter views were read most consistently; sadness was read *less* from behind. The figure-from-behind is Teodor's top habit, and the research says it costs legibility (round 12 learned the same: satire needs a face).
3. **Use the documented postures.** Sadness: head and chest forward and down, arms hanging. Shame: head down, shoulders in, the body made small, often turned away. Pride: chest open, head slightly back, arms out or on hips, small smile. Joy: head back, arms up and out, straight elbows. Anger: arms forward, weight forward, fists. Fear: head back, forearms raised, weight pulled away. Surprise looks like joy unless the face and context separate them.
4. **Let the body decide the face.** At high intensity, the body decides whether an open-mouthed face is ecstasy or agony. Make the two agree, or disagree on purpose.
5. **Abstraction costs agreement.** Without faces or bodies, viewers agree less on the emotion. Abstract commissions must work harder through colour, value, line and gesture (sections 2, 3, 5, 8).
6. **Joy is the hard one to make felt in a painting** (the art frame mutes it); grief, fear and anger survive the frame intact.

## 7. Expectation, surprise, ambiguity and being moved

**[S] Findings.**
- **Insight is pleasure.** Muth & Carbon (2013): people looked repeatedly at hard-to-see two-tone images (hidden faces) and matched meaningless images; *having an insight*, and how strong it was, predicted a later rise in liking. Muth, Hesslinger & Carbon (2015) tied appreciation of artworks to their ambiguity, how solvable it felt, and the opportunity for insight. Muth & Carbon (2016) name **Semantic Instability (SeIns)**: artworks that hold several possible meanings. They propose that pleasure comes not only from resolving it but from *anticipating* a resolution, which is why unresolvable works can still please. Evidence: one clear experiment (small student sample) plus a follow-up; SeIns itself is a theory paper.
- **Some ambiguity is liked, too much is not.** Jakesch & Leder (2009): artworks shown with spoken statements that partly matched and partly contradicted them; only the *level of ambiguity* (the proportion of matching to non-matching information) predicted liking and interest, and a middle level was preferred.
- **Being moved is mixed, social and intense at low arousal.** Menninghaus et al. (2015; three studies): being moved is mostly triggered by **relationship and life events** (death, birth, marriage, separation, reunion); its two main versions are sad and joyful, both mixing positive and negative feeling; it is felt as highly intense although arousal is only low to medium; and it happened for fictional artworks as much as for real events. Zickfeld et al. (2019; 3,542 people, 19 countries, 15 languages): "kama muta", the experience labelled being moved, is evoked by a **sudden intensification of communal sharing** (closeness, belonging, love made visible) and comes with warmth in the chest, moist eyes, chills and a lump in the throat.
- **Transformation starts from a discrepancy.** Pelowski (2015; theory plus three museum surveys in the UK, Japan and the US, including what the paper calls the 20th century's most tear-inducing abstract paintings): "feeling like crying" in front of art went with the full progression of discrepancy, then schema change, then a new understanding, and with rating the art as good, beautiful and understood.
- **Negative feelings are a resource in art, held at a distance.** Menninghaus et al. (2017), the Distancing-Embracing model: the art frame gives safety and control, which lets viewers *embrace* sadness, fear or disgust as intense, interesting and moving; mixing positive and negative feeling in one work is one of the ways artworks do this. Gerger et al. (2014) found the data side: negative artworks were liked more in an art context (section 6).
- **Awe needs vastness and a need to accommodate.** Keltner & Haidt (2003): the two core appraisals of awe are *perceived vastness* (size, power, number, complexity) and *a need for accommodation* (the experience does not fit existing mental structures). A theory paper, but one that has shaped a large literature since.
- **Taste for art is personal.** Vessel et al. (2018): viewers agreed strongly about which faces and landscapes they liked but much less about artworks and architecture. Vessel, Starr & Rubin (2012): the artworks that move a person most are idiosyncratic to that person and engage the brain's default mode network (self-related thought). Evidence: solid lab studies; agreement on *liking* is not agreement on *perceived emotion*, which is higher (sections 2, 8).

**[H] What it means for a painter agent.**
1. **Plan one discrepancy, with certainty around it.** The visual equivalent of Spectre's "build certainty first, then spend one surprise": set up a clear, legible picture, then put one thing in it that does not fit at first and that, once understood, *is* the feeling (round 14b's shadow that stayed on the floor; round 13's phone filming the scene). Random oddness everywhere is just confusion.
2. **Keep ambiguity at a middle level.** One question the viewer can work on, not five. Teodor's critics who wrote "an odd group portrait" or "puzzled" were past the useful level.
3. **For being moved, paint a relationship at a moment of intensified closeness or loss**: a reunion, a farewell, care given, someone held. Low-to-medium arousal (no frenzy), high intensity, a mix of warm and sad. Round 15's hands (his hand over hers) are the right kind of cue; the research suggests the closeness must be made more visible (bodies leaning in) to move a viewer.
4. **Negative feelings are not a risk to avoid.** Grief, dread and shame survive the art frame (and are often liked more for it). A painting with a positive core gains depth from a negative element held inside it, if that element has its own form (round 11's lesson).
5. **Awe needs size and something that does not fit**: a vast scale relative to a figure or the viewer, and something that cannot be taken in at once.
6. **Don't expect consensus on liking.** Measure Teodor against whether critics *perceive* the intended feeling, not against whether they like the painting.

## 8. Abstract art and emotion: do viewers agree?

**[S] Findings.**
- **Yes, on the broad dimensions.** Van Paasschen et al. (2014): 19 people with no art background rated 340 abstract artworks on valence and arousal; agreement across observers was high (intra-class correlation) and they used the whole scale. **Works with many edges (complex) and dark colours were rated more arousing and more negative; works with clear lines, bright colours and geometric shapes more positive and calmer.** Van Paasschen, Bacci & Melcher (2015; 40 novices, 20 experts, 150 abstract works and portraits, lab and museum): experts liked the works more, but valence and arousal ratings did not differ between experts and novices. The affective reading of abstract art is largely shared; liking depends on the viewer. Melcher & Bacci (2013) review this programme.
- **Yes, for basic emotions, well above chance but far from perfect.** Damiano et al. (2023): viewers identified the intended emotion (one of six) in abstract colour drawings at 40 to 43% and in line drawings at about 32% (chance 17%). The authors note that telling *negative* emotions apart is harder with contours than with colours. Takahashi (1995): line drawings made to express emotion concepts were ranked by a larger group with "considerable agreement". Stamatopoulou & Cupchik (2017): 12 non-representational drawings made by artists to communicate specific emotions, rated by 152 Canadians, 48 Greeks and 68 Japanese, showed "a systematic sharing of affective meaning across artists, spectators and cultures".
- **Less in museums' modern art.** In WikiArt Emotions (Mohammad & Kiritchenko 2018), agreement was lower for modern and contemporary art, which is more often abstract, than for older figurative art, and lowest for complex emotions (optimism, shame, guilt, regret).
- **Machines can predict it.** A classifier trained on low-level features (colour, texture, edges) predicted human emotion judgements of abstract paintings (Yanulevskaya et al. 2012, as cited by van Paasschen et al. 2015), consistent with the feelings living in measurable features.

**[H] What it means for a painter agent.**
1. **In abstraction, aim for the dimensions first**: pleasant vs unpleasant, calm vs aroused. These are what viewers share. A specific emotion is reachable for basic feelings (joy, anger, fear, sadness, wonder); complex feelings (shame, guilt, nostalgia, jealousy) are not reliably read from abstract form alone.
2. **Complexity and darkness = negative arousal; clarity, brightness and simple geometry = positive calm.** Edge density is a direct arousal control.
3. **Colour before line.** In abstract work colour carries more of the specific emotion than contour does; line mainly carries arousal and pleasantness.
4. **For a complex feeling in an abstract commission, build it from two readable halves** (one region or movement for each basic component) and give each its own form, the lesson of round 11. Expect the critic to name the dominant half.
5. **A title is part of the painting.** Titles changed the reported emotion in many WikiArt works. Teodor's titles should open the right door without naming the feeling.

## 9. Value and contrast, texture and surface, scale

**[S] Findings.**
- **Light is good, high is good.** Meier, Robinson & Clore (2004; five studies): people were slower to categorise words as positive or negative when the brightness of the letters mismatched the meaning (a negative word in light letters); the authors conclude that people automatically assume bright things are good and dark things bad. Meier & Robinson (2004; three studies): positive evaluations were faster for words high on the screen and negative ones for words low; positive evaluations drew attention upward. The brightness-valence and up-valence links are among the best-replicated "metaphor" effects in emotion research, and they agree with the colour literature (section 2).
- **Complexity is arousal.** Marin & Leder (2013; 152 participants, environmental scenes, representational paintings and music): subjective complexity rose with arousal across domains. Van Paasschen et al. (2014): many edges plus dark colours → aroused and negative (section 8). Liking against complexity is often an inverted U (Berlyne's classic proposal), but Güçlütürk, Jacobs & van Lier (2016) showed the average curve hides groups of people with different preferences.
- **Paintings share the statistics of natural scenes.** Graham & Redies (2010, review): artworks across cultures tend to have spatial-frequency (roughly 1/f, scale-invariant) and luminance statistics like natural scenes, which the visual system is tuned to process. Taylor et al. (2011, a review of ten years of their own studies): Pollock's poured paintings are fractal, and the research group reports positive perceptual and physiological responses to fractal patterns of mid-range complexity. Evidence: consistent correlational findings, mostly from two research groups.
- **Context and reality add value.** Brieber, Nadal & Leder (2015): the same exhibition was found more arousing, more positive, more interesting and better liked in the museum than as a computer simulation in the lab, and was remembered better. Carbon (2020): for large paintings by Pollock and Rothko, the preferred viewing distances, and the distances where the most intense experiences (including aesthetic "aha" moments) happened, were much larger than in typical lab studies.
- **Scale and awe.** Keltner & Haidt (2003) place perceived vastness at the core of awe. Bai et al. (2017; six studies, N = 2,137, several cultures): awe, but not other positive emotions, made people feel smaller (measured partly by how large they drew themselves), across collectivist and individualist cultures. Joye & Dewitte (2016): monumental buildings evoked awe and a sense of "freezing" (slowed perceived and actual movement). Piff et al. (2015) tie awe's "small self" to more generous behaviour.

**[H] What it means for a painter agent.**
1. **The key of the picture is its first emotional statement.** High-key (mostly light values) = positive; low-key (mostly dark) = negative or grave; a light region *high* in the picture = hope, release; a dark mass *above* = oppression. Teodor's critics often read his dark-room paintings as dread or melancholy for this reason.
2. **Contrast and edge density control arousal.** Few edges, soft transitions and close values for calm, grief, tenderness; many edges, hard transitions and wide value jumps for anger, fear, ecstasy.
3. **Texture should be natural, not uniform.** Paintings resemble natural scenes in their statistics; a surface of identical dabs at one scale breaks that (the "digital filter" complaint). Vary mark size across scales, from large masses to small accents, and let texture density follow the feeling: dense and broken for agitation, smooth and fused for calm.
4. **Teodor's work is seen on screens**, so physical scale is lost; *depicted* scale remains. For awe, make the viewer and any figure small against something vast (sky, architecture, crowd, light) and keep it partly beyond the frame. Shrinking the figure is itself the "small self" of awe.

## The cue hierarchy, in one paragraph

Spectre's rule is "mode and tempo are the strongest cues, then register, dynamics, articulation, timbre". The nearest equivalent for Teodor, as far as the evidence goes:

**If there is a figure, its posture is the strongest cue** (and the face only with it), because figures raise viewer agreement and the body decides the reading at high intensity (sections 6, 8). **Then the overall value key and colour saturation**: light/dark sets pleasant/unpleasant, saturation sets energy (section 2). **Then hue**, read as a shared but many-to-many code (section 2). **Then line and gesture**: angular vs curved, dense vs sparse, fast vs slow marks, which mostly add arousal and threat (sections 3, 5). **Then composition**: where the figure sits and faces, high or low, inward or outward, how big against its world (sections 4, 9). **Last, the clue that needs a second look** (an object, a detail, the title), which only some viewers reach but which turns a correct reading into a moving one (sections 1, 7). Abstract paintings lose the first cue and must carry everything through the next three; they can reliably carry pleasantness, energy and the basic emotions, and rarely complex ones (section 8).

This ordering is an inference from separate studies, not a measured ranking like Eerola et al. (2013) for music. Nobody has varied all these cues in one painting experiment.

## 10. Nearest wrong feelings

Which feelings viewers confuse in pictures, why, and what separates them. Each pair names the research reason and, where there is one, the Teodor round where it happened.

|---|---|---|---|

| **Joy / happiness** | Surprise, wonder | Static postures of happiness and surprise are confused [Coulson 2004] | Face and context; wonder uses more colours and dark blue [Damiano 2023] |
| **Ecstasy** | Agony, panic | At peak intensity faces are ambiguous [Aviezer 2012] | The body: open, reaching, light vs. contracted, guarding; curves vs angles |
| **Ecstasy / high** | A party, cheerful vertigo, a fun dream | Brightness and saturation say "positive" loudly; the art frame mutes the edge [Gerger 2014] | The cost must have its own form: something cold, dissolving or out of control (round 14b's shadow came closest) [Teodor round 14] |
| **Bittersweet joy** | Plain elation | The positive half is brighter and louder; viewers name the dominant half [H; Teodor round 11] | Give the bitter half its own form and real area: a counter-movement, a cooling that takes space from the light, an interval of rest [Teodor round 11; Menninghaus 2017 on mixed emotions] |
| **Shame** | Grief, sadness | Head down and slumped in both [Tracy & Matsumoto 2008; Coulson 2004] | Shame turns *away* from someone and makes the body small; it needs a witness or a visible cause the figure is responsible for. Grief turns *toward* the lost thing. Being moved goes with appraising the event as *not* caused by oneself [Menninghaus 2015]; shame is the opposite appraisal, so the picture must show the figure as the cause [H] |
| **Rage → shame (remorse)** | Held-in anger, dread | The anger posture is louder and arrives first [Teodor round 9] | The turn must be visible in the body: fist still clenched but head dropping, gaze on the damage [Teodor round 9; Tracy & Matsumoto 2008] |
| **Jealousy** | Longing, loneliness | An outsider looking at a lit window is the longing picture [Teodor round 10] | Jealousy needs the rival pair, a corrosive gesture turned inward and something spoiled or sour in the paint [H; Teodor round 10] |
| **Pride** | Anger, defiance | Both expand the chest with the head back [Tracy & Robins 2004; Coulson 2004] | Pride: small smile, hands on hips or arms up and open, light; anger: arms forward, fists, weight forward, red-black [Tracy & Robins 2004; Coulson 2004] |
| **Pride mixed with loss** | Nostalgia, hope after grief | Clue-based pride is not seen at the gist stage [Leder 2004; Teodor round 4] | Pride must live in posture and light, not in props [Teodor round 4] |
| **Relief mixed with guilt** | Grief, hope after grief | Mourning postures read as grief; warm-next-to-dark reads as hope [Teodor round 6] | Make the causal link visible: one fire, one wind, a gesture aimed at the other's loss, not inward [Teodor round 6] |

| **Awe** | Fear, dread | Awe has a threat-tinged variety; vastness plus darkness becomes menace [Keltner & Haidt 2003] | Light and openness above, a stable vantage for the viewer, curves; threat-free vastness [Keltner & Haidt 2003; Meier & Robinson 2004] |
| **Satirical mockery** | Solemnity, mild amusement | A figure seen from behind keeps the joke at arm's length [Teodor round 12] | A readable face and gesture close up; exaggeration in the body [Teodor round 12; Mohammad & Kiritchenko 2018 on faces] |
| **Terror with tenderness** | Comedy ("a group selfie") | Wide-angle ring of similar faces reads as a known photo genre [Teodor round 13] | Fewer, more specific witnesses with distinct faces and hands [Teodor round 13] |
| **Love / tenderness** | Pity, sadness | Soft, low-arousal, curved: the same line family as sadness [Lundholm 1921] | Warmth, pink and light faces; bodies leaning in, contact, mutual gaze [Jonauskaite & Mohr 2025; Zickfeld 2019; Teodor round 15] |
| **Longing** | Melancholy | Both low-arousal, both warm-against-dark in Teodor's work | Longing keeps a goal: a direction, a lit object of desire in reach of the eye but not the hand [H] |

**General lessons from these confusions.**
1. **Viewers name the loudest component.** In every mixed-feeling round the critic named the half carried by posture, value or colour, and missed the half carried by a clue. Complex feelings also have the lowest agreement in the wider research [Mohammad & Kiritchenko 2018]. Give each half of a mixed feeling its own *perceptual* form.
2. **Low arousal is where feelings blur.** Sadness, serenity, longing, nostalgia, tenderness and resignation share the same line family. They are separated by value key, saturation, warmth, posture and the presence or absence of a goal or a second person.
3. **High arousal is split by angle and colour.** Joy, ecstasy, anger and panic share energy; acute angles, red-black and contracted bodies make it negative, curves, yellow-pink and open bodies positive.
4. **Relationship feelings need the relationship in the picture.** Guilt, jealousy, pride in someone, being outgrown: the viewer must see both parties and the causal link between them.
5. **Check the reading the way the research does:** a fresh critic who sees only the canvas, naming the feeling at first glance (the gist) and again after a minute (the mastering stage). The intended feeling should appear in the second reading at least.

## How much to trust this

- **Strongest:** colour lightness and saturation as carriers of valence and arousal (tens of thousands of participants, many countries, a systematic review); posture as a carrier of basic emotions, pride and shame (many studies, cross-cultural, including blind athletes); faces and bodies raising agreement in art (a 4,000-painting dataset); viewers' shared reading of valence and arousal in abstract art (several labs); curvature preference (a meta-analysis, medium effect, not universal).
- **Moderate:** angularity as threat; line shape tracking imagined movement; specific colour-emotion pairs for basic emotions in drawings (one strong recent study plus older small ones); the role of insight and ambiguity in liking; being moved as a mixed, relational emotion.
- **Weak or indirect:** embodied simulation of brushstrokes (small EEG samples; tiny behavioural effects; some contrary evidence); composition and balance as carriers of *emotion* (good evidence on preference, little on feeling); tension and its resolution (art theory); every cue for longing, jealousy, nostalgia, dread and awe beyond the core appraisals (no direct painting studies found).
- **Who was studied:** mostly Western university students in labs, looking at reproductions on screens for seconds. Museum studies show longer viewing and stronger effects of size and context. Teodor's audience sees his paintings on screens too, which makes the lab results unusually relevant here.
- **What was not found:** no study varies all the cues in one set of paintings and ranks them, as Eerola, Friberg & Bresin (2013) did for music. The hierarchy above is assembled from separate studies.
