# Process research: what Teodor can learn from Spectre and from how painters work

Written 2026-11-25. It compares Teodor with its sibling experiment Spectre (github.com/robertontiu/spectre, the same idea applied to music, whose output is now clearly stronger), then lists what human painters do that Teodor's process still lacks, and ends with ranked proposals. It adds to `docs/research.md`, which already covers Gombrich, Miall & Tchalenko, Tchalenko & Miall, Drake/Riccio/Chamberlain/Kozbelt, Park/Williams/Chamberlain, Getzels & Csikszentmihalyi, Mace & Ward, Freedberg & Gallese and Hertzmann. Those are not repeated here.

Labels, as in Spectre's docs: **[S]** a named source (paper, museum study, studio text), **[T]** an established studio-practice text or painter's own account (practitioner knowledge, not an experiment), **[M]** measured in this repo or Spectre's, **[H]** a judgement of this document. Where a source's content is given from its abstract or from a review of it rather than a re-reading, that is said.

Teodor files read: `AGENTS.md`, `docs/orchestration.md`, `docs/prompts/painter-brief.md`, `docs/research.md`, `docs/habits.md`, `docs/handover.md`, `docs/experiments.md` (rounds 11-15), `docs/styles/README.md`, `studio/v7/studio.py`, round 15's visitor reading. Spectre files read: `README.md`, `AGENTS.md`, `docs/ORCHESTRATION.md`, `prompts/producer.md`, `docs/LESSONS.md`, `docs/PRODUCTION.md`, `docs/MASTERING.md` (skimmed), `docs/HANDOFF.md`, `engine/ear.py`, `engine/clarity.py` (skimmed).

## Part 1. What Spectre does that Teodor doesn't (or does worse), and how it maps to painting

The short version: both projects share the loop (feel, act, look, journal; mind/hand split; short episodes; fresh agents per phase). The difference is everything *around* the loop. Spectre does research before every new genre and turns it into sourced data the engine reads; it measures its own output against targets taken from real records of that genre and era; it can see each instrument separately and knows when one is buried; it plans one deliberate surprise inside a predictable frame; and it treats the owner's ear as part of the instrument. Teodor has one generic brief, one set of views that are only pictures (no numbers), no target from real paintings, and a critic that is another model guessing.

### 1.1 Research-first genre setup
**Spectre.** Before the first track in a new genre, the orchestrator researches it (length, form, tempo, instruments, mastering practice, modern reference artists, cultural context), writes `docs/<GENRE>.md`, adds an entry to `engine/genres.json`, and builds the genre's missing instruments before any session (ORCHESTRATION.md "Starting a new session", step 0-1; standing rule 7: "Genre settings come from research, not guesses"). The producer never researches the genre from scratch inside a session.
**Teodor.** The painter does a few minutes of research inside phase 1 ("RESEARCH BEFORE YOU SKETCH"), with whatever it remembers or finds. `docs/styles/README.md` already designs a style system (current, medium, support, technique, finishing; every value sourced), but `studio/styles.json` and the per-style docs don't exist yet and no round has used one.
**For painting.** A *current* (Expressionism, Tonalism, Ukiyo-e, the Venetian glaze method, Fauvism...) is the genre. Its research should happen before the commission, by the orchestrator, and be stored once: palette principle, value key, edge treatment, mark vocabulary, process order, what "finished" meant, typical compositions, and its nearest wrong look. The painter then reads a researched doc instead of improvising one. The template in `docs/styles/README.md` is already the right shape; what's missing is the rule that it is filled *before* painting, and the engine reading it.

### 1.2 Genre configs as sourced data
**Spectre.** `engine/genres.json`, `engine/eqmaps.json` and `engine/targets.json` hold every per-genre number with provenance; the master and the clarity analysis read them (`--genre <name>`, no default). Unsourced values are marked hand-tuned and queued for research.
**Teodor.** Every number lives in prose in the brief ("3-5 value groups", "darks thin, lights thick"), the same for every painting, and none is checked by the engine.
**For painting.** A style entry in `studio/styles.json` that the studio *reads*: value key and range (e.g. a Tonalist high-key range against a Caravaggesque low key with 70% of the canvas in the darkest group), the number of value groups, the palette gamut (a polygon in a colour space), edge ratio (how much hard vs lost edge), mark-size distribution, and the image statistics of reference paintings (1.4 below). Then the views can report "your value histogram has 4% darks; this current's references have 40-60%".

### 1.3 Instruments added per genre
**Spectre.** Generic synths "can't fake" a culture's instruments, so each folk genre got its own (oud, darbuka, cimbalom, kokle, trembita, violin waveguide), checked by spectrogram against recordings before use (LESSONS "Folk-fusion genres need three things").
**Teodor.** One oil engine with five brushes (flat, round, fan, knife, liner), one mixing model and a fixed canvas. `docs/styles/README.md` names media (watercolour, ink, charcoal, gouache, tempera) that do not exist in the studio.
**For painting.** A medium is an instrument. Watercolour (transparent, works light to dark, granulation, blooms, hard edges where a wash dries), ink (one value, line weight), charcoal (erasure as a mark, smudge), gouache (matte, flat, value shift on drying), tempera (hatched small strokes, no blending), and within oil a filbert, a rigger, a palette knife scrape and a rag wipe-out. Each needs checking against real examples before it's used, the way Spectre checks a new instrument's spectrogram against a recording: render a test swatch sheet and compare its texture statistics with photographs of real swatches.

### 1.4 A mastering stage per genre (a measured target)
**Spectre.** `master.py` moves the track toward a genre's target spectrum, measured from catalogue data of a named era (`targets.json`, with the number of tracks, years and method), reports every deviation, and refuses to fix too much: "When the master has to correct too much, Spectre goes back and fixes the mix". The big lesson (PRODUCTION.md, point 3): Spectre had been carving sounds "to fit a wrong ruler"; checking where the target came from mattered more than any mix move.
**Teodor.** There is no target. "Finished" is the painter's judgement plus a generic "PROFESSIONAL FINISH" paragraph. There is no final pass that unifies the whole and no measurement against real paintings.
**For painting.** Two parts. (a) **A measured target per current**: global image statistics computed from a set of reference paintings in that current (public-domain museum images): value histogram and key, the share of the canvas in each value group, saturation distribution, colour-temperature balance, edge density and the share of hard vs soft edges, the Fourier spectral slope (paintings, like natural scenes, cluster around a slope of about -2 in power; Graham & Field 2007; Redies, Hasenstein & Denzler 2007), and edge-orientation entropy. Report the painting's deviation from the target band per measure, like the master's report. Nothing is "corrected" automatically: a large deviation sends the painter back to paint (Spectre's rule "fix the mix"). (b) **A finishing stage per current**: what painters of that current actually did last (a unifying glaze, scumbled atmosphere, final accents, varnish that saturates the darks, or deliberately leaving it raw), written into the style doc and done as the last phase with its own checks. The studio's `glaze` is the tool; what's missing is the stage and the measurement.

### 1.5 Nearest wrong feelings
**Spectre.** Every brief names the nearest wrong feelings and what separates them from the target (rage vs hype, rebellion vs triumph, anti-war vs battle anthem; "Each genre has its own trap: name the trap in the brief"). The loop asks every iteration "whether it drifts toward a nearby wrong feeling". Session 015's brief named "a lullaby, a toy commercial, a sad piano elegy, generic stock inspirational music".
**Teodor.** The brief asks "would a passerby feel it?" but never names what it would be mistaken for. Yet almost every critic reading is exactly a nearest wrong feeling: round 11's bittersweet joy read as plain exhilaration; round 12's biting satire as "more pathetic than biting"; round 13's terror slid to "a fisheye group selfie"; round 14's drug high read as "cheerful vertigo", "a fun dream", "a candy wrapper"; round 15's eternal love as "sweet but not moving", "a hospital ward". These were predictable.
**For painting.** The orchestrator names two to four nearest wrong readings per commission in the brief (for "High": a party, a fun-house, a candy ad, a psychedelic poster), and the painter writes in `feeling.md` what visibly separates the target from each (a value structure, a colour, a gesture, a crop). Critic readings are then scored against both lists: which feeling did they name, the target or one of the wrong ones? Add a "nearest wrong *look*" too (Spectre's PRODUCTION.md checklist item 7, "the nearest wrong sounds"): a digital filter, a 3D render, an illustration, a greeting card, a student study.

### 1.6 The expectation ledger (correct vs compelling)
**Spectre.** PRODUCTION.md section 4: "the difference between correct and compelling is, as far as research can say, a difference of planned expectation". Build certainty, then spend one surprise (Cheung et al. 2019: pleasure peaks where a strong deviation lands in a context of low uncertainty); prefer documented triggers; "don't confuse surprise with randomness"; "imperfection by structure, not by jitter"; "one ugly thing, taught early"; verify after rendering that the surprise is actually audible. The producer keeps an EXPECTATION LEDGER in the journal (producer.md stage 3).
**Teodor.** The brief asks for "a focal area with the sharpest edges, the strongest contrast and the finest detail" (correct), but nothing about what the picture sets up and then breaks. Critics keep finding the paintings even and predictable: the same mark everywhere ("a filter"), six heads of equal weight (round 13), "every edge is equally soft" (round 15).
**For painting.** A painting sets up expectations too: a rhythm of repeated shapes, a dominant direction, a palette key, a value key, a scale of mark. The ledger, in the handoff: what the painting establishes (the repeated vertical, the cool family, the soft-edge world, the even mark) and the one or two places where it breaks it on purpose (one hard edge in a soft world, one warm accent in a cool key, one diagonal against the verticals, one figure that faces the other way, one mark ten times bigger). Uniformity everywhere else is what makes the break visible: Arnheim's and the ateliers' "dominance and contrast" (Dow 1899 calls it subordination). Then check that the break is measurable on the canvas (the edge map or the saliency view shows it as the strongest point), Spectre's "verify the break is audible".

### 1.7 "The ears are the bottleneck", and the clarity and stack views
**Spectre.** AGENTS.md: "Quality is capped by how well the agent can judge what it hears. Improving the ears is usually the highest-value engine work." The ears give pictures *and numbers*: arrangement lanes, a mel spectrogram, band energies, a loudness curve, per-section stats, warnings ("sounds almost identical to the previous section", "sub is very dominant"), a timing roll, squint checks (`--mono`, `--lowpass`, `--phone`: "like a producer switching between headphones, monitors and a laptop"). `listen --clarity` re-renders the stems and reports per region the share of energy, the stems within 10 dB of the loudest (layer census), occupancy, the share of each stem that is masked (over about 90% means "not in the mix"), low-mid build-up against the genre target, crest per region, and EQ-map deviations, with flags. The stack view shows what each layer of one sound contributes, so "a layer that supplies nowhere more than a few percent comes out or gets a job".
**Teodor.** `look` offers seven pictures (`full`, `thumb`, `gray` posterized to 8 levels, `crop`, `ref`, `diff`, `compare`) and **no numbers and no warnings at all**. The painter must judge everything by eye from a 600×800 image. Missing: a mirror, a value-group map, an edge map, a temperature map, a saliency or focal-point check, a per-element view, any statistic.
**For painting.** The painter's equivalents of Spectre's ears are the classic painter's checks (Part 2.2): step back (thumb), squint (blur), the mirror, upside down, grayscale, and the notan. Each becomes a view, and each view also prints a few numbers and flags, like `listen`:
- *value view*: the histogram, the share of canvas in each value group, the number of distinct groups, a flag when the middle third of the value range holds more than, say, 50% of the canvas ("murky mid-value": round 6's and 15's critiques), or when the darkest and lightest values are far from the style's key;
- *edge view*: an edge-strength map with hard, firm, soft and lost classes; the share of hard edges inside the planned focal region against outside it; a flag when edge sharpness is uniform ("every edge equally soft": round 15);
- *clarity view* (the masking analogue): per *element* of the blockout (each figure, the hand, the face, the sky, the ground), its value and colour contrast against its surround, and the share of its silhouette that is "lost" (the element dissolves into its neighbours at squint distance). An element whose contrast with its neighbours is below a threshold at thumbnail size is the painter's "stem over 90% masked": it is not in the picture. The blockout gives the element masks for free, as the engine gives Spectre its stems;
- *saliency view*: where the eye goes first (a contrast-and-edge saliency model; Itti & Koch-style), compared with the focal point the painter declared. Round 13's "six heads of equal weight" is a saliency map with six equal peaks;
- *stack view* (the layer analogue): what each layer or pass contributed (the block-in, each match, the glazes), measured by how much it changed the canvas per region, so a pass that changed nothing visible is seen as wasted and a glaze that flattened contrast is seen.
The engine already keeps the scene, the schema and every frame, so all of this is cheap to compute.

### 1.8 Mind/hand split
**Spectre.** The producer writes musical intentions in a compact notation ("a snare that drags, ghost notes, an 808 sliding up"); the hand performs swing, micro-timing, velocity phrasing, voicing. "Don't make the agent place every sample, and don't let the engine make musical decisions." And its hand improved by *measurement*: humanizing moved from independent jitter to correlated drift because listeners prefer long-range-correlated deviations (Hennig et al. 2011), and Senn et al. (2016) showed exaggerated looseness is rated worse.
**Teodor.** Has the split (intentions vs `play`/`gesture_path`), but the hand is weak where it matters: the handover says "square dabs everywhere and uniform edges"; critics call it "a filter", "a brush preset", "round-dab stamping". And the mind leaks into the hand: painters wrote their own stroke helpers, face generators and figure rigs, and long hand-placed stroke lists (rounds 13-15).
**For painting.** Two lessons from Spectre. (a) Give the hand richer *intentions* to perform, as Spectre's notation names articulations: a stroke that starts loaded and drags dry, a stroke that turns at the form's edge, a broken-colour scumble, a lost-and-found contour, a calligraphic stroke that swells and tapers. (b) Correlate the hand's variation over a passage instead of jittering each stroke independently: pressure, angle and load drift slowly across a sequence of strokes, as a real painter's do (the visual analogue of Hennig's finding). That attacks the "filter" look at its cause. Keep painters from writing helpers that generate the picture; give them the vocabulary instead.

### 1.9 The standing rules
**Spectre.** ORCHESTRATION.md opens with 17 numbered standing rules from the owner (research before settings, master like the industry per genre, clean up, every production unique, voices are instruments, report misses honestly...), each dated and attributed, so no agreement is lost between sessions.
**Teodor.** The equivalent decisions are scattered: "Conventions and rules" in AGENTS.md, preferences in handover.md, "It must be a painting" in two places, the no-likeness rule in handover.md only.
**For painting.** Gather Robert's decisions into one numbered list in `docs/orchestration.md` (the name Teodor, always signed, feelings published, plain public text, MIT/CC BY, it must be a painting, no likeness of real people, shocking yes / gore no, break habits, Opus as the default painter, research before style), each with its date, and add the rules this document argues for once Robert agrees (style research before the first painting in a current, every per-style value sourced).

### 1.10 Lessons across genres
**Spectre.** `docs/LESSONS.md` has a per-session entry *and* an "Across genres" section, rewritten after every genre switch and framed as principles ("subtraction", "two time feels as meaning", "a decay can be the form", "one ugly thing taught early"), not structures to copy. The producer's brief carries the three most important lessons.
**Teodor.** `docs/experiments.md` has good per-round "Learned" lines and `docs/habits.md` lists what to avoid, but nothing collects what *worked* as transferable principles. The brief's "LESSONS FROM EARLIER PAINTINGS" are three rules from early rounds.
**For painting.** A "What transfers" section in `docs/experiments.md` (or a `docs/lessons.md`), positive principles drawn from rounds 0-15: a drawn tonal study before faces and hands (round 15); the idea carries a satire (round 12's shadow bigger than the man); the viewpoint can be the departure (round 13's worm's-eye); the "bitter" half needs its own form, not darker edges (round 11); direction decides meaning (round 7 or 8's doorway). Habits say what not to repeat; lessons say what to reuse as a principle.

### 1.11 Handoff
**Spectre.** `docs/HANDOFF.md`: a map of the whole project for a fresh session (how a session runs end to end, the orchestrator's habits, the owner's preferences, the state of every session in a table with open issues, decisions waiting on the owner, an engine and knowledge map, known engine gaps, *tried and rejected*, the queue).
**Teodor.** `docs/handover.md` is shorter and good, but lacks the per-round state table with open issues, the known engine gaps list (partly in "Studio gaps painters worked around"), and a "tried and rejected" list (v5's fixed levels, Fable's compositing, the `transfer` colour, pure single-constant KM).
**For painting.** Same structure; small work. The value is that a fresh orchestrator stops re-trying rejected ideas.

### 1.12 Unique productions; quotes as ingredients
**Spectre.** Standing rule 13: "Every production is unique; quotes are ingredients." A licensed quote (an anthem phrase, a carol line) is allowed only inside Spectre's own arrangement, harmony and form; session 012's first plan "arranged a whole carol and sounded like a rehearsal", and the fix was to let the quote "appear only where the story turns" (LESSONS, "A quote can be the hinge instead of the material").
**Teodor.** The brief tells Teodor to take "a composition device, a palette, a way of treating the figure" from research, but has no rule about how much may be borrowed, and no rule about quoting a specific public-domain painting.
**For painting.** Painters quote constantly (Manet's *Olympia* from Titian's *Venus of Urbino*; Picasso's 58 *Meninas* variations; Bacon after Velázquez's *Innocent X*). Allow a quote of a public-domain work's *device* (a pose, a gesture, a crop, a light), never its composition as a whole, and use it as a hinge: the one place where the painting turns. And state the rule plainly: never in the manner of a living artist (already in `docs/styles/README.md`).

### 1.13 The owner as the ear test
**Spectre.** PRODUCTION.md: "the owner's ear, and now a professional's, are part of the instrument, not a review at the end". A sound audition before the arrangement (two or three candidate stacks as 8-bar loops; the owner picks), one mid-session checkpoint, verdicts recorded as symptoms with a timestamp ("0:55 thin"), never as prescriptions; "every verdict logged next to the measurements of that version ... the only way the ears can be calibrated". Standing rule 10: Robert is the ear tester; research decides the fix.
**Teodor.** The only human sees the finished painting (and sometimes checkpoints in the chat, as information). All in-process judgement comes from model critics, which share the painter's blind spots and drift toward the commission's title. Critic readings are not logged next to any measurement.
**For painting.** (a) A **thumbnail audition** at the end of conception: two or three value thumbnails (or blockouts) shown to Robert, who picks the one he feels fastest; a few seconds of his time at the point where the most is decided. (b) His verdict on checkpoints as symptoms ("the middle is mud", "her hand reads as a glove"), which the painter diagnoses. (c) A log: each critic's and Robert's reading next to the views' numbers for that canvas, so over rounds it becomes visible which numbers predict "reads as a filter" or "murky middle" and which don't. That is how Teodor's eyes get calibrated.

### 1.14 Research beats suggestions
**Spectre.** Standing rules 10 and 11: the owner's notes are "symptoms to diagnose ('it sounds like reverb' is a hypothesis to test)"; brief numbers that contradict the genre research are flagged and the research decides. Session 010's tempo 50 became a slow-dance door rather than the dance tempo.
**Teodor.** Critics' suggestions are often followed literally or pushed back on by hand (the studio manager's note refused "dim the sky to violet" in round 13 because it was the old habit). There is no rule for how to weigh a suggestion against research or the style.
**For painting.** Treat critic advice the same way: the reading (what they felt, what they saw) is data, the remedy is a hypothesis. The painter diagnoses the cause with the views ("the middle reads muddy": is it value, saturation, edges or mark?) and applies the fix that the style research and the measurement support. Robert's taste decides what he likes; research decides how to get there.

### 1.15 Other things Spectre does that matter
- **Break your own habits, by reading your earlier work's structure** (producer.md stage 1). Teodor already copied this (habits.md plus the contact sheet) and it works for palette, light and mood. Spectre names *structural* patterns (section order, where the drop-outs fall); Teodor's equivalent, staging (one central figure, a radial pull, eye level), is exactly what survived round 14. The contact sheet should be accompanied by *structure* thumbnails of past work: value notans and the main directions, where habits are easier to see than in colour.
- **The sound stage before the loop** (producer.md stage 2): build the two or three sounds the track stands on, render each in variants, choose, *then* compose. Teodor's equivalent is a **material and mark study** before the block-in: a swatch sheet of the two or three marks and the palette the painting will stand on, chosen against the feeling. Today the marks are whatever `match` produces.
- **Forward only, versioned and reproducible.** Every Spectre render can be rebuilt from its `project.json`; Teodor's frames are kept but a painting can't be replayed from its log. Not a quality issue, but it makes A/B tests of engine changes possible (Spectre's `examples/production/` A/Bs).
- **Subtraction as the strongest move** (LESSONS "What transfers everywhere"). In painting: the scrape-out, wiping back, painting out a passage, losing an edge, *removing* an object (Vermeer's *Milkmaid*, Part 2.5). Teodor almost never removes; its painters add.
- **Clean-up, honest reporting of misses.** Teodor already reports misses honestly in experiments.md.

## Part 2. What human painters do that Teodor's process still lacks

Research note: the web fetch tool hit its limit partway through this research, so several sources below are given from their abstracts, from reviews that summarise them (named), or from the standard studio texts as widely known; those are marked "(as reviewed in ...)" or [T]. Sources read in full or in part for this document: Chamberlain & Wagemans (2016), Chamberlain's chapter on expertise in the visual arts, Ostrofsky et al. (2016), Drake, Riccio, Chamberlain & Kozbelt, the Rijksmuseum's 2022 report on *The Milkmaid*, and the DiPaola, Riebe & Enns (2010) study (via its press summary).

### 2.1 What expert draughtsmen and painters actually do better
- **Errors come mainly from how the subject is seen and categorised, not from the hand.** Following Cohen & Bennett (1997), Chamberlain & Wagemans (2016) sort drawing errors into misperception of the object, misperception of the drawing, representational decisions, motor skill and visual memory, and conclude that "fine motor skills are unlikely to play a key role"; what separates artists is flexible control of attention (switching between local and global), better *representational decisions* (segmenting the scene and choosing the features that matter) and better visual memory while drawing. Artists are not, on the whole, immune to perceptual constancies; the evidence there is "highly inconsistent" (Chamberlain's review). [S]
- **Categorical schemas distort; pictorial schemas help.** The same review separates "negative categorical schemas", which pull a drawing toward the label or canonical form of a thing (an eye drawn as the symbol of an eye), from positive pictorial schemas that help segment the scene. Teodor's recurring "disc faces", "mannequin people" and "hands like tubes" are textbook categorical-schema errors: the painter draws the category *face* and *hand*, not observed shapes of light and dark. Round 15 found the cure by itself: a drawn tonal study first, then matched with small brushes. [S]; the mapping to Teodor [H]
- **Look little, look often.** The best-supported technique in the review: increasing the frequency of gaze shifts between subject and drawing improved accuracy (Cohen 2005, as reviewed in Chamberlain & Wagemans 2016). Teodor's one-look-per-episode rhythm already follows this (Miall & Tchalenko in `docs/research.md`). [S]
- **Artists look at structure, relations and the background.** Artists fixate more on background and on relations between objects than laypeople (Vogt & Magnussen 2007), make more global scanning movements, and are less driven by locally salient spots (as reviewed in Chamberlain's chapter). Trained viewers of compositions look more at the relations between elements, untrained viewers at the objects (Nodine, Locher & Krupinski 1993). Artists also include the features that carry object recognition, such as junctions and occlusion boundaries (Kozbelt et al. 2010; Ostrofsky et al. 2012). [S]
- **Negative space.** Shapes of negative space are drawn more accurately than identifiable positive shapes (Carson et al. 2014, as reviewed in Chamberlain & Wagemans 2016), which is the rationale of the atelier advice to draw the spaces between things. [S]
- **What Teodor lacks:** a *measuring* step for the figure and the main shapes (proportion, angles, alignments, negative shapes) checked against the blockout, and a habit of evaluating *relations* (this value against that one, this edge against that one) rather than objects.

### 2.2 The painter's checks
Painters have long used a small set of tricks to see their own canvas freshly, because the eye adapts to a picture it has looked at for hours and starts seeing what it intended rather than what is there.
- **Stepping back.** Leonardo advises going some distance away, because the work then appears smaller, more of it is taken in at a glance, and a lack of harmony or proportion is more readily seen; and to rest and come back, since judgement weakens with continuous work (*Notebooks*, Richter ed., in the paragraphs on the painter's judgement, §§529-530 in that numbering). Teodor's `thumb` is this. [T]
- **The mirror.** In the same passages Leonardo recommends a flat mirror: the picture seen reversed looks like another painter's work, so its faults are easier to judge. A reversed image defeats the adaptation to one's own picture; asymmetries, a lean, a heavy side and a weak drawing jump out. No experiment testing the mirror's effect on painting quality was found; it is a near-universal studio practice. Teodor has no mirror view. [T]
- **Squinting.** Narrowing the eyes removes detail and most colour and leaves the big masses of light and dark: Harold Speed's "mass drawing" (1917) teaches seeing in masses before lines, and atelier teaching uses the squint to compare values and find edges that disappear (those are the lost edges). The physiology behind it: the coarse, low-spatial-frequency content of an image carries the layout and the "gist" (Livingstone 2002 discusses why low-resolution vision reads the masses and the expression). Chamberlain & Wagemans note squinting as suppressing local detail but found no direct test. Teodor's `thumb` blurs, but nothing simulates the squint's merging of nearby values into groups. [T], [S]
- **Upside down.** Turning the canvas or the model upside down (Edwards 1979) is supposed to stop the painter from seeing objects. Experiments don't support it for *accuracy*: Cohen & Earls (2010) found no improvement, and spatial positioning of features was judged worse in upside-down drawings; Ostrofsky et al. (2016) found that inversion made long-range relationships (the eye-to-mouth distance) less accurate and short-range ones no better, "inconsistent with the assertion that drawing from upside-down models improves drawing accuracy". As a *check of the abstract design* (balance, the pattern of darks, directions) of an already painted canvas it is still widely used; that use is untested. [S], [T]
- **Grayscale.** Value carries form, depth and legibility; colour carries much less of it. Livingstone (2002) shows that the brain's "where" pathway is colour-blind, so depth, shape-from-shading and motion come from luminance, and that colours of equal luminance (Monet's sun in *Impression, Sunrise* is about the same luminance as its sky) lose their solidity and seem to shimmer. A painter checks a grayscale version to see whether the picture still reads. Teodor has `gray`, posterized to 8 levels, as a picture only. [S]
- **Black mirror and reducing glass.** The Claude glass (a tinted convex mirror used by 18th-century landscape painters) compressed the value range and simplified the scene; the reducing glass (a concave lens) shows the canvas small. Both are stepping-back tools. [T]
- **What Teodor lacks:** a mirror, a squint that merges values into groups, upside-down, and above all *numbers* from these views (Part 1.7), plus a rule for when to use which: at the end of every stage, and every N episodes during development.

### 2.3 Value studies, thumbnails and notan
- **Thumbnails, many and quick.** Studio texts make the small value thumbnail the main planning tool: Edgar Payne (*Composition of Outdoor Painting*, 1941) catalogues basic compositional structures (steelyard, balance scale, S or compound curve, triangle, circle, radiating lines, tunnel, cross, pattern) and urges many small sketches before painting; Andrew Loomis (*Creative Illustration*, 1947) plans every picture in three or four values first. [T]
- **Notan.** Arthur Wesley Dow (*Composition*, 1899) built art teaching on the Japanese idea of *notan*, the pattern of dark and light as a design in itself, practised in two and then three values before colour. A painting whose two-value notan is a readable shape usually reads at a glance; one whose notan is scattered does not. [T]
- **Planes have values.** John F. Carlson (*Carlson's Guide to Landscape Painting*, 1929/1958): in daylight the sky is the lightest plane, then the flat ground, then slanting planes, then upright ones; a painter keeps each plane inside its value family and doesn't let a detail break it. James Gurney (*Color and Light*, 2010) gives the same idea for figures and objects as grouping the light family and the shadow family, and "shapewelding": merging neighbouring shapes of near value into one. [T]
- **Exploration at the sketch stage is real and non-linear.** Picasso's 45 dated studies for *Guernica* (May-June 1937) are the classic dataset. Weisberg (2004) argued the process was structured and built on earlier work; Simonton (2007) argued from the same sketches that the path was non-monotonic, with variants tried and dropped, i.e. a search. Either way the painter explored many variants of the main figures and the overall composition before and during the painting, and the canvas itself kept changing (Dora Maar photographed its states). [S]
- **The academy's stages.** The 19th-century French academy taught a fixed sequence (Boime 1971): *croquis* (a quick sketch of the idea), *esquisse* (a small compositional oil sketch, judged on its own in the *concours de l'esquisse*), *études* (studies from the model for each figure and part), the *ébauche* (a monochrome or simply coloured lay-in of the big masses on the canvas) and the *fini*. Each stage had its own criteria. Teodor's phases have the shape but skip the *études*: no separate study of a figure, a hand or a face before it is painted on the canvas (round 15 found this out independently). [S]
- **What Teodor lacks:** a value-thumbnail stage (many small 2-4 value notans before any blockout, in different structures from Payne's list), a check that the chosen blockout's notan is a readable shape, value families per plane, and the *étude* for every key element.

### 2.4 Colour studies and colour mixing
- **Colour is relative.** Josef Albers (*Interaction of Color*, 1963) shows by exercise that one colour looks different on different grounds and that two different colours can be made to look the same; colour decisions are made in context, never one swatch at a time. [T]
- **A palette is a gamut.** Gurney (2010) recommends choosing the painting's gamut before painting (a shape drawn on the colour wheel; every mixture comes from inside it): a limited gamut gives colour unity, and the one colour outside it becomes an accent. Limited palettes (the "Zorn palette" of yellow ochre, vermilion, ivory black and white is the classic example) serve the same purpose. [T]
- **Premixing.** Many realist and atelier painters set out the palette before painting: strings of each colour mixed in value steps (the method taught by Frank Reilly and passed on by his students), so that value is decided at the palette and the painter "mixes once, places once". Richard Schmid (*Alla Prima*, 1998) recommends colour charts of every pair of palette colours as a way to learn the palette's range. [T]
- **Temperature.** Light and shadow differ in temperature, usually opposite (warm light with cool shadows or the reverse), and atmosphere cools and lightens distance (Gurney 2010; Leonardo's aerial perspective). [T]
- **The colour study.** Plein-air oil sketches (Constable, Corot, Valenciennes) were small colour notes made fast to fix the relations of light, separate from the studio painting. [T]
- **What Teodor lacks:** a palette and a gamut decided before painting (today any hex can appear, and `match` samples every colour from a render); a colour study at thumbnail size; a check that the canvas stays inside its gamut and where its one accent is; temperature as a measured relation between light and shadow families.

### 2.5 Working from life, from photographs and from a render
- **Translating, not copying.** Painters who work from photographs are taught to correct the camera: photographs compress values (shadows go black, lights burn out), give every part the same sharpness, distort with the lens and flatten the space; painters regroup the values, choose edges and select detail (Gurney 2010; Schmid 1998). Atelier "sight-size" teaching (canvas next to the subject, judged from a fixed distance) was devised to compare subject and canvas at the same size and distance. [T]
- **Selective detail is what makes a portrait guide the eye.** DiPaola, Riebe & Enns (2010) rendered portraits in Rembrandt's manner, sharp at the eyes and softer elsewhere, and tracked viewers: they reached the detailed eye faster, stayed longer, moved their eyes more calmly, followed a path along the "lost and found" edges, and preferred these versions to uniformly detailed ones. A follow-up (DiPaola, Riebe & Enns 2013, *Perception*) extended it. [S]
- **First impressions.** Viewers take in the gist of a painting within the first fixations and then scan it (Locher, Krupinski, Mello-Thoms & Nodine 2007, from the abstract). The commission's "a few seconds" is an empirical fact. [S]
- **What Teodor lacks:** Teodor's blockout is a CG render: even detail, hard ray-traced shadows, uniform sharpness, render lighting. The brief tells him to match it ("faithful 0.85"), so the paintings inherit the render's look (critics: "render-like", "a 3D render", "a digital filter"). Painters treat a reference as information to be *translated*: simplified into value groups, detail chosen by focus, edges decided. The schema should be translated before matching (a "painter's reference": grouped values, softened periphery), and `match`'s faithfulness should fall with distance from the focal area.

### 2.6 Revisions and pentimenti: what technical art history shows
- **Underdrawing and changes are normal.** Infrared reflectography (introduced by van Asperen de Boer, 1968-1969) made underdrawings visible in Netherlandish panels and showed painters changing positions of heads, hands and whole figures between drawing and paint; X-radiography shows changes in the lead-white-rich paint layers. Pentimenti ("repentances") are present in a very large share of old master paintings. [S]
- **Vermeer worked rough and removed things.** The Rijksmuseum's 2022 study of *The Milkmaid* (macro-XRF and reflectance imaging) found that Vermeer "first quickly painted the scene in light and dark tones before developing the detail", with "a hastily applied thick line of black paint" under the arm; he sketched a jug holder with jugs on the wall and "didn't develop them any further", and covered a fire basket with the foot stove, tiles and floor. The museum's conclusion: the picture of a slow, precise painter "is now being revised". The changes simplified the picture: things were taken out. [S]
- **Matisse restaged on the canvas.** Matisse had his *Large Reclining Nude (The Pink Nude)* (1935, Baltimore Museum of Art) photographed in its many successive states over about six months, each a substantial restaging of the figure and the grid behind it. The Art Institute of Chicago and MoMA exhibition *Matisse: Radical Invention 1913-1917* (2010) documented how he scraped, incised and repainted canvases such as *Bathers by a River* over years. [S]
- **What it means for Teodor.** Painters revise the composition *on the canvas*, late, and very often by removal or simplification; the first lay-in is tonal (Vermeer's light and dark, the academy's *ébauche*, "dead colouring" in Dutch practice). Teodor revises the blockout and then paints toward it; he rarely removes anything from the canvas and has no tool to scrape back, wipe out or paint out a passage on purpose; his block-in is in full colour from the start.

### 2.7 The medium's resistance
- **The situation talks back.** Schön (1983) described design as "a reflective conversation with the situation": the designer makes a move, the material or drawing "talks back" with consequences that were not intended, and the designer reframes the problem from them. [S]
- **Workmanship of risk.** David Pye (*The Nature and Art of Workmanship*, 1968) distinguishes the workmanship of certainty (the result fixed before making, as in a jig or a machine) from the workmanship of risk (the result depends on judgement, dexterity and care at every moment), and locates the quality we value in handmade things in the second. Ingold (2010) argues that making follows the flows of the material rather than imposing a form on it; James Elkins (*What Painting Is*, 1999) writes about oil paint's own behaviour as a partner in the painter's thinking. [S], [T]
- **What Teodor lacks.** `match` is a workmanship of certainty: it measures the distance to the schema and paints where the canvas differs, so the painting converges on the render. The paint's resistance (wet pickup, depletion, dry-brush breaks) exists in the engine, but the process never asks the painter to *notice* what the paint did that he didn't plan and to decide whether to keep it. A journal line "what the paint did on its own, and whether I kept it" and a habit of letting a happy accident change the plan would put the conversation back.

### 2.8 Deciding when a work is finished
- **Two kinds of painters.** Galenson (*Old Masters and Young Geniuses*, 2006) separates experimental innovators, who work by trial and error toward goals they can't state precisely, revise endlessly and often find it hard to call a work finished (Cézanne), from conceptual ones, who plan precisely and execute (Picasso of the early period). Teodor's brief asks for both at once. [S]
- **Finished vs complete.** Whistler: "a picture is finished when all trace of the means used to bring about the end has disappeared" and his distinction, argued in the Ruskin trial (1878), that a work is finished when the artist's intention is achieved, not when every part is detailed (Whistler, *The Gentle Art of Making Enemies*, 1890). The Met Breuer's exhibition *Unfinished: Thoughts Left Visible* (2016) shows how often painters left visibly unfinished passages on purpose. [T], [S]
- **Artists evaluate more.** In a think-aloud study of artists and non-artists drawing, artists produced more evaluative and metacognitive statements, and planned globally while working locally (Fayena-Tawil, Kozbelt & Sitaras 2011, "Think global, act local"; given from its title and abstract as cited in later reviews, not re-read). Mace & Ward's finalization stage (already in `docs/research.md`) includes the decision to stop. [S]
- **Studio criteria** [T]: stop when the painting says what it set out to say; check the whole in the mirror and from a distance before stopping; prefer stopping early to overworking; leave the unimportant parts less resolved.
- **What Teodor lacks:** Teodor stops at an episode budget ("aim for 60-100 episodes") and finishes with a list of fine touches. There is no stopping test: does a cold viewer read the feeling, do the checks pass, and would one more move only explain?

### 2.9 Developing and breaking a personal style
- **Habituation drives change.** Martindale (*The Clockwork Muse*, 1990) argues that the need for arousal potential against habituation pushes artistic styles to change, alternating between more novel content and new stylistic means; an artist's own development has the same pressure. Teodor's habits list is this mechanism, imposed from outside. [S]
- **Copies, series and variations.** Painters learn and change style by copying masters (the copyists' permits of the Louvre and the Prado), by series (Monet's grainstacks and cathedrals: one motif under many lights) and by variations on another painter's work (Picasso's 58 *Las Meninas* paintings of 1957). These change one thing while holding the others. [T]
- **What Teodor lacks:** a positive account of his voice (what is *his* and should stay), alongside the negative habit list; series (one motif under different constraints); and exercises that change one dimension at a time (a master copy of one passage, a limited-palette study).

### 2.10 Critique in ateliers and art schools
- **Corrections on the work.** In the academy and in today's ateliers the teacher corrects on or beside the student's work, comparatively and concretely ("this shadow is lighter than that light", "this edge is harder than any edge on the head"), daily; the *concours* judged sketches and finished work separately (Boime 1971). [S], [T]
- **The crit.** Art-school critiques mix intention and reception: the student says what they meant, peers and tutors respond. James Elkins (*Why Art Cannot Be Taught*, 2001) analyses the crit as the central and most unpredictable event of art teaching; Blair (2006) documents how students experience it (anxiety, unclear criteria). [S]
- **What Teodor lacks:** Teodor's critic is a cold reader only, which is right for reading the *feeling*, but gives no informed, comparative correction of the craft. Painters get both: the stranger's first reading (an exhibition) and the master's specific corrections (the atelier).

## Part 3. Proposals, ranked by expected impact on output quality

Ranked by how much each should change what a visitor sees, per unit of work [H]. Each is marked **brief** (`docs/prompts/painter-brief.md`), **orchestration** (`docs/orchestration.md`, the orchestrator's steps) or **engine** (the studio, `studio/v7` or a v8). All thresholds below are starting values [H], to be tuned against critic and owner readings (proposal 18).

1. **Eyes that measure: a value view with numbers and flags.** *(engine, brief)* `look values` writes a 3-5 group value map (k-means on lightness after a squint blur) and prints the share of canvas per group, the number of distinct groups, the key (mean lightness), the range, and flags: "middle third of the value range holds over 50% of the canvas" (the murky middle of rounds 6 and 15), "fewer than 3 groups", "one group over 45% with no detail" (round 15's empty blue block), "darkest group under 5% in a low-key style". The brief makes it part of the look at the end of every stage and every 8 episodes. Spectre's lesson that "the ears are the bottleneck" applies here first: today Teodor has no numbers at all.
2. **Translate the blockout into a painter's reference before matching.** *(engine, brief)* A `ref` option (or a `translate` command) that groups the render's values into the planned families (shapewelding), softens detail and edges with distance from a declared focal point, and lifts the render's black shadows; and `match` whose `faithful` and minimum brush size fall with distance from the focal point. That is how painters use photographs (2.5) and what DiPaola et al. measured (selective detail guides the eye and is preferred). It attacks "render-like", "digital filter" and "every edge equally soft" at the source.
3. **An edge view and a focal check.** *(engine)* `look edges` classifies edges as hard, firm, soft or lost (gradient magnitude across scale) and prints the share of hard edges inside the declared focal region against outside; `look saliency` (a contrast, edge and colour saliency map) prints where the top three peaks are and whether the first is in the focal region. Flags: "edge sharpness uniform", "focal region not the most salient", "several equal peaks" (round 13's six heads). The brief asks the painter to declare the focal region in the handoff.
4. **A value-thumbnail stage with an owner audition.** *(brief, orchestration)* Before any 3D blockout, six to ten tiny 2-3 value notans (SVG, about 120×160) in different structures from Payne's list, chosen by how fast the feeling reads; the chosen one becomes the value plan the blockout is lit to. The orchestrator shows Robert the two or three best as one contact sheet and he picks by gut, as Spectre's owner picks between sound stacks. Cheap, and it is where staging habits (one central figure, a radial pull) are cheapest to break.
5. **An element clarity view, Spectre's masking measure for paintings.** *(engine)* The blockout knows its objects, so the studio can render an object-ID mask with the schema. `look clarity` prints, per element (each figure, head, hand, the main object, sky, ground), its value and colour contrast against its immediate surround at thumbnail scale, the share of its silhouette that is lost, and its share of the saliency; flag an element that should carry the feeling but is "masked" (contrast below the threshold at thumb size), and a figure whose silhouette fails (rounds 12 and 13: "a tiny unreadable figure").
6. **Études before painting key elements.** *(brief)* Every face, hand and key gesture gets a drawn tonal study (an SVG or a small canvas) checked in the gray view before it is matched on the main canvas: the academy's *études*, and round 15's own finding. The brief also tells the painter to measure the figure against the blockout (proportions, angles, negative shapes) and to draw observed light shapes, not symbols of eyes and mouths (categorical schemas, 2.1).
7. **Nearest wrong feelings and nearest wrong looks, named in the brief.** *(orchestration, brief)* The orchestrator writes two to four wrong readings per commission (from the theme and from past critics: "a fun dream", "a candy wrapper", "a hospital ward") and the wrong looks (a filter, a render, an illustration, a greeting card); the painter writes in `feeling.md` what visibly separates the target from each; every critic reading is scored against both lists in the journal. Spectre's most transferable single habit.
8. **Style research as sourced data, and a measured target per current.** *(orchestration, engine)* Fill `docs/styles/<id>.md` and `studio/styles.json` before the first painting in a current (the template already exists), and add image statistics measured on 20-40 public-domain reference paintings of the current: value histogram and key, saturation and temperature balance, edge density and hard-edge share, spectral slope, edge-orientation entropy. `look stats` reports the canvas against the current's band, like Spectre's master report; a large deviation sends the painter back to paint (Spectre's "fix the mix", never an automatic correction). The finishing stage per current comes from the same doc.
9. **A hand with articulations and correlated variation.** *(engine)* New stroke articulations (load-then-drag, swell-and-taper, a turn at the form's edge, broken-colour scumble, a knife scrape, a rag wipe) and variation in pressure, angle, width and load that drifts across a passage instead of jittering per stroke (the visual analogue of Hennig et al.'s correlated humanizing, which Spectre adopted). The handover names the uniform square dab as the ceiling; this is its cause.
10. **An expectation ledger: set up, then break once.** *(brief)* In the handoff: what the painting establishes (a dominant direction, a value key, a temperature family, a soft-edge world, a mark scale) and the one or two places where it breaks it on purpose (the one hard edge, the one warm accent, the one counter-diagonal, the figure that turns away); verify with proposals 1 and 3 that the break is the strongest point on the canvas. Correct is even; compelling is a planned exception (Spectre PRODUCTION.md section 4; Dow's subordination).
11. **The painter's checks as views: mirror, upside-down, squint.** *(engine, brief)* `look mirror`, `look flip`, `look squint` (a blur that merges values into groups, coarser than `thumb`). The brief requires a full check round (thumb, squint, mirror, gray, values, edges) at the end of each stage and before stopping, and asks the painter to journal one thing each check showed that the normal view hid. Upside-down is for judging design, not accuracy (2.2).
12. **A palette and gamut set before painting, with a temperature view.** *(brief, engine)* The painter chooses a gamut (a limited palette or a gamut mask) in conception; `match` maps colours into it by default, and anything outside becomes an explicit accent. `look temp` shows warm against cool relative to the canvas average and prints the light-family vs shadow-family temperature difference; `look gamut` plots the canvas's colours against the declared gamut.
13. **A tonal lay-in (ébauche) before colour.** *(brief)* Block in the value plan in one or two earth colours first (Vermeer's light-and-dark lay-in, the academy's *ébauche*, Dutch dead colouring), check it with proposal 1, then paint colour over it. Value is settled before colour can muddy it.
14. **Tools to subtract, and revision on the canvas.** *(engine, brief)* `scrape` (knife back to the layer below), `wipe` (rag lift toward the ground), and painting out a passage, recorded as moves; the brief encourages removing an element when the painting asks for it (Vermeer's jug rack, Matisse's states). Spectre's "subtraction is the strongest move", applied to paint.
15. **Two critics: the cold reader and the master.** *(orchestration)* Keep the cold critic for the feeling. Add a craft critic who sees the canvas, the feeling, the handoff and the measurement views, and gives three concrete, comparative atelier corrections ("this shadow is lighter than that light"; "this edge is harder than any edge on the head"). The painter treats both as symptoms and diagnoses with the views (Spectre's standing rule 10).
16. **A stopping test.** *(brief)* Replace the episode target with: stop when (a) the last cold critic's first-three-seconds reading is the feeling, not a nearest wrong one, (b) the check round passes or each flag is a decision written in the journal, and (c) one more move would only explain. Episode counts become a budget, not a goal.
17. **The conversation with the paint.** *(brief)* Each journal line also notes what the paint did that wasn't planned (a dry-brush break, a muddy mix, a lucky edge) and whether it was kept; once per phase the painter must keep and build on one accident (Schön's back-talk, Pye's workmanship of risk).
18. **A verdict log to calibrate the eyes.** *(orchestration)* For every checkpoint, record the critics' and Robert's readings next to the numbers of proposals 1, 3, 5 and 8 for that canvas (`rounds/NN/critiques/metrics.json`). Over rounds this shows which numbers predict "muddy", "filter" or "unreadable" and which don't; the thresholds above are tuned from it. Spectre: "the only way the ears can be calibrated".
19. **Lessons that transfer, standing rules and a fuller handoff.** *(orchestration)* A "What transfers" section of positive principles (rounds 0-15), Robert's decisions as numbered, dated standing rules in `docs/orchestration.md`, and in `docs/handover.md` a per-round state table, a known engine gaps list and a tried-and-rejected list. Low effort; protects everything above from being lost between sessions.
20. **Structure contact sheet, quotes as hinges, series.** *(orchestration, brief)* Make the past-work contact sheet also in notan form (two values) so staging habits are visible; allow a quote of a public-domain painting's device only as the hinge of the picture, never its composition; now and then commission a series (one motif under changed constraints) to develop a voice, not only to avoid habits.
