## 1. In one paragraph
Painting in fifteenth- and early sixteenth-century Italy and the Low Countries, from the first Florentine perspective pictures and Netherlandish oil panels to the High Renaissance around 1500-1520. It wanted a convincing, measured world: a picture as a constructed space seen from one point, bodies modelled in light, and materials (gold brocade, skin, landscape) imitated in paint rather than applied [1][2][3]. Brunelleschi demonstrated linear perspective around 1420 and Alberti codified it in *On Painting* within two decades [2]. Technically it is the period in which Italian painters moved from egg tempera to oil, learning from the Netherlandish handling of oil on panel [1][3]. Representative painters (context only): Masaccio, Piero della Francesca, Jan van Eyck, Giovanni Bellini, Leonardo, Raphael.

## 2. How it carries feeling
- **Measured order and calm:** a single vanishing point on the viewer's horizon and receding orthogonals put the viewer in a stable, rational relation to the scene [2]. Feeling is contained in a lucid structure **[J]**.
- **Soft transitions of light (sfumato):** smoky, edgeless shadows make a face seem inward and alive; in Leonardo's *Virgin of the Rocks* the flesh is two opaque layers with shadows blended by thinning the surface layer, giving an intended "marble-like" cast [4].
- **Restrained gesture and glance:** emotion carried by the tilt of a head, a hand, a shared look inside a balanced group **[J]**.
- **Avoided:** violent contrast, theatrical darkness, open brushwork, asymmetrical drama (these come with the Baroque) **[J]**. Emotion research: `docs/research/emotion.md` once written (for now `docs/research.md`).

## 3. Media, supports and grounds
- **Italy, early:** egg tempera on gessoed poplar panel: size, glued canvas, gesso grosso then gesso sottile, scraped flat [5]. Tempera dries fast and light and "must be applied thinly in short, hatched strokes", giving a schematic rendition of light and shade [1].
- **Shift to oil:** oil "dries slowly and can be worked wet-on-wet", allows transparent glazes, and painting gold "with colour" came to be valued more than gilding, following Alberti [3]. Oil was the major medium in Europe by the sixteenth century [1].
- **North:** oil on panel with virtuoso glazing; "admixture in oils makes most pigments translucent", producing "rich, glowing reds and greens", and even earths can be glazed across broad areas [1].
- **Leonardo (example):** poplar panel, gesso and glue ground, a light grey oil priming, later a greyish-cream priming; walnut and linseed oils [4].
- **Default for Teodor:** oil on `panel`, white gesso ground `#f3efe4` with a thin light grey priming `#cfcac0` **[J]** following [4]; alternative egg tempera on panel for the early (quattrocento) manner.

## 4. Palette
- **Principle:** clear local colours modelled in value within each colour zone; jewel-like blues and reds as the colour climaxes; earths and greens for landscape; atmospheric blue for distance in the High Renaissance **[J]** (aerial blue: standard description, not in a read source).
- **Pigments** (from [4] unless marked; hex values approximate **[J]**):
  - natural ultramarine `#253f8e`, azurite `#2f5d97`
  - red lake `#9b1f35`, vermilion `#c8361f` **[L]** [6]
  - lead-tin yellow `#e3c654`, yellow ochre `#c79b4a`
  - verdigris `#3f7d63`, green earth (flesh underlayer) `#7f8b62` **[L]** [6]
  - earth browns `#6b4a2f`, charcoal black `#22201e`, lead white `#f2ede2`
- **Avoid:** muddy chiaroscuro, high-chroma modern colour, colours that break up into strokes.

## 5. Light and value
A single, steady, usually daylight source from one side; full-value modelling in High Renaissance oil, a lighter, more even key in tempera [1][4]. Shadows soft and transparent-looking in oil; in tempera they are built by hatching over a green-earth underlayer in flesh **[L]** [6].

## 6. Drawing and form
Drawing is the foundation: an underdrawing on the ground, in Leonardo's case a fluid brownish-black design that he could recast entirely [4]. Form is modelled in light; anatomy and proportion are studied and idealised **[J]**. Contours firm in the Early Renaissance, softened (sfumato) in the High [4].

## 7. Composition
Linear perspective with a single vanishing point on the horizon [2]; centralised, symmetrical or pyramidal groups; architecture or a measured landscape as the stage **[J]**; Northern pictures often use a high viewpoint and dense, minutely described detail [1].

## 8. Marks and edges
Small soft brushes; tempera in fine hatched strokes [1]; oil laid smooth and blended, brush marks largely hidden **[J]**. Edge hierarchy: crisp outlines in early tempera; lost, smoky edges in High Renaissance flesh [4].

## 9. Process (the order of work)
**Oil (High Renaissance, after [4]):**
1. Panel, gesso and glue ground.
2. Underdrawing in a fluid dark medium.
3. Light grey (or greyish-cream) oil priming.
4. Monochrome underpainting: dark yellow, brown and blackish-brown, broadly brushed [4].
5. Lay-in of colour per zone: e.g. grey underpaint, then azurite, then ultramarine for blue drapery; earths, black and verdigris over the underpaint for rocks and foliage [4].
6. Flesh: opaque layers, shadows blended by thinning the top layer (sfumato) [4].
7. Glazes and scumbles of ultramarine and azurite over red-lake-and-black bases for rich drapery [4].
8. Last details; gilded accents applied over the final paint if any [4].

**Tempera (Early, Italy):** panel and gesso [5]; drawing; gilding; green-earth underlayer on flesh **[L]** [6]; local colours in three values per colour, hatched light over dark **[L]** [6]; varnish once dry **[L]** [6].

Teodor's phases **[J]**: phase 1 = drawing, perspective construction (`scene` blockout suits this current) and `transfer`; phase 2 = monochrome underpainting and colour lay-in; phase 3 = modelling, sfumato, glazes, details.

## 10. Finishing
"Finished" meant a seamless surface with resolved detail, though real works can be uneven: Leonardo's panel is "notably uneven in its level of completion" [4]. The last stage: final glazes, small gilded accents, then varnish **[J]** for the varnish layer (standard practice; not in a read source).

## 11. Pitfalls for Teodor
- **Pastiche:** the "Mona Lisa" smile and blurred everything; a perspective grid floor as decoration; cracked-varnish yellowing.
- **Nearest wrong feelings:** sentimental sweetness (late Victorian pseudo-Renaissance), cold diagrammatic geometry, Baroque melodrama creeping in via too-dark grounds **[J]**.
- Sfumato is not overall blur: contours inside a sfumato face are lost by thinning the top layer, but the structure stays drawn [4].
- **Engine:** wet blending works in oil; tempera hatching and gold-as-material don't exist yet.
