# Dutch Golden Age painting (genre scenes, still life, landscape)

Researched 2026-11-25. Citations in brackets refer to the numbered sources at the end. **[J]** marks a judgement call.

## 1. In one paragraph
Painting in the seventeenth-century Dutch Republic, made largely for an open market of private buyers and specialised by subject: scenes of everyday life (genre), still life, landscape, portraits, church and town views. Genre painting ranked in the middle of art theory but was hugely popular, partly for the chance "to gaze into a private interior" [1]; still life became an independent genre in the early 1600s, with flower pieces, the monochrome "banquet" or "breakfast" piece, vanitas and later the sumptuous pronk piece [2]; landscape moved through a "tonal" phase from the late 1620s into the 1640s, with "a unified space, convincing light and atmosphere, a tonal palette", toward more colourful pictures by the 1650s-60s [3]. What it wanted: the convincing, quiet truth of ordinary things, light and weather, often with moral or associative undertones [1][2][3]. Context names only: Frans Hals, Jan van Goyen, Pieter de Hooch, Johannes Vermeer, Willem Claesz Heda, Gerrit Dou.

## 2. How it carries feeling
- **Quiet stillness and order:** the Delft painters' "compositional clarity, balance, and order" evokes "a private world of quiet stillness" [1].
- **Daylight and weather as feeling:** landscapes are "rarely symbolic but ... usually rich in associations", with the pleasure of fresh air, daylight, wind and weather [3]; "towering vaults of clouds" over wide views to the horizon [3].
- **Transience held in objects:** still lifes carry messages about the fleeting nature of life and worldly desire, a watch or a half-eaten meal standing for it [2].
- **Humour and appetite:** genre scenes can be moralising and "just as often hilarious in effect" [1].
- **Tonal unity:** a coloured ground left showing in thin shadows warms and unifies the picture [4][5].
- **Avoided:** heroic drama, idealised bodies, theatrical darkness (contrast with the Baroque doc), visible bravura in most specialties (Hals is the exception) **[J]**. Emotion research: `docs/research/emotion.md` once written (for now `docs/research.md`).

## 3. Media, supports and grounds
- **Historical:** oil on oak panel, canvas, occasionally copper [4][6]. Coloured grounds were the norm in the seventeenth century: grey, beige, brown and red are most frequent; on canvas a grey-over-red double ground is common; on panel a chalk-glue layer under a pigmented oil layer from light beige to light brown [4]. The light-versus-dark choice mattered more than the precise colour [4]. Delft painters tended to use single warm cream layers; Haarlem painters light peachy-beige before 1650 [4].
- **Ground functions:** a mid-tone, a "safety net" for tone (van de Wetering), "a middle tone between the shadows and the highlights"; Jan Davidsz de Heem advised to "leave the ground a little to be seen between light and shadow" [4].
- **Default for Teodor:** oil on `panel`, warm cream-beige ground `#cdb894` **[J]** (within [4]'s light beige range); for landscape a warm beige too [4]; for a fijnschilder interior or flower piece a lighter cream `#e3d6bb` **[J]**.

## 4. Palette
- **Principle:** a restrained, harmonised palette of earths with a few precious accents; a near-monochrome tonal palette for the banquet piece and the tonal landscape [2][3]; fuller colour later in the century [3].
- **Pigments** (standard Dutch palette **[J]**; hex approximate **[J]**):
  - lead white `#efe9dc`, lead-tin yellow `#e1c75a`, yellow ochre `#c39a4c`
  - vermilion `#be3a26`, red lake `#8c2236`
  - natural ultramarine `#26418f`, smalt `#5a6f9c`, azurite `#33608f`
  - green earth `#727a57`, verdigris/copper-resinate green `#3f6b4a`
  - raw umber `#62513b`, Cassel/brown earth `#4a3a2a`, bone black `#1b1916`
  - tonal-landscape greys and browns `#9a8b6e`, `#b9ad92` (a mixed range, after [3]'s "tonal palette")
- **Avoid:** high saturation across the picture, cool modern greys unrelated to the ground, colour used for expression rather than description **[J]**.

## 5. Light and value
Interiors: daylight from a side window, naturalistic and "painstakingly" studied [1]; night pieces lit by candle or hearth (Dou) [1]. Still life: glistening light against a dark or neutral background in the pronk piece [2]; even, silvery-grey light in the monochrome banquet piece **[J]**. Landscape: diffuse daylight under a large sky; middle key with tonal unity [3]. Shadows thin, semi-transparent, with the warm ground showing [5].

## 6. Drawing and form
Accurate observational drawing; form built tonally over the dead colour [5]. Fijnschilders: "small-format works of high finish and minute detail" [1]. Textures (glass, pewter, silk, bread crust) described through value and highlight **[J]**.

## 7. Composition
Genre interiors: a measured box of space, windows and doorways, figures in quiet occupation, clarity and balance [1]. Still life: a tabletop seen slightly from above, objects in a diagonal or pyramidal pile, an overhanging edge (knife, cloth, peel) projecting toward the viewer **[J]**. Landscape: low horizon with most of the panel given to sky; wide river or dune views [3] (low horizon: **[J]**, implied by [3]'s "wide views extending to the horizon and towering vaults of clouds").

## 8. Marks and edges
Mostly fine, smooth handling with soft-hair brushes; impasto reserved for highlights that catch raking light [5]; tonal landscapes painted quickly in two stages, the brown sketch left exposed in the darks [4]. Edges soft where the ground is left "between light and shadow" [4]; crisp on highlights and fine detail **[J]**.

## 9. Process (the indirect method: the order of work)
1. Ground: chalk-glue on panel then a warm oil priming, or a grey-over-red double ground on canvas [4].
2. Sketch of the composition on the ground (brown or chalk lines) **[J]** (in [6] for Rembrandt: fine sketch lines, then a painted sketch).
3. Dead colouring: a monochrome (brown, or brown and black) underpainting establishing composition, light and dark [5][6].
4. Working up (*opmaken*): each area "executed as a separate entity and finished in one or two sessions", lights in full body colour of one or two pigments, shadows in thin semi-transparent layers over the warm ground [5]; often from back to front, setting the wall and the light first [5].
5. Glazes to deepen and unify colour in the final stages [5].
6. Highlights in impasto and last accents (reflections on metal and glass, the "ground showing between light and shadow") [4][5].
- **Variant, tonal landscape:** two stages only: a monochrome brown sketch on a warm beige ground, then a light final layer, the sketch left in the darkest areas [4].

Teodor's phases **[J]**: phase 1 = drawing and dead colouring (`match` with a brown palette); phase 2 = working up area by area (`clip` to a region, back to front); phase 3 = glazes, highlights, accents.

## 10. Finishing
Finished meant a coherent surface with every area resolved; for fijnschilders, a high, smooth finish [1]. The last stage: unifying glazes, small impasto highlights, then varnish **[J]** (standard practice; not in a read source).

## 11. Pitfalls for Teodor
- **Pastiche:** a pearl earring, a blue-and-yellow maid at a window, a skull and candle; fake varnish yellowing.
- **Nearest wrong feelings:** sterile photographic hyperrealism; decorative Victorian clutter; a Baroque spotlight drama; Teodor's hushed doorway habit (a de Hooch doorway is exactly that habit: avoid unless the commission asks for it) **[J]**.
- **Monochrome ≠ grey:** tonal pictures are warm, harmonised earths, not desaturated photos **[J]**.
- **Engine:** fine detail at fijnschilder scale is beyond the brush model at 1200x1600; aim for the still-life or landscape scale of handling.

## 12. Engine mapping (studio v8)
- **Medium:** `oil --support panel` (exists); canvas for larger landscapes.
- **Schema:** `scene` blockouts suit interiors and still-life tabletops (one window light).
- **Tools:** `round` and `filbert` for working up; `liner` for reflections, glints and fine detail; `blend` for soft transitions; `knife` not used **[J]**; `glaze` intention for the final unifying layers.
- **Intentions:** `match` with a brown palette for the dead colouring; `match` with `clip` per region for the working up; low `load` and `cover` in shadows so the ground shows; high `impasto` only on highlights.
- **Felt state:** low arousal, low tension, high care.
- **Views:** `look gray` after dead colouring; `look temp` to keep the ground's warmth in the shadows.
- **Gaps:** glazes are uniform regional mixes, not brushed transparent paint; no per-pigment transparency; no varnish finishing op; detail limited by canvas resolution.
