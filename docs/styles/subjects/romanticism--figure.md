# Romanticism: the figure (layer 2)

How Romanticism departs from the layer-1 body ([docs/subjects/figure.md](../../subjects/figure.md)). Read with the style doc ([docs/styles/romanticism.md](../romanticism.md)) and the sibling pages for `face`, `hands-feet` and `hair` in this folder. Researched 2026-11-26. **[J]** marks a judgement call; everything else names its source. Works are named only as examples of what several artists share; no rule rests on one artist.

## 1. The twist in one paragraph
Neoclassical and academic practice set the body as an ideal: "ideal beauties, brain-born images", "a more beauteous and more perfect nature", with "a noble simplicity and sedate grandeur in gesture and expression" (Winckelmann, quoted in [2]). Its studio nude, the *académie*, looks like "well-lit classical sculpture, with firm contours reinforced by gently blended modeling" [6]. Romanticism keeps the anatomy but drops the calm. The body becomes the carrier of a passion and of the weather around it: twisted, off balance, arms flung up along the storm's diagonals, modelled by masses of light and dark rather than by a clean outline, painted with the hand visible [1][3][4][5]. Its people are often ordinary, observed ones (sailors, labourers, a woman fetching water) rather than heroes; the Met calls the *Raft of the Medusa* remarkable for its "horrifying explicitness, emotional intensity, and conspicuous lack of a hero" [1].

## 3. How it departs from layer 1
**Proportions.** The current keeps the natural canon (7 to 7.5 heads) rather than the 8-head ideal; what changes is mass, not length: torsos and arms are heavy and fleshy, recalling Michelangelo and Rubens rather than Greek statuary ([4]; the *Raft* figures "called back to Michelangelo" [3]) **[J]** for the head count, read by eye on the works above. Bodies of working people are drawn as they are: thick waists, broad hands, weight in the hips (Delacroix's water-carrier, Géricault's blacksmith, Goya's dancers) **[J]**, observation.

**Gesture and balance.** The line of action is pushed past life: arms raised to full stretch, torsos twisted against the pelvis, the whole body set on a diagonal that continues the storm, the sea or the sail. In the *Raft*, "bodies extended together as their arms gesture upward in a strong diagonal" [3]; in *Christ Asleep* every disciple's arm is up or out along the boat's tilt. Balance is often unstable, held by an outside force (wind, a rope, another body) or not held at all; even in drawing, the Romantic nude shows "disequilibrium" where the académie stands firm [6]. This is layer 1's "half-committed gesture" mistake turned into a rule: commit fully.

**What it idealises, simplifies, leaves out.** It idealises the *passion*, not the body: a face may get a classical profile (Liberty's is "a perfect classical profile" on an ordinary, striding body [7]) but skin, weight and sweat stay real. It simplifies the anatomy into a few big masses lit from one side; small surface anatomy (tendons, individual muscles below the big groups) is left out at any distance beyond half-length **[J]**. It leaves out the frieze: figures overlap, turn their backs, and are cut by waves and cloth, against David's arrangement where "clear, even light ... makes it possible to see everything" [3].

**The single figure in nature.** Two conventions, both shared across artists: (a) the figure set against sky and sea, often with a low horizon so the body rises into the sky (Géricault's Dedreux and *I Dream of Her*, Delacroix's Ophelia, Friedrich's woodcut); (b) the body turned toward the light or the sky, the face lifted (Friedrich's woman, Fuseli's hag, Goya's woman, the *Raft*'s hope figures). The figure and the weather share one feeling; the Met: landscape as "one state of mind" [1]. In the sublime landscape branch the figure is tiny ([1], Turner's Hannibal "dwarfed"); a large near figure belongs to the French figure branch **[J]**.

**Colour, edges and marks.** Flesh is warm in the light and takes the surround's colour in shadow (the greens of the sea in *Christ Asleep*); shadows are built from "strands of colour", not from local colour mixed with black, "a far cry from conventional academic chiaroscuro" [5]. Contours are broken and discontinuous, the inside volumes dominate [6]; edges are lost where the body turns into shadow or into a dark ground, and found on the lit side against a dark or the dark side against a light sky **[J]**, observed on the works above. Brushwork stays visible: "we easily see the hand of the artist" [7]; "a vigorous painterly technique that prioritized the freshness of the initial sketch" [4]. Light is dramatic, from one side, "strong contrasts of light and dark", against David's even light [3].

**Measured (weak).** A crisp-edge share (the share of pixels with an L* gradient over 8 per pixel, among those over 2, on 800-px-wide Met and CMA images) gave 0.02-0.19 for five Romantic oils (Delacroix *Christ Asleep* 0.16, *Rebecca* 0.18, *Pierret* 0.02; Fuseli 0.14; Géricault *Dedreux* 0.16; Chassériau *Desdemona* 0.19) against 0.23-0.28 for two Davids, but Girodet's 1823 portrait gave 0.15. The tendency (softer edges) is there; the ranges overlap and photography, cracks and backgrounds confound the measure, so no threshold is set from it. Overall value key did **not** separate the currents (the photographed Davids are as dark as the Delacroixs).

**What stays true to life.** Anatomy, weight and the head unit (layer 1 sections 1-2); the line of action and the opposite tilts of shoulders and hips; contact and overlap. Romantic figures were studied from living models and even from the morgue: Géricault "began drawing the bodies of the living and the dead" [3].

## 4. For this brief (a sturdy woman of about forty leaning into a gale, torso twisted toward us, one arm up gripping a flying sheet, open and joyful)
- The pose is a Romantic pose: the raised arm with a cloth against the sky is the *Raft*'s signal figure and Liberty's flag [3][7]; Ophelia's raised arm gripping a branch is the same arm in a calmer key (CMA 1941.215.13). Put the raised arm, the torso and the windward leg on one long diagonal from the braced heel to the gripping hand.
- Heavy, working masses: broad shoulders and hips, a thick waist, the weight visibly in the braced leg (layer 1 "external forces"). Do not slim her toward an ideal; do not give her a heroic eight-head canon **[J]**.
- The twist toward us is good: it avoids the Rückenfigur, Teodor's recorded habit and a Romantic cliché (style doc, section 11).
- Clothing and sheet move with the same wind as the hair (layer 1 hair and drapery); the skirt wraps the windward leg.
- Set her against the sky with a low horizon so the raised arm and sheet are seen against light cloud; she is large in the frame (the figure branch), not a speck in a sublime landscape.

## 5. Conflict flagged
**Joy.** The current's figures in storms register awe, terror, despair or hope ([1][3]: the *Raft* runs "from madness to despair" to "an expression of hope"); none of the Met or Cleveland works checked shows a joyful figure in a gale. The nearest shared models are the hope figure of the *Raft* and Goya's dancers. Follow the research on *how* to paint (diagonal, heavy masses, broken edges, dramatic side light, gesture over detail) and let the joy come from the open, upward gesture; the face page covers the laugh itself. This is the brief's choice of feeling, not a factual error, so it stands **[J]**.

## 6. Checks for Teodor
1. One diagonal line of action from braced foot to raised hand, continuing the wind's direction.
2. Shoulders and hips twisted against each other more than in life; the raised arm at full stretch.
3. Masses heavy and few; no small anatomy beyond the big groups at full-figure distance.
4. Edges: lost on the turning shadow side and into the dark ground, found where the lit body meets a dark or the dark body meets light sky; no continuous outline.
5. Shadows on flesh carry the surround's colour; no black-mixed shadows.
6. The figure reads against the sky; the horizon low.
