# Romanticism: trees and plants (layer 2)

How Romantic landscape painting (c. 1790–1850) renders vegetation, written as a departure from layer 1 (`docs/subjects/trees-plants.md`). This first entry covers the case of rough coastal grass flattened and combed by the wind and catching raking light. Trees in storm are noted briefly at the end. The ground under the grass is in `romanticism--landscape.md`. Style research: `docs/styles/romanticism.md`.

Researched 2026-11-26. **[J]** marks a judgement call. "Measured" values come from the museums' open-access images (method in section 8).

## 2. The twist in one paragraph
Layer 1 treats grass as "a plane with a top-lit colour and a texture that shrinks with distance", and notes that in wind, branches and grasses "bend downwind together". The Romantic treatment makes the wind visible: the grass is painted as a mass of strokes that all lean one way, and its lights are a scatter of warm flicks where raking light catches the bent tops. Detail is spent only at the near edge and against the light. Cox asked students to make "correct drawings from Nature, of weeds, plants … and such objects as usually constitute the foreground" and to draw strongly in the foreground [1]. The Cleveland label for Cox's *On the Thames* puts the effect plainly: "small dashes of color throughout the foreground trees create a sense of rustling vegetation" [2].

## 3. Departures from layer 1

**Shape and proportion (combed and massed):**
- **One direction for everything.** Blades, reeds and tufts lean the same way, which is the sky's wind direction (Leypold's reeds and grass, Cox's field, Martin's heath strokes, Cole's and Church's tossed foliage). Layer 1's near-vertical blades are tilted, often 30–60° from vertical in the nearest tufts **[J]** (read by eye on Leypold and Cox).
- **Grass as a mass with a ragged top.** The body of a grass bank is one dark shape. Only its top edge and the nearest tufts are broken into separate blades (Leypold's foreground reeds, Cox's bank). Interior blades are not drawn.
- **Flattened grass shows as streaks.** Where the wind presses grass flat, the strokes lie almost along the slope in long, curved, combed sweeps rather than upright flicks **[J]**, extrapolated from Martin's and Cox's dragged slope strokes.

**Value and light (raking light, sparse lights):**
- **Dark body, light tips.** The grass mass sits in the foreground's dark band (`romanticism--landscape.md`: bottom 15% at L* 11–25 in the oils). The light is a sparse scatter of bright touches on the tips and bent tops facing the break (Constable's flecks, Leypold's pale seed-heads, Cox's scraped and gouache lights). Constable's "small smatterings of white paint", which critics called "Constable's snow", are the extreme case [3].
- **Raking light.** A low break lights the tops and sides of bent tufts and leaves the hollows between them dark. The lights therefore come in short strokes along the lean, not as dots **[J]**, consistent with layer 1's raking light.
- **Watercolour lights are taken out.** Cox's *Journey Home* lists "reductive techniques" [4] and Martin's downs the same [5]. Grass lights are scraped, lifted or reserved, and some are added in gouache (*Driving sheep*: "heightened with white gouache (bodycolor) and gum arabic" [6]).

**Colour:**
- **Ochre, straw, russet and olive,** not green. Measured: Cox's lit field grass about #a98466 and its shadow #7b6752; Martin's heath #9b7a5d; Leypold's lit grass #5e401d (warmed by varnish); Michel's lit grass #37352b **(measured, approximate)**. Coastal and storm grass in these works is late-season or weathered **[J]**. Brighter greens appear only in spring or summer scenes and are still broken with earth.
- **Warm lights, cool darks.** Cox: "all the lights in a picture should be composed of warm tints" [1]. The lit tips are straw or gold, and the dark between the tufts is olive-brown, cooled toward grey-blue where the sky reflects.

**Simplified or left out:**
- Species detail beyond the nearest tufts is left out.
- Individual blades in the middle distance are left out; there, grass is a toned plane with a few horizontal strokes showing the wind's sweep.
- Flowers and colour accents are almost absent in storm pieces (a few in Leypold's verge).

## 4. Edges and marks
- **Strokes:** short to medium, tapered, flicked in one direction (upward and downwind) for standing tufts; long curved drags for flattened grass. Each stroke is a gesture, not a filled shape **[J]**.
- **Near edge against light:** the crest of the bank against the bright distance or sky is broken into a fringe of separate blades and seed-heads. This is the one place to draw single blades (Leypold, Cox).
- **Order:** dark mass first, then mid-tone strokes in the wind direction, then the sparse lights last, laid on top or scraped out. Cox's general method runs the same way: shadows first, then "a tint something lighter … to break the shadows into the light branches by small touches" [1].
- **Spacing:** the lights cluster where the raking light hits and thin out into shadow. They are never spread evenly ("spotting the foreground all over" was a contemporary complaint against Constable [3]).

## 5. Trees in storm (short note)
- Trees lean, broken or blasted, with foliage swept to one side (Cole's Oxbow tree, Church's blasted tree, Leypold's bending trees, Cox's tossed trees). Church's blasted tree embodies "nature's awe-inspiring power and endless cycle of life and death" [7].
- Their silhouettes are thrown against the light of the break or the lit distance. Leonardo's branch-thickness rule and layer 1's sky holes still hold.

## 6. What stays true to life
- The grass's lean matches the wind shown by the clouds, rain and sea.
- Blades taper and are thinner than they look (layer 1). Tufts grow from the ground with dark bases.
- Texture shrinks with distance: blades near, short strokes in the middle ground, flat tone far off.
- Lights face the light source; grass in the crest's shadow gets almost none.

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. Every grass stroke leans the same way as the rain and cloud streaks.
2. The grass bank reads as one dark mass in the squint view, with lights only on the tips facing the break.
3. Single blades are drawn only at the crest edge against light and in the nearest tufts; elsewhere the grass is massed.
4. The colour is ochre, straw, russet or olive, warm in the light and cool-dark between tufts, with no saturated green.
5. The lights are short strokes along the lean, clustered where the light rakes, not an even scatter of dots.

## 8. Engine notes and method
- **Oil:** the dark mass with a `flat` following the slope; mid-tones with a worn `round` or `fan` flicked downwind; lights last with a small loaded `round` or `liner` along the lean; a few `knife` touches for the brightest tips **[J]**. Watercolour pole: reserve or leave the lightest tips, since v8 has no scraping or lifting tool.
- **Intentions:** `match` with a medium brush for the mass, then accent strokes in the wind direction. Check direction with the stroke log, not by eye **[J]**.
- **Method:** colours are median sRGB of hand-placed regions on the museums' images (aged surfaces, so approximate). Lean angles were judged by eye, not measured.
