# Romanticism: space (layer 2)

How Romantic painting (c. 1790–1850) builds pictorial space, as a departure from the layer-1 baseline in [`docs/subjects/space.md`](../../subjects/space.md). Read the style doc [`docs/styles/romanticism.md`](../romanticism.md) first. Researched 2026-11-26 for the case of a low viewpoint, a low horizon, the sky three-quarters of the canvas, a large off-centre figure, a cropped edge and aerial perspective. **[J]** marks a judgement call; **[M]** marks a value read off the public-domain images in section 2.

## 1. The twist in one paragraph
Layer-1 perspective stays correct but is no longer the point. Romantic space is built from **the sky and a few horizontal value bands of land** rather than from vanishing points. The sky takes about half the canvas or more; for Constable it was "the keynote, the standard of scale, and the chief organ of sentiment" [2]. Picture-making splits into two branches. The landscape branch shows people tiny against vast nature: "dwarfed by the overwhelming scale of the landscape" [3]. The figure branch (Géricault, Delacroix) brings a few large figures right up to the picture plane against a stormy sky. Both are asymmetric. One heavy, dark mass is cut by a side edge, and the other side opens into the distance. Burke's sublime of vastness and "infinity", where "the eye not being able to perceive the bounds", is the aim [4].

## 3. What it stretches and flattens
- **The sky is enlarged** relative to the High Renaissance and classical view **[J]**: about half the height or more in every true-horizon work (0.48 at least) [M], more where the viewpoint is low.
- **Distance is stretched by stacking bands.** The land is three to five horizontal bands of alternating value (lit, shadowed, lit, then the far blue or dark band) rather than a ground grid [M: Michel, Hampstead, Hallingdal, Martin]. The bands thin toward the horizon as layer 1's 1/D² compression predicts.
- **The diagonal sweep.** Roads, rivers and slopes swing in curves or diagonals into depth instead of running along orthogonals: "curving arcs of slopes and plains swirl around the composition" (Martin) [9]. Also the Oxbow's river and Michel's road [M].
- **Vertical contrast of scale.** "The minute with the infinite": a close hillside and sheep set against the distant mountains [13]. Burke: "the last extreme of littleness is in some measure sublime likewise" [4].
- **Elevated viewpoint for the panorama.** 5 of the 12 true-horizon works look down from a height, including every wide panorama (and Martin's, unmeasured) [M]. Burke thought "height is less grand than depth; and that we are more struck at looking down from a precipice", while adding "I am not very positive" [4].

## 4. Conventions
- **One cropped, heavy side.** In 11 of 14 landscapes a dark mass (tree, hill, cliff, building) is cut by one side edge while the other side opens to the distance [M: Oxbow, Branch Hill, Michel, Mill, Moel Cynwich, Burning, Schroon, Flers, Hallingdal, Feige, Hampstead]. This is asymmetric, unlike the paired classical wings (Salisbury's tree arch is the exception [10]). The Oxbow sets storm and wilderness on the left against sunlit farms on the right [5].
- **Emptiness as the other pole.** A low horizon running edge to edge with no foreground device (style doc §2, after Friedrich's *Monk by the Sea*). It is not in the sample, but it is in the style doc's research.
- **Figure scale has two settings.** Landscape branch: figures at about 2–8 % of the picture height, placed in the light or on a path (Michel, Branch Hill, Martin, Hampstead, Flers) [M]. Figure branch: figures filling about three-quarters of the height, close to the picture plane, the group off-centre, heads against the sky (Géricault) [M]. The German contemplative pairs sit in between, at about 15–25 % of the height and seen from behind (Friedrich, Dahl) [M].
- **Figures from behind** draw the viewer in (Friedrich, Dahl [11][12]). Teodor's own habit warning applies (style doc §11).
- **Aerial perspective kept in the darks.** Near-to-far darks lift toward the horizon sky (Oxbow L* 8 → 11 → 20; Branch Hill 2 → 7 → 14) and the far land cools (Moel Cynwich b* +10 → +3 → −11; Hallingdal +9 → +2 → −1) [M]. The light pattern can still put the highest contrast in the middle distance (see light-shadow §3).
- **Little ruled perspective.** Buildings are few and far, and vanishing points are seldom visible. Depth comes from overlap, size, height in the field and air (layer 1's Cutting and Vishton ranking for vista space) **[J]**.

## 5. Colour, edge and mark
- Far bands: cool, low-contrast, lost-edged, laid as thin horizontal strokes or washes [M: Hampstead, Moel Cynwich]. Middle: the lit band in thicker paint. Foreground: dark, broad, loosely brushed, with a few crisp accents **[J]**.
- The cropped dark side mass is the darkest value and the loosest edge against the frame. Its inner contour against the light is the crispest edge on that side [M: Oxbow tree, Mill buildings].
- Skies are painted in sweeps following the cloud structure, and their brushwork is the most energetic in the picture (style doc §8) **[J]**.

## 6. What stays true to life
- One horizon at eye level; figures on flat ground keyed to it (layer 1 §1). In Géricault the horizon cuts the standing figures at about knee height, which puts the viewer's eye low [M].
- Size diminishes with distance, ground intervals compress, and overlap is clear.
- The aerial perspective law (Koschmieder) for darks and saturation.

## 7. For the low-viewpoint case (sky about three-quarters, large off-centre figure, cropped edge)
- **Flag, not conflict.** A horizon at 0.75 is beyond every measured work but within reach of the current: Constable's *Mill* reaches 0.71 and Géricault about 0.72 [M], and the style doc's *Monk by the Sea* is lower still [style doc, 5]. The typical Romantic landscape sits nearer 0.55. Keep 0.70–0.75 as a deliberate choice and state it in the plan **[J]**.
- **The low viewpoint belongs to the figure branch.** With the eye at about knee to hip height of the figure, the figure rises well above the horizon and its head and torso are seen against the sky [M: Géricault]. The large figure follows the figure branch: about 50–75 % of the height, off-centre (its centre about a third in from one side), close to the picture plane **[J]** from Géricault.
- **The cropped edge:** let the post, the line or a sheet run out of the frame on the side opposite the open distance. That side is the heavy, dark, cut side; the other side opens to the low distance and the light [M convention].
- **Aerial perspective:** the strip of land under the low horizon is only a few bands. The far band is cool and lifted (or dark under cloud shadow), and the near ground is dark. Give each band one value **[J]**.
- **Avoid** the elevated panorama formula here (the brief asks for the opposite), centring the figure, and small pastiche figures.

## 8. Avoid
- A centred horizon with symmetrical wings (the classical Claudean frame).
- A ruled perspective grid as the main depth cue.
- In this case, a mid-sized figure that is neither dominant nor part of the land **[J]**.
- Blue haze on the near ground.
- A sky under half the height in a storm picture.
- The lone figure from behind as a default (Teodor's habit).
