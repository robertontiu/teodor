# Romanticism: sky (layer 2)

How Romantic landscape painting (Britain, France, Germany, Scandinavia, America, c. 1790–1850) renders the sky, written as a departure from layer 1 (`docs/subjects/sky.md`), with the squall front in mind: a slate cloud mass, a cold silver break low under the cloud's edge, rain veils, cloud streaming across. Style research: `docs/styles/romanticism.md`.

Researched 2026-11-26. **[J]** marks a judgement call. "Measured" values come from the museums' open-access images (method in section 8); old varnish warms the oils, so their hues are warmer than painted.

## 2. The twist in one paragraph
The sky becomes the subject and the carrier of feeling. Constable called it landscape's "chief organ of sentiment", and his stormy pictures "feature dark, turbulent skies that carry the brunt of the works' emotional weight" [1]. In Cox's teaching, a sublime effect comes from "clouds rolling their shadowy forms in broad masses over the scene", with tones "deep and impressive" [2]. Burke had already ranked the cloudy sky as "more grand than the blue" [3]. Against layer 1, the current enlarges the sky's share of the picture, darkens and simplifies the cloud mass, concentrates the light into one break, and lets the paint marks show the direction of the weather. The physics of layer 1 still decides the logic: one wind, cloud bases on a common level, light from one side.

## 3. Departures from layer 1

**Proportion and placement (stretched):**
- **The sky takes most of the picture.** Measured horizon height in twelve storm, coast and open-country pictures from seven artists: the horizon sits 0.51 to 0.83 of the way down (median about 0.57), so the sky fills 50–80% of the height. The Oxbow (0.53), Branch Hill Pond (0.52), Harrow (0.58), Journey Home (0.63), Cotman (0.74) and Constable's cloud study (0.83) all fit. A high horizon is the exception, found in close studies of the sea (Delacroix's *Sea at Dieppe*, 0.38).
- **One cloud mass, not many clouds.** The storm is a single dark body covering one side or the top third, its lower edge running roughly level or in one long diagonal (Harrow, Michel, Cotman, Dahl, Cox, the Oxbow). Layer 1's scattered cumulus field is replaced by Cox's "broad masses" [2]. Small clouds appear only as torn fragments streaming off the main body.
- **The diagonal of the weather.** Cloud bands, rain veils and torn edges all lean one way (Hadleigh Castle, the Oxbow, Wanderer in the Storm). Cox teaches that "abrupt and irregular lines are productive of a grand or stormy Effect", while "even and horizontal lines" give serenity [2].

**Value (compressed, then pushed):**
- **Dark top, light band low.** This reverses layer 1's clear-sky logic only in the sense that the zenith is darkest and the lightest sky lies just above the horizon, in the break under the cloud's edge. Measured: in Harrow the top fifth of the sky averages L* 33 and the brightest band low in the sky L* 63; in Dahl's sea picture the figures are 23 and 47. The break is 25–30 L* lighter than the mass above it **(measured, two works; treat as an order of magnitude)**.
- **The land is darker than any sky.** In the oils, the brightest sky band is 22–38 L* lighter than the median of the land (Harrow 38, Branch Hill 36, Dahl 34, Michel 33, the Oxbow 23, Hadleigh 22). Layer 1's caution against over-darkening clouds (Ruskin) is kept in a sense: the cloud mass stays lighter than the dark land under it **(measured)**.
- **Mid-greys, not black.** The darkest cloud masses in the oils measure about #544c46 (Harrow), #544840 (Branch Hill), #564e41 (Oxbow) and #595b5c (Michel), dark but well above black. The style doc's "avoid pure black skies" is confirmed.

**Simplified or left out:**
- Clear-sky gradient detail (layer 1's four starting colours) mostly disappears under the overcast. Blue shows only in small rifts, high up or behind the storm (Branch Hill, the Oxbow, Cotman).
- Cloud genera are not catalogued. The storm reads as one nimbostratus or cumulonimbus mass with ragged low cloud (fractus) under it, which matches the WMO description of a dark base with ragged cloud beneath (layer 1) **[J]**.
- The sun's disc is rarely shown in a storm. The light is a band or a glow, not a source.

**Conventions:**
- **The break.** The one bright opening, low in the sky or under the cloud's edge, is the light centre of the whole picture (Harrow, Dahl, Michel, Barber, Hadleigh). Cox places the principal light on the leading feature [2], and in a storm piece the sky's break is that feature **[J]**.
- **Rain veils as streaks.** Rain falling from the cloud base is painted as parallel streaks, usually leaning with the wind. Constable drags paler paint down through the dark (Harrow), Cole lays a soft grey veil between the cloud and the hills (Oxbow, measured #76695f against the cloud's #564e41), and Lucas's mezzotint rules straight diagonal lines (Hadleigh). The veils are lighter than the cloud mass where they cross the break, and darker where they cross a lit sky **[J]**, consistent with layer 1's note that rain shows against a contrasting ground.
- **Light falls on the land in patches.** "Fleeting lights of the sunbeams, struggling, between the interstices of the blowing clouds" is Cox's prescription for a flat country "beneath a grey, clouded sky" [2]. The sky and the land are planned together (see `romanticism--landscape.md`).

## 4. Colour
- **Low-chroma greys, warmed or cooled by the medium.** The oils read brown-grey (partly from old varnish). The unvarnished watercolours read neutral to blue-grey: Cotman's slate sky measures about #8e8d87 with its darkest passages #677278, and Constable's cloud study #b6bab9 with darks near #99a2a2 **(measured)**.
- **A slate-indigo cloud should be a greyed indigo.** Mix the blue with an earth (Prussian or ultramarine with burnt sienna, umber or red earth), as the style doc's storm greys `#5c6470` and `#8d8f8c` already do. A saturated indigo would contradict every work measured here. *Flag:* where a brief asks for "slate-indigo", follow the research and keep its chroma low.
- **The break is cool silver or warm cream, never white.** Harrow's break measures #b2ac9e, Dahl's lit cloud edges are pinkish cream, and in Cotman and Cox it is the paper itself, slightly toned. A cold silver break fits the watercolour and Northern pole; a warm one fits evening or the oil pole **[J]**.
- **Warm-cool polarity.** Cox: "the opposition or proximity of a cool to a warm colour assists greatly in giving brilliancy to the lights", and "if the picture … should have a cool sky, the landscape ought to be principally composed of warm tints" [2]. A cool storm sky over warm ochre and russet land appears throughout the sample.

## 5. Edges and marks
- **Lost edges in the mass, a few torn hard edges at the break.** The dark body melts into itself, wet-in-wet or scumbled. The edge where it meets the break is broken and ragged, and it is the sharpest edge in the sky (Harrow, Dahl, Cole's *Clouds*, Cox's *Study of clouds*).
- **Visible direction.** Strokes follow the wind: long horizontal drags for streaming cloud (Constable's cloud study, Dahl), dragged verticals for rain. Constable's oil sketches were "painted rapidly, wet-in-wet … with short strokes and a restricted color palette" [1].
- **Reductive lights in watercolour.** Cotman's storm sheet lists "scratching out, stopping out, and gum arabic" [4]; Cox's *Journey Home* and Martin's downs list "reductive techniques" [5]. The break and the lit cloud rims are lifted or left as paper, not painted on.
- **Ground colour sets the mood.** Constable used "a brown ground for a stormy day" and a pink one for a sunny evening [6], so in oil a warm-dark ground shows through the thin storm greys.
- **Impasto only on the lights.** Thick light paint sits on the lit cloud rims and the break, with "small smatterings of white paint" (the flecks critics called "Constable's snow") [7]. The darks stay thin **[J]** (consistent with the style doc's "selective impasto").

## 6. What stays true to life
- One wind: every cloud fragment, rain veil and torn edge leans the same way.
- Cloud bases recede on one level and crowd toward the horizon (layer 1). Distant rain is a narrower, paler veil.
- Rain falls from the base of the dark cloud, never from the break.
- Light on the land comes from the break's side, and the land's lit patches lie where the break would let light through.
- Aerial perspective at the horizon: the farthest land and sea under the break are bluish and pale or dark-banded, never sharp.

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. The horizon is in the lower half (about 0.5–0.75 of the height down), and the sky gets at least half the picture.
2. The squint view shows one dark mass, one bright break low under its edge, and land darker than the break (break about 25–35 L* above the land median).
3. The cloud's darkest value is a coloured mid-dark (about L* 25–40), not black. Its colour is a low-chroma grey with a cool or warm bias, never a saturated blue.
4. Rain veils are parallel streaks hanging from the cloud base, leaning with the wind, lighter across the break.
5. The hardest edges in the sky are the torn edge of the mass against the break and the lit rims; the body of the mass has no outlines.
6. Strokes in the sky run with the weather: long drags for streaming cloud, verticals for rain. No uniform dabbing.

## 8. Engine notes and method
- **Oil:** large `flat` and `round` for the mass; `blend` and `smudge` wet-in-wet inside it; `knife` or thick light `round` only on the break and rims; rain as long `flat` drags in a lighter, slightly warmer grey over the dry mass **[J]**. Watercolour pole: a wet-in-wet mass, with the break reserved, since v8 has no lifting tool (see the style doc's gaps).
- **Views:** `look squint` and `look notan 2` to confirm the single break; `look temp` for the cool sky over warm land.
- **Method:** horizon heights were read by eye on the museum images (±0.03). Values are CIE L* computed from the sRGB images after trimming 6% on all sides; "brightest band" is the brightest row mean in the lower 40% of the sky; "land" is the median below the horizon. Colours are median sRGB of hand-placed regions. The images are museum photographs of aged surfaces, so treat all numbers as approximate (**measured, approximate**).
