# Romanticism: objects (layer 2)

How Romantic painting (c. 1790–1850) renders everyday objects inside a sublime or stormy scene, as a departure from the layer-1 baseline in [`docs/subjects/objects.md`](../../subjects/objects.md). Read the style doc [`docs/styles/romanticism.md`](../romanticism.md) first. Researched 2026-11-26 for the case of a clothesline, a weathered post, wooden pegs and a basket. **[J]** marks a judgement call; **[M]** marks something read directly off the public-domain images below.

## 1. The twist in one paragraph
Layer 1 renders an object for its material: gloss, transmission, an inventory of texture. Romantic painting keeps objects **small, used, weathered and summary**. They are traces of ordinary life set against the vastness, and they make the scene real and give it scale. The Cleveland text on a Turner Alpine watercolour says it directly: "the inclusion of figures going about their daily lives within the vastness of the Alps is a reminder of the reality of the scene" [2]; another sheet "invites a comparison of the minute with the infinite" [3]. Objects take the picturesque character, "roughness, and … sudden variation, joined to that of irregularity" [4]. Constable loved "old rotten planks, slimy posts, and brickwork" [5]. Objects are painted in a few loaded strokes, kept "at a picturesque distance" [6], and lit only where the light falls.

## 3. What it stretches, simplifies and leaves out
- **Scale is reduced.** Objects are small against land and sky, and close ones are placed low and cropped [M: Branch Hill, Oxbow, Flers]. Burke: "the last extreme of littleness is in some measure sublime likewise" [13].
- **Detail is summarised.** A cart is a dark mass, a wheel ellipse and a few light touches; a basket is a textured lump with one light side; laundry is a flat bright stroke [M: Branch Hill, Flers]. Material cues (layer 1's highlights, Fresnel, gloss) shrink to one or two touches of light on the lit side **[J]** from the corpus.
- **Wear is stressed.** Weathered, irregular, mended or old: Price's "old, mossy, rough-hewn park pales of unequal heights are an ornament", while a neat post-and-rail fence is "one of the most unpicturesque" boundaries; "an old mill has the greatest charm for a painter" [4]. Constable's slimy posts and rotten planks [5].
- **What is left out:** new, polished, manufactured or symmetrical things; still-life display and arrangement; reflections of surroundings in glossy surfaces; legible fine detail at a distance **[J]**.

## 4. Conventions
- **Objects bear the weather.** Mill sails, trees, smoke, laundry and boats agree with the wind and are wet or lit by the same storm [M: Mill, Oxbow umbrella, Flers]. An object is evidence that the weather is real **[J]**.
- **Upright against the sky.** Posts, masts, mill sails and blasted trees are often silhouetted against the sky [M: Mill, Dahl etching, Oxbow tree]. Burke: "A perpendicular has more force in forming the sublime, than an inclined plane; and the effects of a rugged and broken surface seem stronger than where it is smooth and polished" [13].
- **The brightest small accents.** White cloth, a white horse, a white wall or a scratched-out light on an object are often the brightest notes on the land, placed in the light [M: Branch Hill, Flers, Oxbow, Mill]. They stay below the sky's lights (see `romanticism--light-shadow.md`).
- **Use is visible.** Objects are in use or just left: a cart being loaded, linen bleaching, an easel abandoned mid-sketch, fallen arms [M]. Not a still-life arrangement **[J]**.
- **Small warm or red accents.** A few touches of red on harness, cloth or clothing in the earth-and-grey land [M: Branch Hill, Flers, Mill's red sail].

## 5. Colour, edge and mark
- **Colour:** earths and greys for wood, wicker and rope, close to the land's colour; weathered wood goes grey and silvery on the lit side and dark brown-olive in shadow **[J]**. White cloth goes cold in cold light and grey-brown in shadow.
- **Edges:** an object's silhouette against the sky is crisp on the lit side and lost where it joins dark ground [M: Mill, Oxbow].
- **Marks:** one stroke per plane; dragged, broken paint for rough wood and wicker; a knife or impasto touch, or a scratched line in watercolour, for the single light on the object [M: Mill's scratching; style doc §8]. No outlines.

## 6. What stays true to life
- Construction and proportion: wheel ellipses, the post's taper and lean, the line's sag between supports (a catenary, layer 1 and drapery's diaper), pegs at regular use spacing, a basket's ellipse on its axis [M: the Branch Hill wheel is a correct ellipse even when summary].
- Scale against the figure (layer 1 sizes): a peg about a hand's width, a laundry basket about 50–70 cm across, a clothes post about 2 m **[J]** (no Romantic source; real sizes).
- Contact shadows, and the object sitting on the ground.

## 7. For the clothesline case
- **The post:** weathered, irregular, leaning slightly, grey-silver on the lit face, dark on the shadow side. It is the one strong vertical against the sky, crisp at the top and lost at the base in dark grass [4][13][M].
- **The line:** a thin, slightly sagging curve, pulled down toward the sheets' weight and kept continuous. It is a single fine stroke, lost where it crosses dark and crisp against the light sky **[J]**.
- **The pegs:** a few dark notches on the line, no more than one light touch each. Do not inventory them **[J]** from the summary rule.
- **The basket:** a low, rough lump of wicker texture with one lit side and a contact shadow. Its ellipse is correct, it is set low and possibly cropped, and it holds a pale mass of linen as a light accent [M: Flers's pannier and sack].
- Everything agrees with the gale and the single light.

## 8. Avoid
- New or neat things (a straight, painted post; uniform pegs).
- Still-life gloss and reflections.
- Detail legible beyond what the light picks out.
- Outlines.
- Objects that ignore the wind.
- Objects brighter than the sky.
- An object as large or as finished as the figure, unless it is the subject.
