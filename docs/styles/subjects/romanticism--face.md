# Romanticism: the face (layer 2)

How Romanticism departs from the layer-1 head and face ([docs/subjects/face.md](../../subjects/face.md)). Read with the style doc ([docs/styles/romanticism.md](../romanticism.md)) and [romanticism--figure.md](romanticism--figure.md). Researched 2026-11-26. **[J]** marks a judgement call. Works are examples of what several artists share; no rule rests on one artist.

## 1. The twist in one paragraph
The neoclassical face is calm whatever happens to the body: "a great soul lies sedate beneath the strife of passions in Greek figures", with "noble simplicity and sedate grandeur in gesture and expression" (Winckelmann, quoted in [2]). Romanticism lets the passion reach the face and makes the face particular. "Traditionally, records of individual likeness, portraits became vehicles for expressing a range of psychological" and emotional states [1]; Géricault "probed the extremes of mental illness in his portraits of psychiatric patients" [1]; the *Raft* runs a "spectrum of emotion, from madness to despair" to "an expression of hope" [3]. The face is individual, weathered if the life was hard, often lifted toward the light, half in shadow, and painted in a few big touches rather than finished feature by feature.

## 3. How it departs from layer 1
**Proportions.** True to life (layer 1 sections 1 and 9). The current does not stretch the face; it individualises it: different noses, jaws and brows in one picture, against the neoclassical tendency toward one ideal profile (the "perfect classical profile" is kept only for allegory, as in Liberty [6]) **[J]** for the general claim, read across the works above.

**The lifted head.** A shared convention: the head tipped back, the face turned up toward the sky or the light (the *Raft* study's upturned head, Fuseli's hag, Goya's distressed woman, Rebecca, the hope figures of the *Raft* [3]; Friedrich's woman turned to the evening sky, CMA [1995.68](https://clevelandart.org/art/1995.68)). It reads as yearning, ecstasy, terror or hope depending on the rest of the face and body.

**What it simplifies and leaves out.** Below half-length scale the face is three to five value shapes: the lit brow and cheek, the dark socket, the dark mouth, the shadow under the jaw (seen on *Christ Asleep* and Goya's album). Features are not outlined: the eye is the socket's shadow with at most one light touch, the mouth a dark accent. Wrinkles are given only where the light rakes them **[J]**, observation. Polished finish is left out: "freshness of the initial sketch" over conventional finish [4].

**Expression.** Stronger than neoclassical, never mask-like: the open mouth is allowed (Fuseli, the *Raft*, *Christ Asleep*) where Winckelmann's ideal kept it closed or barely parted [2]. Expression is carried by the whole head (tilt, throw-back, the neck's strain) as much as by the features **[J]**.

**Laughter.** Romantic theory and imagery separate *joy* from *laughter*. Baudelaire (1855): "Laughter is satanic: it is thus profoundly human", "the consequence in man of the idea of his own superiority" [7]; but "La joie est une", while "Le rire est l'expression d'un sentiment double, ou contradictoire", and a child's laugh "est comme un épanouissement de fleur. C'est une joie de plante" [8]. The laughing faces of the period's prints are mostly satirical or mocking (Goya's *Caprichos*, not viewed this round; Blake, *The Just Upright Man is Laughed to Scorn*, 1825, CMA [1968.260.k](https://clevelandart.org/art/1968.260.k), title only, image not viewed **[J]** for the generalisation). No Met or Cleveland work checked shows a single figure laughing with joy in a storm.

**Light, colour, edges, marks.** Dramatic side light out of a dark ground, against David's "clear, even light" [3]. Lit planes in thicker, warm, visible strokes; shadow planes thin, cool, taking the surround's colour, merging with the hair and the ground at a lost edge [5]. A crude sample of two Delacroix figure crops (*Christ Asleep*, *Rebecca*, full-size Met images, flesh-coloured pixels only, so greenish shadows are under-counted) gave lit flesh about L* 34-37 with chroma about 22 (hue 60-69°, warm orange) and the darkest quartile about L* 8-10 with chroma about 10: shadows lose about half their colour and go very dark.

**What stays true to life.** The action units (layer 1 section 5): Romantic painters drew from models and recorded real expression [3]; the laugh-versus-scream table still decides what the face says.

## 4. For this brief (three-quarter, tipped back, a real laugh, weathered, low raking light)
- The tipped-back head is the Romantic lifted face. Keep the throw moderate and the view three-quarter, so the lifted cheeks (AU6), the narrowed eyes and the up-and-back mouth corners (AU12) stay visible; layer 1 warns that a head thrown back from below loses the face, as Rebecca's does (there the loss is the point: she has fainted).
- Paint **joy, not the satanic laugh**: the whole face opened and lifted into the light, eyes crinkled, upper teeth showing, no sneer, no one-sided mouth, no caricature grin [7][8]. Layer 1's laugh column (lifted cheeks, crow's feet, upturned crescent, deep nasolabial fold) is the check.
- Low raking light is Romantic light: let it pick out the brow, cheekbone, nose and the weathering (crow's feet, the nasolabial fold, forehead lines) on the lit side in a few warm, thick strokes; keep the shadow side one simple cool shape that merges with the hair.
- Weathered and individual: a broad, sun-browned face of about forty with its own nose and jaw; no smoothed ideal, no classical profile.

## 5. Conflict flagged
The joyful laugh is outside the current's usual faces (awe, terror, longing, despair, hope; laughter mostly mocking). There is no shared Romantic model to copy, so: keep the true Duchenne laugh from layer 1 (research, measured) and render it with the current's means (lifted head, side light, few big touches, lost shadow edges). Do not borrow the period's satirical laughing faces, which would read as mockery [7] **[J]**.

## 6. Checks for Teodor
1. Squint: three to five value shapes describe the head before any feature; the light side is one shape, the shadow side one shape.
2. Is the laugh readable from the upper face alone (lifted cheeks, narrowed eyes) as well as the mouth?
3. No outlined features; eyes are sockets with at most one light.
4. Lit planes warm, thick and stroked; shadow planes thin, cool, lost into hair or ground.
5. The face is particular (its own nose, jaw, weathering), not an ideal.
6. Nothing in the mouth reads as a sneer or grimace.
