# Romanticism: landscape (layer 2)

How Romantic landscape painting (c. 1790–1850) renders terrain, written as a departure from layer 1 (`docs/subjects/landscape.md`), with this case in mind: a rounded grassy hill crest in the foreground cutting across the bottom, and a low coast beyond. The sky is in `romanticism--sky.md`, distant sea in `romanticism--water.md`, and grass in `romanticism--trees-plants.md`. Style research: `docs/styles/romanticism.md`.

Researched 2026-11-26. **[J]** marks a judgement call. "Measured" values come from the museums' open-access images (method in section 8).

## 2. The twist in one paragraph
Layer 1 orders the land by Carlson's planes: flat ground second-lightest, slopes third, uprights darkest, with steady aerial recession. Romantic storm landscape keeps the recession but lets the weather override the plane values. The foreground is thrown into shadow as a dark base, a patch of the middle distance is lit by the break, and the far land or coast becomes a low, simple band. Hills are reduced to a few broad, smooth curves. Cox: in sketching a mountain "it will be sufficient to describe the extreme Outline, without descending to the diversified and numerous ridges", while the foreground must show "decided forms" [1]. The land is a dark, warm stage under the sky, not a survey.

## 3. Departures from layer 1

**Proportion and shape (flattened and smoothed):**
- **The land is the lower third to half of the picture,** often less (see the horizon heights in `romanticism--sky.md`: median 0.57 down).
- **Rounded crests as long simple curves.** A foreground crest or knoll crosses the bottom of the picture as one or two long convex arcs with few breaks (Martin's South Downs; Dahl's Hallingdal knoll; Michel's hill; Dahl's shore). Martin's Tyne shows "curving arcs of slopes and plains" that "swirl around the composition" [2]. Layer 1's "retirement and succession of masses" survives, but as fewer, larger masses **[J]**, from the sample.
- **The crest is often tilted.** In the sample it rises or falls diagonally across the frame more often than it runs level (South Downs, Michel, Harrow's slopes), which carries Cox's "abrupt and irregular lines" of a stormy effect into the land [1] **[J]**.
- **The far land is a low band.** A distant coast or headland is a thin, nearly level silhouette at the horizon with little modelling (Girtin's Tynemouth, Harrow's distant ridge, Constable's cloud study).

**Value (weather over planes):**
- **Dark foreground.** The bottom 15% of the picture is the darkest band in the storm oils: measured median L* 11–25 (Dahl 11, Branch Hill 12, Michel 19, Harrow 23, the Oxbow and Hadleigh 25) **(measured)**. Layer 1's flat-ground-second-lightest is overridden by cloud shadow, which layer 1 itself names as the tool for alternating bands in depth.
- **One lit patch in the middle ground.** A field or slope struck by the break is the brightest land (Michel's sunlit field, Harrow's lit flecks, Cox's lit field, Cole's sunlit plain). Measured, the land's 90th percentile is 37–54 L* in the oils against medians of 12–30 **(measured)**. Cox describes the effect as "fleeting lights of the sunbeams, struggling, between the interstices of the blowing clouds" [1].
- **Watercolour runs lighter overall but keeps the pattern** (Martin's downs land median L* 50, Cox's 45), with a darker near bank or shadowed slope against a lit one.

**Simplified or left out:**
- Small-scale relief on distant ground: left out. Ridges, gullies and field boundaries vanish beyond the middle ground, as Cox instructs [1].
- Geological detail on soft hills: none. Rounded grassy crests are modelled only by broad light and shadow and the direction of the strokes.
- Linear field patterns: few. Where a path or track exists it curves with the ground and leads into the distance (Michel, Cox, Leypold, Martin).

**Conventions:**
- **Repoussoir dark.** A dark foreground band or bank at the bottom edge pushes the lit distance back (all the oils, Cox's bank). With a crest cutting across the bottom, the crest is that dark band, and it is lit only at its top edge or in a raking stripe if the break is low **[J]**.
- **The edge of the crest against the distance** is a clear, slightly broken silhouette (grass breaks it, see `romanticism--trees-plants.md`). It is the strongest edge in the lower half.
- **Small figures and animals for scale,** placed on the far side of the crest or in the lit patch (Michel, Cox, Martin) **[J]**. The style doc warns about Teodor's lone-figure habit: use them only if the brief asks.

## 4. Colour
- **Warm earths for the land under a cool sky.** "If the picture … should have a cool sky, the landscape ought to be principally composed of warm tints" (Cox [1]). Measured land colours: Martin's heath about #9b7a5d, Cox's lit field #a98466 and shadowed bank #7b6752, Michel's foreground shadow #35291c, and Branch Hill's foreground #251e16 (oils warmed by varnish) **(measured, approximate)**.
- **Greens are olive and broken,** never saturated. Michel's lit grass measures about #37352b; even Cole's green woodland is held dark. This agrees with the style doc's "avoid saturated synthetic greens".
- **Lights are warm.** "All the lights in a picture should be composed of warm tints" (Cox [1]). The lit patch is ochre or gold, and the shadowed land is brown or olive with a cooler grey in the distance.
- **The far coast is grey-blue,** cooler and lighter than the foreground but not light: Harrow's far land band measures about #5a6061 **(measured)**.

## 5. Edges and marks
- Foreground: thicker, broken, directional strokes that follow the slope (Cox and Martin drag and stipple; Constable jabs and scrapes [3]). Cox asks for drawing "strongly marked in the shade and fore-ground … but more delicately in the lighter parts, and as the distance gradually increases" [1].
- Middle-ground lit patch: a few warm, opaque touches over a darker underlayer; in watercolour, lifted or reserved light (Martin's sheet: "reductive techniques" [4]).
- Distance: flat, thin, horizontal washes or scumbles with lost edges, except where the coast meets the sea, which is crisp.

## 6. What stays true to life
- Aerial perspective: each band back is lower in contrast and cooler (layer 1), even though the near band is dark.
- Cloud shadows follow the terrain and fall where the cloud is. The lit patch must lie where the break would let light through.
- A path or track follows the contours and narrows into the distance.
- Texture size shrinks steadily with distance; there is no foreground texture on the far coast.
- A grassy crest is convex: its top edge catches the most sky light, and its face toward the viewer turns away into shadow if the light is behind or above it **[J]** (form logic of `light-shadow.md`).

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. The foreground crest is one or two long, smooth arcs across the bottom, with no lumpy repeated bumps.
2. In the squint view the foreground is the darkest band, the brightest land is one patch or stripe lit by the break, and the far coast is a low grey band.
3. The land is warm (ochre, russet, olive, brown) under the cool sky, with no saturated green.
4. Detail and thick, directional strokes are in the foreground only; the distance is flat and thin.
5. The crest's silhouette is the strongest edge in the lower half, broken by grass, not ruled.
6. The light on the land agrees with the sky's break in position and in its warm or cool tint.

## 8. Engine notes and method
- **Oil:** after the sky, block the land in a dark warm mix with a large `flat`, following the curve of the crest. Lay the lit patch as thick ochre `round` touches over it. Drag the far coast as a thin cool band with a `flat` and `blend` it into the sea's edge, keeping the coastline crisp **[J]**.
- **Schema:** a `scene` blockout or SVG schema should fix the crest's curve, the horizon and the position of the lit patch together with the sky's break.
- **Method:** foreground value is the median L* of the bottom 15% of each image (after a 6% trim); land median and 90th percentile are taken below the estimated horizon. Colours are median sRGB of hand-placed regions. The numbers come from photographs of aged surfaces and are approximate.
