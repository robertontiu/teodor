# Romanticism: drapery (layer 2)

How Romantic painting (Britain, France, Germany and their followers, c. 1790–1850) renders cloth, as a departure from the layer-1 baseline in [`docs/subjects/drapery.md`](../../subjects/drapery.md). Read the style doc [`docs/styles/romanticism.md`](../romanticism.md) first. Researched 2026-11-26 for the case of heavy wool clothing and big wet linen sheets in a gale. **[J]** marks a judgement call; **[M]** marks something measured or read directly off the public-domain images listed below.

## 1. The twist in one paragraph
In Romantic pictures cloth carries the weather and the feeling: it is one of the things the storm acts on, so it shows the wind's direction and force more than the fabric's construction. Folds are fewer and bigger than in life. Each cloth mass is a broad shape of colour and value that the brush states in a few loaded strokes rather than models fold by fold. A critic in 1832 said this painting "wilfully sacrifices the rigours of drawing to the demands of the drama" and puts "the brilliance of colour over the purity of line" [2]; a modern introduction sets this "expressive, modern painterly style focused on color" against the "primacy of line" [14]. Gravity and the support points of layer 1 still hold. Detail, pattern and small wrinkles go first. Cloth is the strongest local colour in an earth-and-storm picture, but it is deep colour, not pretty colour [3].

## 3. What it stretches, flattens and simplifies
- **Fewer, bigger folds.** One or two big fold systems per cloth mass. The secondary folds of layer 1 shrink to a stroke or vanish, and small wrinkles are left out [M: Rebecca, Christ Asleep, Martin]. Layer 1 already warns against "a great confusion of many folds"; Romantic painting goes further.
- **The belly of wind-filled cloth is exaggerated.** Cloth caught by wind becomes a convex pocket or sail with a lit belly and a dark hollow; the silhouette, not internal folds, carries it [M: Christ Asleep, Faust]. Billowing cloth rises above the heads in Delacroix's storm boat, larger than the wind on the water would strictly lift **[J]**.
- **Flag folds are stretched taut and long.** The free edge streams downwind as one long tapering shape held tight from its anchor [M: Faust cape, Martin shawl], instead of the many small flutters layer 1 describes for free edges.
- **Fabric identity is simplified.** Heavy wool (Géricault) and silk (Rebecca) differ in fold size and weight, but stitching, weave and pattern are left out. Stripes or patterns, where they exist, become a few broken marks [M: Rebecca's striped cloth].
- **Small scale means a stroke.** At landscape distance a figure's clothing is two or three strokes: one light shape and one dark shape streaming the same way [M: Martin; Michel's and Constable's distant figures].

## 4. Conventions
- **Cloth agrees with the storm.** Cloth, hair, smoke, grass, branches and spray all lean on one diagonal [M: Faust, where everything is hatched on the same slant; Christ Asleep]. This is layer 1's rule, raised to the main device.
- **Saturated cloth against earth and grey.** In storm pictures the clothing holds the strongest local colour, a few deep reds and blues set against earths and sea-greys [M: Christ Asleep, Rebecca]. Burke: in history painting "a gay or gaudy drapery can never have a happy effect", and sublime colours are "sad and fuscous", "except perhaps a strong red" [3]. So the reds are deep and the blues dark **[J]** for the reading.
- **Heavy cloth shows weight; light cloth shows wind.** Wool greatcoats and cloaks hang plumb even in a winter march [M: Géricault]. Capes, shawls, sails and sheets carry the wind [M: Faust, Martin, Whalers].
- **White cloth is a light source in the landscape.** Sails, laundry and white dresses are the brightest notes below the horizon. They are placed where the eye should go, and still stay below the sky's brightest value [M: Flers, Whalers, Martin, Oxbow umbrella; see `romanticism--light-shadow.md`].
- **Not the Neoclassical "wet drapery".** Cloth clinging in sculptural folds to show an idealised body is the classical convention (layer 1, Met [13]). Romantic cloth is pressed against the body only on the windward side and otherwise moves or hangs **[J]**, following the colour-over-line contrast in [2].

## 5. Colour, edge and mark
- **Colour:** local colour stays deep in shadow because each fold reflects its own colour (layer 1, Leonardo §281). Lit planes are broken with warm or cool light; white linen in storm light goes cold grey-white on the lit plane and warm grey-brown in the hollows **[J]** (from the measured storm palettes, `romanticism--light-shadow.md`).
- **Edges:** crisp along the lit belly or edge facing the light, lost into the background on the shadow side, so the cloth merges with the storm around it [M: Rebecca, Christ Asleep]. In watercolour or wash, a few dark accents in the hollows over a light wash [M: Martin].
- **Marks:** each fold or plane in one loaded stroke following the cloth's movement. Long strokes with the wind for streaming cloth; short dragged strokes for heavy wool; impasto or a knife touch on the brightest cloth light **[J]**, consistent with the style doc's selective impasto. In prints the hatching direction follows the wind [M: Faust].

## 6. What stays true to life
- Support points and gravity (layer 1 §1): a cloak still hangs from the shoulders, a sheet from its pegs or line, and a heavy coat falls plumb unless wind is visibly stronger [M: Géricault].
- Wind and gravity combine diagonally (layer 1, Wind and gravity), and heavy cloth lifts less than light cloth.
- The windward side presses against and reveals the form; the leeward side billows (layer 1; Leonardo §391).
- Wet cloth is darker, heavier and clings (layer 1). No public-domain Romantic work in this sample shows translucent wet linen against light, so translucency follows layer 1, not a style convention **[J]**.

## 7. For the gale case (heavy wool skirt and shawl; wet linen sheets like sails, one torn free)
- **The skirt:** heavy wool barely lifts. Long pipe folds pressed against the legs on the windward side and pushed back as one connected mass, with no fluttering hem [M: Géricault; layer 1 wind note]. The **shawl** is the flag: one long dark shape streaming back from the shoulders, taut, its fringe at most a few broken touches [M: Martin, Faust].
- **The sheets:** each sheet is a sail-like belly, a big lit convex plane with a dark hollow. Only two or three fold lines run from each peg toward the free edge, and they get wider as they go. The sheets are the brightest shapes on the ground [M: Whalers, Flers]. Wet linen stays heavy at the bottom edge and sags between pegs (diaper) even while bellying **[J]**, from layer 1.
- **The torn-free sheet:** a twisting flag in the air, its leading edge crisp and its trailing edge lost into the sky haze, smaller and higher than the others so it reads as carried off [M: Whalers, where the sails dissolve into the air; **[J]** for the application].
- **Colour:** wool in deep earths or one strong deep colour (red or blue); linen cold white on the lit planes, grey-brown in the hollows.

## 8. Avoid
- Many small, even wrinkles; stitching and texture inventories; fold lines drawn over flat colour.
- Pastel or "gay and gaudy" cloth colour [3]; uniformly saturated cloth with no deep shadow.
- Flapping in all directions: every cloth, hair and smoke shape must take one wind.
- Neoclassical sculptural "wet drapery" on a still, idealised body.
- Cloth brighter than the sky's brightest light.
