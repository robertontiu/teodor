# Ukiyo-e: architecture (layer 2: the subject in the style)

How ukiyo-e renders buildings, streets and ground surfaces, with a market street in focus: a cobbled or paved pavement seen from directly above as a pattern, and the edge of a stall's awning. Read [the layer-1 page](../../subjects/architecture.md) first, and [the style doc](../ukiyo-e.md). Researched 2026-11 from public-domain prints at the Met and the Cleveland Museum of Art (CMA) and museum texts; values marked **judgement** have no published source.

## 1. The departure from layer 1, in one paragraph
Layer 1 builds buildings in linear perspective from one eye level, lights them with a sunlit face and a sky-lit shadow face, and lets textured ground shrink and blur as it recedes. Ukiyo-e mostly draws buildings in an **oblique parallel view from above**, inherited from the "blown-off roof" (*fukinuki yatai*) of narrative scroll painting, where "the viewer looks inside from above" (JAANUS; Met, *Yamato-e Painting*). Western one-point perspective came in as a separate genre, the *uki-e*, from about 1739 (Wikipedia, *Uki-e*; CMA on Masanobu). Light is absent: walls, roofs and the ground are flat colours, and **ground surfaces are either left as bare paper or treated as a flat pattern** (boards, slabs, tiles) that does not shrink with distance the way layer 1 expects.

## 2. Proportion and structure: what stretches or flattens
- **Oblique view, parallel edges.** Floors, verandas, bridge decks and paving are drawn with their joints as parallel lines on a slant, without convergence (Met 55053 veranda boards, 55222 bridge planks, 54377 paved forecourt; CMA 1985.304 floor). Some earlier designs even widen with distance (*gyaku enkinhō*, "inverted perspective", used in yamato-e and by Harunobu: JAANUS).
- **High eye.** Ground and floors take a large share of the picture; roofs are seen from above as flat planes of tile pattern (Met 55287 Mitsui roofs; 55222 roof in the foreground).
- **Scale bends to the design.** Buildings are cut, stretched or shrunk to fit the composition; the series *One Hundred Famous Views of Edo* is known for exaggerated foreground scale (Wikipedia, secondary; CMA 1992.73).
- **Uki-e exception:** one-point perspective with converging floor lines and rows of lanterns, used mostly for interiors and long streets (Met 36669; CMA 1916.1154: "Masanobu was probably the first printmaker to use this method"). Later landscape designers mixed it in: Hokusai's Sazai Hall veranda has floorboards fanning to one point while the view beyond is flat bands (Met 37321).

## 3. Ground planes and street surfaces as pattern
What the prints share (Met 36922, 54670, 54377, 37321, 55222, 55733, 55053):
- **Bare paper or one flat tone** is the commonest street: Kiyonaga's shop front stands on unprinted paper (measured `#d0bfa9`, aged), Hiroshige's Nihonbashi road is one flat grey-brown (`#908375`, measured, web image of an aged print) (Met 54670, 36922).
- **When the surface is described, it is described as a pattern drawn in line**: flagstones as a grid of fine double lines on pale grey with a faint stipple (Met 54377); floorboards and bridge planks as evenly spaced lines, parallel or (in Western-influenced designs) converging (Met 55053, 55222; 37321 converging); roof tiles as a grid or rows of scallops (Met 55222, 55287); snow-covered ground as scattered marks on white paper (Met 55733).
- **The pattern keeps its scale** across the visible ground; there is no texture gradient (**judgement**, from these works; contrast layer-1 `landscape.md`, where texture shrinks with distance).
- **Steps and kerbs** are marked by a narrow band of darker flat tone along the riser, not by shadow (Met 54377).

**From directly above (the brief's view), judgement:** no print looks straight down on a pavement; the nearest is the hawk's-eye view over Susaki (Met 55733). Built from the shared conventions: the pavement becomes a flat field of the ground colour with cobbles drawn as a repeated cell (rounded rectangles or fans of setts) in thin grey or sumi line, all cells the same size across the picture, with perhaps a faint second tone in alternating cells; cracks, drains and kerbs as clean line; no sheen, no shadow in the joints.

## 4. The stall's awning edge
The ukiyo-e model is the shop front: a strip of eave or bamboo blind with a fringe, and curtain panels hanging from it, cut by the top edge of the picture (Met 54670; CMA 1930.211 "shop curtains hanging from their eaves"). The edge is a single clean key line with its repeated motif (fringe, scallop, curtain slits) drawn identically along it. See `ukiyo-e--objects.md` for the stripes.

## 5. Colour, edge and mark
- **Ruled sumi line** for structure; thin, even lines for joints and pattern (all works).
- **Flat colour per surface:** wood in pale ochre, plaster in paper white, tiles in blue-grey, ground in paper, grey-brown or a soft green (palette in style doc).
- **Bokashi** on the ground only as a band at a near or far edge, never as shading (Met 36922 sky; 55733 sea and snow; **judgement** on ground use).
- **Edges:** all hard.

## 6. What stays true to life
Construction and its repetition (number of planks, tiles, posts), how a curtain hangs from a rail, kerbs and steps where they would be, the relative size of a cobble to a foot (**judgement**).

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. The pavement is a flat field with a repeated cell pattern in line, the same cell size everywhere.
2. Receding edges of boards, slabs and stalls stay parallel (or follow one deliberate *uki-e* vanishing point, not an accidental mix).
3. No shadows in joints, under kerbs or under the awning; a step is a band of flat tone.
4. The awning edge is one clean line with an identical repeated motif, cut by the frame.
5. Roofs and ground seen from above as flat planes of pattern.

## 9. Sources
- Met and CMA object pages above.
- Columbia University, JAANUS, [fukinuki yatai](https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/record/fukinukiyatai) ("rendering a building without a roof and ceiling so that the viewer looks inside from above"; the viewer's position "tends to become higher") and [gyaku enkinhō](https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/node/4330) (lines "drawn to spread apart as they go further into the distance"; used by Harunobu).
- Met, Heilbrunn Timeline, [Yamato-e Painting](https://www.metmuseum.org/en/essays/yamato-e-painting): roofs removed "to provide a glimpse into the interior from above"; cloud bands that "obscure and divide space".
- CMA, [Perspective View of the Interior of the Nakamura Theater](https://clevelandart.org/art/1916.1154): first Japanese perspective manual 1734; Masanobu "probably the first printmaker to use this method".
- Wikipedia, [Uki-e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uki-e) (secondary): Masanobu studied European engravings around 1739; Toyoharu developed the form; interiors favoured.
- Wikipedia, [One Hundred Famous Views of Edo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Hundred_Famous_Views_of_Edo) (secondary): exaggerated scale of foreground features.
- **Judgement calls:** no texture gradient, bokashi on ground bands, the straight-down pavement construction (no print found), cobble size relative to a foot, measured hexes (web images of aged prints).
