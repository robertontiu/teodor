# Romanticism: water (layer 2)

How Romantic landscape painting (c. 1790–1850) renders water, written as a departure from layer 1 (`docs/subjects/water.md`), with this case in mind: a thin strip of distant sea at a low horizon, dark, a lit band where the break in the clouds touches it, and rain veils. The sky over it is in `romanticism--sky.md`. Style research: `docs/styles/romanticism.md`.

Researched 2026-11-26. **[J]** marks a judgement call. "Measured" values come from the museums' open-access images (method in section 8).

## 2. The twist in one paragraph
At a low horizon, layer 1 says distant water is a near-perfect mirror of the sky just above it, equal to it in value. The Romantic strip of sea instead works as a dark ruled bar that sets the bottom edge of the sky, with light laid across it in one place only: the band where the break's light touches the water. The sea is subordinate to the sky ("every other subject introduced should be subservient", Cox [1]), drawn as a line and a band rather than as waves, and lit as a stage for the sky's effect.

## 3. Departures from layer 1

**Proportion (flattened):**
- **A thin strip.** Where the sea is distant, it occupies a band only a few percent of the picture height between the horizon and the shore or foreground (Dahl, Constable's cloud study, Cotman's far sea, Hadleigh). Near water is a separate subject (Cotman's and Isabey's churned foreground waves) **[J]**, from the sample.
- **The horizon is a ruled straight line,** usually the one continuous hard edge in the picture: Dahl, Delacroix, Atkins and Cotman all hold it dead level and crisp across the sheet while everything above is soft. Rain or mist is allowed to cut it in places (Hadleigh, Harrow).

**Value (darkened):**
- **The far sea is darker than the sky just above it.** Measured 4% strips below the horizon against 5% strips above: Constable's cloud study L* 57 against 72, Delacroix 73 against 83, Girtin 58 against 67. That is 9 to 15 L* darker even in daylight studies **(measured)**. Layer 1's mirror rule would make them equal on calm water. The painters' rule has a physical basis: wind-ruffled water tilts its facets toward higher, darker sky and away from the bright horizon sky (layer 1's small-mirror reasoning), and under a storm the higher sky is the dark cloud **[J]**.
- **Under the storm, the strip is the darkest part of the distance.** In Dahl and Cotman the sea under the cloud mass is near the darkest value of the land and sea, while the lit band within it rises 30–40 L* above it. Measured column means along Dahl's strip run from L* 16 to 55, and along Hadleigh's from 25 to 69 **(measured)**.

**Conventions:**
- **One lit band.** The light from the break lies on the far sea as a single horizontal band, brightest at the horizon and fading toward the viewer. Its ends are broken, not ruled (Dahl's moonlit band, Hadleigh's lit estuary, Isabey's streak under the cloud, Cotman's pale patches). Its position follows layer 1's glitter-path geometry, under the light source and on the line toward the viewer. A low light makes it long and thin.
- **The band is made of touches.** In Dahl it is a row of short horizontal impasto flecks of warm light over the dark sea (measured about #856956 against #2e2724). In watercolour it is reserved or scratched-out paper; Cotman's sheet lists "scratching out, stopping out, and gum arabic" [2] **[J]**, matching the style doc's watercolour process.
- **Rain veils cross the strip.** Where rain falls on the distance, the veil softens or hides the horizon and lightens or darkens the strip locally (Hadleigh, Harrow). It is the only thing allowed to break the ruled line.
- **Boats and sails as scale and silhouette.** A small dark or sunlit sail on the far strip gives the sea its scale (Dahl, Cotman, Atkins) **[J]**. Keep it small; the brief decides whether any appears.

**Simplified or left out:**
- Wave forms at a distance: none. The far strip shows no wave shapes, only horizontal value bands. Wave drawing is kept for near water (Cotman's waves "milky, foam-topped", made partly by "mixing flour paste into his washes" [2]).
- Reflections of objects on the far strip: left out, apart from the lit band.
- Transparency and bottom colour: left out at this distance (consistent with layer 1's grazing-angle view).

## 4. Colour
- **Dark sea:** a cool dark mixed from blue and earth, close to the cloud's colour but darker and slightly greener or bluer: Constable's cloud-study band measures about #7e8886, Cotman's far sea #918b7a against a #8e8d87 sky, and Dahl's night sea about #2e2724 (warmed by varnish) **(measured, approximate)**.
- **Lit band:** the colour of the break, cool silver by day and warm cream or pinkish in evening or moonlight light. In Dahl it is warmer than the sea around it.
- No saturated blue or turquoise on a distant storm sea. Layer 1's "transmitted light at wave crests is the most saturated colour" applies only to near breaking waves, where Cotman and Isabey show greenish crests **[J]**.

## 5. Edges and marks
- Horizon: one long, level, hard stroke or a ruled edge. In oil, a `liner` or the edge of a `flat` pulled straight; in watercolour, the edge of a wash laid on dry paper.
- Strip body: long horizontal strokes only, the full width of the strip, with no vertical marks except rain.
- Lit band: short horizontal flecks of thicker light paint, denser at the horizon and thinning toward the viewer, with broken ends.
- Rain: soft vertical or slanting drags over the strip, matching the sky's rain direction.

## 6. What stays true to life
- The horizon is level and straight (it is the eye level, see `space.md`).
- The lit band sits under the light source, between it and the viewer, and is long and narrow for a low light (layer 1's glitter path).
- The sea's darks agree with the sky above: dark under the cloud, lit only under the break.
- Wind direction in the rain, cloud and any whitecaps agrees across sea and sky (layer 1's single Beaufort force).

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. The far sea is a thin strip with a dead-level, crisp horizon, the hardest long edge in the picture, interrupted only by rain.
2. Outside the lit band, the strip is darker than the sky directly above it (about 10 L* or more in daylight, more under the storm).
3. There is exactly one lit band, under the break, made of horizontal touches, brightest at the horizon, with broken ends.
4. There are no wave shapes on the distant strip, only horizontal value bands.
5. The colour is low-chroma, close to the cloud's, never a saturated blue.
6. Rain veils cross the strip at the same angle as in the sky.

## 8. Engine notes and method
- **Oil:** lay the strip with a long `flat` in a dark blue-earth mix after the sky is dry enough, rule the horizon with a `liner` or the flat's edge, then put in the lit band last with short, loaded `round` or `knife` touches. Rain goes on as `blend` drags over everything **[J]**. Watercolour pole: reserve the band, because v8 has no scratching-out or lifting tool (style doc's gaps).
- **Views:** `look squint` should show the strip as one dark bar with one bright slot; `look notan 2` should put the lit band in the light shape together with the break.
- **Method:** the horizon was found as the strongest row-to-row change in L* within ±4% of an estimated line. The 5% strip above it and the 4% strip below were averaged over the open-sea columns. L* is computed from the museums' sRGB images. Colours are median sRGB of hand-placed regions. The numbers come from photographs of aged surfaces and are approximate.
