# Ukiyo-e: space (layer 2: the subject in the style)

How ukiyo-e builds pictorial space, with a very high bird's-eye view, shallow flattened space, a strong diagonal and hard crops at the frame in focus. Read [the layer-1 page](../../subjects/space.md) first, and [the style doc](../ukiyo-e.md) section 7. Researched 2026-11 from public-domain prints at the Met and the Cleveland Museum of Art (CMA) and museum texts; values marked **judgement** have no published source.

## 1. The departure from layer 1, in one paragraph
Layer 1 builds space with one eye, one horizon and vanishing points on it, aerial perspective, texture gradients and a cone of vision of about 60°, and warns against several horizons in one picture. Ukiyo-e space comes from a different tradition: an **oblique, parallel view from high above** inherited from scroll painting's "blown-off roof" (*fukinuki yatai*: JAANUS; Met, *Yamato-e Painting*), with depth carried by **overlap, sudden jumps in scale and flat value steps**, not by converging lines or haze. Western linear perspective arrived in the *uki-e* ("floating pictures") from about 1739 and was absorbed selectively (Wikipedia, *Uki-e*; CMA 1916.1154). Landscape designers combined the two with radical **crops** and **close foregrounds**: the "dramatic cropping" and "surprising, unconventional perspectives" of Hiroshige's late Edo views (Portland Art Museum), which the Nabis and others later took up as "piquant, unusual viewpoints" (Met, *Japonisme*).

## 2. How space is flattened
- **Parallel receding edges.** Floors, decks, paving, boxes and stalls recede on parallel diagonals; the picture has a dominant slant rather than a vanishing point (Met 55222, 54377, 55053; see `ukiyo-e--architecture.md`). Inverted perspective also occurs (JAANUS, *gyaku enkinhō*).
- **Few depth layers, each flat.** A foreground band, a middle band and a far band, each a flat shape; mist or cloud bands may separate them (Met, *Yamato-e*: "large bands of clouds that obscure and divide space"; Met 55733 snow plain and sea as stacked bands).
- **Scale jumps instead of gradual recession.** A huge near object against a tiny far view, with little in between: the hawk over the whole plain (Met 55733), an oar against distant ships (CMA 1992.73), a ship's mast across a shore (CMA 1985.315: "the viewer seems to be floating in the bay on board a ship").
- **Flat value steps for distance:** three greys from three blocks for near-to-far bamboo (CMA 1948.306), not atmospheric blur.
- **No texture gradient:** patterns keep their scale across the ground (**judgement**, from the works listed).

## 3. The very high bird's-eye view
- The ukiyo-e high view looks down **obliquely**: the ground fills most of the picture, the horizon is high or at a band of sky at the top (Met 55733, 55222, 56508, 36922). Portland on Susaki: the print brings the viewer "to a height far beyond that of human scale".
- Verticals stay vertical; there is no three-point convergence downward (**judgement**, from all works listed; contrast layer-1 `space.md` "verticals converge downward if the view tilts").
- People and objects seen from such a height become a pattern of repeated small shapes on the ground (Met 55222 crowd on a bridge; Met 56508 bird's-eye of Ryōgoku Bridge).
- **Straight down (the brief),** judgement: no ukiyo-e print looks vertically down. The closest is the oblique hawk's-eye view. A straight-down view in the ukiyo-e language is the logical end of its high oblique view: the ground plane becomes the picture plane, all pattern is shown undistorted, and things on it (birds, crates, a bag, the edge of an awning) are flat silhouettes seen from their tops. Keep it honest in the journal as an extension, not a convention.

## 4. The strong diagonal and the crop
- **Diagonal:** a bridge, a deck, a road or a row of stalls crosses the sheet corner to corner and carries the composition (Met 55433 *Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi*, CMA 1985.318 "figures scurry across the bridge amid slanting sheets of rain"; Met 55222 bridge rail; Met 37321, where the veranda's floorboards fan to one vanishing point, a Western borrowing set against a flat banded view).
- **Hard crops:** gates cut on both sides (Met 36922), shop curtains cut at the top (Met 54670), roofs and signboards cut at the bottom and sides (Met 55287, 55222), a hawk's wing cut at the top (Met 55733), a boat's oar or mast cutting through the foreground (CMA 1992.73, 1985.315). The frame cuts objects that are close to the viewer; distant things are seldom cut (**judgement**).
- **Format:** the vertical format of *One Hundred Famous Views of Edo*, which Hiroshige pioneered for landscape (Wikipedia, secondary), forces tall crops; narrow pillar prints even more (style doc).
- **Asymmetry:** the weight sits off-centre, often a large near shape on one side and open space on the other (Met *Japonisme*: "asymmetrical compositions").

## 5. What stays true to life
Overlap order, the relative size of like things at the same depth, the ground's continuity, the direction of gravity (verticals vertical).

## 6. Colour, edge and mark (spatial use)
- Flat bands of colour separate depth layers; bokashi at the top edge or horizon closes the space (Portland; style doc).
- The near layer has the strongest colours and darkest key lines; the far layer is paler and simpler (**judgement**, from Met 55733, 36922; CMA 1948.306).
- Edges stay hard at every depth; distance never softens an edge.

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. One dominant diagonal crosses the picture.
2. At least two near objects are cut hard by the frame; the far view is not.
3. Receding edges are parallel (or one deliberate uki-e vanishing point, never an accidental mix); verticals vertical.
4. Depth is two or three flat bands plus a jump in scale, not a gradual recession.
5. No haze and no softening with distance; distance only as paler, simpler flat tones.
6. The ground's pattern keeps one scale across the picture.
