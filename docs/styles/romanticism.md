# Romanticism (the sublime landscape and the British watercolour tradition)

Researched 2026-11-25. Citations in brackets refer to the numbered sources at the end. **[J]** marks a judgement call.

## 1. In one paragraph
A movement in art and literature, named by the early nineteenth century, that gained momentum in France and Britain (and Germany) from about 1800 and flourished until about mid-century [1][2]. It reacted against Enlightenment reason and classical restraint with "a new emotionalism", "a new interest in human psychology, expression of personal feeling and interest in the natural world" [2], and an "emphasis on the imagination and emotion" and the artist's individuality [1]. In painting its central ground is landscape: nature as powerful, unpredictable and potentially catastrophic [1], seen through the eighteenth-century idea of the sublime, which Burke (1757) connected "with experiences of awe, terror and danger" [3]. In Britain the same years were the "Golden Age" of watercolour, which moved from tinted topographical drawing to loose washes that sought "to capture fleeting atmospheric effects" [4]. Baudelaire put it as "a way of feeling" rather than a choice of subject [1]. Context names only: Turner, Constable, Girtin, Cotman, Friedrich, Géricault, Delacroix.

## 2. How it carries feeling
- **The sublime: scale and threat.** Human figures dwarfed by storm, sea or mountain (an army against "a swirling snowstorm" [1]); nature's violence, shipwreck and struggle [1]. The sublime is "awe-inspiring through its power and its terror" [5].
- **Emptiness and boundlessness.** A low horizon running edge to edge, no foreground devices to lead the eye in, a vast sky: "as if one's eyelids had been cut off" (Kleist on a Friedrich seascape) [5].
- **Atmosphere and the moment.** Fleeting weather, mist, light breaking through [4]; a landscape as "one state of mind" (Constable) [1]; dramatic skies and ruins for melancholy reverie [1].
- **Veiling.** Forms partly obscured by the misty paint of the sky [6]; hazy, unresolved transitions by scumbling [5].
- **Gesture of the brush** (Turner, Delacroix, Constable) carries energy and turbulence; the German branch is instead still and precise **[J]**.
- **Avoided:** classical restraint, moral history-painting lessons, even and rational order [1][2]. Emotion research: `docs/research/emotion.md` once written (for now `docs/research.md`).

## 3. Media, supports and grounds
- **Oil on canvas** for exhibition landscapes and history pictures [1]. Friedrich (example): thin, transparent oil washes over a delicate underdrawing, so the paintings look like coloured drawings, with hatching, stippling and selective impasto [6].
- **Watercolour on paper:** dry soluble cakes from the 1780s, moist pans later, tubes in 1846; wove paper allowed smooth, uninterrupted washes; sizing and stretching resisted warping; sable brushes [4]. Traditional papers were off-white or buff; Turner also used light grey or blue paper with opaque colour [7]. Body colour (gouache) became easier with "Chinese White" in 1834 [4].
- **Default for Teodor:** oil on `canvas` with a light warm ground `#ebe3d1` **[J]** for the sublime landscape; **watercolour** on cold-press white paper `#f6f2e8` **[J]** for the British watercolour branch; gouache (body colour) on toned grey-blue paper `#a9b1b8` **[J]** as a variant after [7].

## 4. Palette
- **Principle:** atmosphere over local colour; a warm-cool polarity (golden light against blue-grey storm) **[J]**; few pigments with tonal gradation (Friedrich's winter scene: lead white, red earth and grades of smalt) [6]; in watercolour, the white of the paper is the light [7].
- **Pigments** (hex approximate **[J]**):
  - lead white `#efe9dd` [6], red earth `#9a4b33` [6], smalt `#6a7fa8` [6]
  - Turner's watercolour beginning: warm yellow `#e8c55a`, then cool light blue `#a9c3d9` [7]
  - Prussian blue `#1f3b5a`, ultramarine `#2b438f`, cobalt blue `#2f55a4` (new blues of the period **[J]**; [6] notes Friedrich chose smalt over cobalt and Prussian)
  - yellow ochre `#c79c4b`, burnt sienna `#8a4a2c`, indian red `#7d3a32`, sepia `#4b3a2a`, ivory black `#1d1b19` **[J]**
  - storm greys mixed from blue and earth `#5c6470`, `#8d8f8c` **[J]**
- **Avoid:** flat local colour, pure black skies, saturated synthetic greens, uniform daylight **[J]**.

## 5. Light and value
Light is the protagonist: breaking through cloud, low sun, moon, fire or storm-light; wide value range, often a bright centre in a dark or turbulent surround (Turner) or a quiet graded sky over a dark land (Friedrich) **[J]** from [1][5]. In watercolour lights are reserved paper or lifted out; first washes dry with soft edges, later strokes on dry paper are hard-edged [7].

## 6. Drawing and form
Two poles **[J]**: dissolved, vortex-like form in which drawing gives way to atmosphere (Turner); precise drawing veiled by thin paint (Friedrich, with ruled lines for architecture and a freer sketch reinforced by a firmer line [6]). Figures small, seen from far, or from behind (the Rückenfigur draws the viewer in but limits what they see [5]).

## 7. Composition
Low horizon and big sky; edge-to-edge emptiness without picturesque foreground framing [5]; a vortex of storm around a light centre; ruins, mountains, sea; tiny figures against vast nature [1][5]. Symmetry and frontal stillness in the German branch **[J]**.

## 8. Marks and edges
Oil: scumbling for hazy transitions [5]; stippling and hatching for misty surfaces [6]; selective impasto [6]; vigorous sweeping strokes in turbulent skies **[J]**. Watercolour: broad washes, wet-in-wet for sky and distance, hard-edged strokes on dry paper for nearer forms, tiny brushes for telling details, scratching out, rubbing out, lifting with water or sponge, reserved paper [4][7]; a skim of gum arabic to make dark water glossy [7]. Edge hierarchy: lost edges in sky and distance, a few crisp edges near the focus **[J]**.

## 9. Process (the order of work)
**Watercolour (after Turner, [7]):**
1. Soak the paper; lay a thin warm yellow wash, then cool light blue, adding colours as the paper dries ("colour beginning").
2. Wet-in-wet sky and distant hills while the paper is wet.
3. Middle-ground forms in strokes on drier paper (harder edges).
4. Reductive lights: lift with clean water or sponge, rub out, scratch out with a sharp point; leave paper blank for the brightest lights.
5. Last: tiny brushes for figures and details where they are "most telling"; gum arabic in darks.

**Oil (after Friedrich, [6]; Turner-type sublime **[J]**):**
1. Light ground; underdrawing, ruled for architecture, freer for nature, reinforced.
2. Thin transparent washes establishing the tonal scheme with few pigments.
3. Forms painted, then veiled by the sky paint where atmosphere should swallow them.
4. Sky blended by scumbling; stippling for mist; selective impasto on lights.
5. Final small figure and accents.

Teodor's phases **[J]**: phase 1 = drawing (`scene` or SVG schema with the horizon and light decided) and the colour beginning; phase 2 = atmosphere (sky, washes, veiling); phase 3 = near forms, lights, the few telling details.

## 10. Finishing
Oil: varnish **[J]**; Turner notoriously reworked exhibition pictures up to the last moment **[J]** (not in a read source). Watercolour: no varnish; finished when the telling details are placed and lights lifted or scratched; unfinished "beginnings" were also kept [7]. Finishing for Teodor is restraint: stop before the atmosphere is filled in **[J]**.

## 11. Pitfalls for Teodor
- **Pastiche:** the wanderer above a sea of fog; a generic orange sunset vortex; calendar mountains.
- **Teodor's own habit:** "a lone figure from behind" is Teodor's main recorded habit (`docs/habits.md`) and the Rückenfigur is a Romantic device. Use it only if the commission demands it; carry scale with nature itself, a crowd, a ship, an animal, or no figure **[J]**.
- **Nearest wrong feelings:** kitsch grandeur (fantasy-cover epic), soft-focus nostalgia, gloom without awe, a pretty picturesque view without threat **[J]**.
- **Watercolour:** overworking kills transparency; muddy greys from too many layers **[J]**.
- **Engine:** no lifting, scratching or reserve tools yet.

## 12. Engine mapping (studio v8)
- **Medium:** `oil --support canvas` (exists); **watercolour** (being added) on cold-press paper for the British branch; gouache (being added) on toned paper for the body-colour variant.
- **Schema:** `scene` blockouts work for terrain and sea, but the sky is the subject: a painted SVG schema for cloud structure is better **[J]**.
- **Tools (oil):** large `flat` and `round` for skies; `blend` and `smudge` for scumbled haze; `fan` for misty foliage and spray; `knife` for impasto lights; `liner` for small figures and masts.
- **Intentions:** `match` with large brushes and low `faithful` for atmosphere; `glaze` to veil forms in mist; `blend` for sky transitions.
- **Felt state:** high arousal and tension for the stormy sublime; low arousal, high care for the still German branch; the painter's feeling decides between them.
- **Views:** `look squint` and `look notan 2` to keep the light centre; `look temp` for the warm-cool polarity.
- **Gaps:** watercolour medium not yet in v8 (wet-in-wet blooms, granulation, staining, hard drying edges); no lifting, sponging, scratching-out or masking/reserve tools; no gum arabic gloss; no toned-paper support; glaze is a regional mix rather than a scumble with broken coverage; no varnish finishing op.
