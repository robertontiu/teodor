# Graphite hyperrealism (photorealist pencil drawing)

Researched 2026-11. Every value names its source; values marked **judgement** have no published source and are editorial choices for Teodor. No artist's name drives any setting (rule 3 in `docs/orchestration.md`). The current is still practised and its works are in copyright, so no work of it is named or measured here; sources that are interviews or tutorials are cited for how the drawings are made, not as models.

## 1. In one paragraph
Hyperrealism is "a genre of painting and sculpture resembling a high-resolution photograph", an art movement and style in the United States and Europe that developed from the early 1970s, out of Photorealism ([Wikipedia, Hyperrealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperrealism_(visual_arts))). Photorealism itself, a late-1960s American movement, "encompasses painting, drawing and other graphic media"; its working definition is that the artist "uses the camera and photograph to gather information" and "a mechanical or semi-mechanical means to transfer the information" ([Wikipedia, Photorealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photorealism), quoting the 1969-72 definition by the dealer who coined the term). Hyperrealism differs from photorealism in being "often ... narrative and emotive", and in being tied to the very high resolution of digital photographs (Wikipedia, Hyperrealism). Graphite hyperrealism is that ambition in pencil: a monochrome drawing that reads, at viewing distance, as a black-and-white photograph. A working draughtsman of the genre says a drawing is finished when "it looks like the photograph" ([artnet, In the Studio](https://news.artnet.com/in-the-studio/go-inside-artists-studio-cj-hendry-watch-make-viral-photorealistic-drawings-1404796)).

## 2. How it carries feeling
- **Through the subject and the shock of the medium.** The image carries the feeling a photograph would; the second feeling is the viewer's disbelief that it is a drawing, and the patience it implies (hundreds of hours: section 9). Hyperrealism is "often ... narrative and emotive" and addresses social, cultural or political themes (Wikipedia, Hyperrealism).
- **Through the photograph's own devices:** depth of field (a sharp focal plane, the rest blurred), single-source light, extreme close-ups and enlargement: hyperrealism "capitalizes upon photographic limitations such as depth of field, perspective and range of focus" and often has "a softer, much more complex focus on the subject" (Wikipedia, Hyperrealism). A blurred background was rare in painting before photography and reads as photographic ([Eclectic Light, Painting on the edge: depth of field](https://eclecticlight.co/2023/10/13/painting-on-the-edge-depth-of-field/)).
- **Through scale:** works are enlarged far beyond the photograph (paintings "typically 10 to 20 times the size of the reference photo": Wikipedia, Hyperrealism), so skin, paper fibre, a wrinkle become landscapes. **Judgement:** the felt register is held still, scrutinising, intimate; the hand disappears, so gesture carries nothing.
- **Avoided:** visible hand, expressive line, stylisation, any mark that says "drawing" at viewing distance (section 10). See `docs/research/emotion.md` on how marks read as a hand: here that reading is deliberately suppressed.

## 3. Media, supports and grounds
- **Graphite pencils in a range of grades**, mechanical pencils for detail, often with charcoal or carbon pencils for the darkest darks: a large hyperrealist drawing described as "a combination of graphite and charcoal pencils" ([My Modern Met](https://mymodernmet.com/dylan-eakin-hyperrealistic-pencil-drawing)); past about Munsell value 4, graphite only gets shinier, so draughtsmen switch to graphite powder or black (graphite-carbon) pencils ([Vitruvian Studio, graphite shine](https://vitruvianstudio.com/blog/3-ways-to-reduce-graphite-shine-in-your-drawings/)). Graphite powder brushed on as a base layer is part of the practice ([Creative Bloq, graphite tips](https://www.creativebloq.com/how-to/get-more-from-graphite-with-these-tips)).
- **Mechanical pencils:** leads from 0.2 to 0.9 mm, graded 6H-4B; 0.3 mm (up to 2B) for "ultimate control", 0.5 mm (up to 4B) as the all-rounder, 0.7 mm for looser speckled effects; an even line at a constant angle, no sharpening, "made for precision drawing", but broad tone is slow and darks need several layers ([Wildlife Art Store, mechanical pencils](https://www.wildlifeartstore.com/draw-with-mechanical-pencils/)).
- **Supports: smooth bristol, vellum bristol or hot-pressed paper.** The sources disagree on which, and why matters for the engine:
  - Smooth (plate) bristol has "little to no tooth" and is meant for "fine lines, detail drawings", mechanical pencil and pen; vellum bristol's "peaks and valleys ... grab dry media such as graphite", and "more even shading and deeper tones can be achieved on a vellum surface" ([Strathmore, smooth vs vellum](https://strathmoreartist.com/blog-reader/the-difference-between-bristol-smooth-and-bristol-vellum); [Strathmore, what is bristol](https://strathmoreartist.com/blog-reader/what-is-bristol-paper)).
  - Smooth papers give "a narrower range of tone"; "the smoother the paper grain the more difficult it will be to produce an even shade" ([Strathmore, graphite shading](https://strathmoreartist.com/blog-reader/tips-for-graphite-shading-which-paper-to-use)). Smooth bristol's "shiny toothless" surface makes graphite "pale and insipid", so vellum is recommended; bristol is "often used ... for easy blending"; hot-pressed paper is smooth and suited to pencil but expensive ([Wildlife Art Store, paper](https://www.wildlifeartstore.com/paper-for-pencil-drawing/)). A tutorial drawing on bristol board is in [Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/how-to/get-more-from-graphite-with-these-tips).
  - **Teodor default: vellum bristol** (`vellum`), white (`#f7f6f2`, **judgement**). `plate` for the smoothest blends and the finest detail at the price of pale darks; `hot_press` for many layers and erasing.
- **Sheet size:** large. Hyperrealist works are enlarged many times over the photograph (Wikipedia); drawings are described as "large-scale" (My Modern Met) and "quite large" (artnet). **Judgement:** 48 x 64 cm (2400 x 3200 px at the studio's 5 px/mm), the size the engine was tested at.

## 4. Palette
- **Monochrome.** Grey from the paper's white to graphite's dark grey, plus carbon black where graphite stops. The grades: "H" for hardness, "B" for blackness, 9H to 9B, HB in the middle; more clay makes a harder, lighter lead ([Met, Graphite](https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/materials-and-techniques-drawing-graphite); the engine's ramp is in `docs/calibration/graphite.md`).
- **Values the media reach** (`docs/calibration/graphite.md`, `charcoal.md`, `graphite-hyperrealism.md`): graphite alone stops near L* 40 as an area (it only gets shinier past Munsell value 4) and dense soft graphite lines at L* 32.5-34.7; that is **graphite's** limit, not the drawing's. Charcoal reaches the photograph's darks when it is laid first and built in cycles with workable fixative: about L* 19 on vellum in three cycles (measured, patch test). White paper is about L* 96. A photograph reaches L* 17 at its 5th percentile (section 5) and a close-up portrait L* 5.5 (`graphite-hyperrealism-closeup`).
- **The rule for the schema:** the schema keeps the reference's values. No value floor is planned in: the darks are laid first, in charcoal, before any blending pass touches them (sections 9 and 12). The old note "graphite stops near L* 40, carbon near 32" described a light-to-dark recipe on a full tooth; used as a planning floor it removed every dark from round 19 (docs/research/hyperrealism-gaps.md, G2).
- **Avoid:** the shine of over-burnished soft graphite in the darks, which reads as a grey glare in raking light (Vitruvian Studio; Met: graphite "often look[s] shiny in raking or oblique light").

## 5. Light and value
- **One photographic light, the photograph's full range.** Measured (`studio/targets.parts/graphite-hyperrealism.json`, 45 public-domain black-and-white photographs, section 13): key L* 50 ± 12, spread 21 ± 5, p5-p95 L* 17-82, about 29% dark and 32% light. That is the ruler for the finished drawing.
- **For contrast, finished 19th-century graphite drawings** (8 public-domain Met sheets, the most worked window of each): key L* 83 ± 6, spread 11 ± 4, p5 L* 60, darkest (0.1%) L* 42 ± 9. A graphite hyperrealist works far darker and fuller than the academic graphite tradition.
- **Shadows** keep the photograph's soft penumbras and dark contact shadows (**judgement**, from photographic optics; the test drawing renders them).

## 6. Drawing and form
- **Transfer, not freehand construction:** the outline comes from the photograph by "a mechanical or semi-mechanical means" (Photorealism definition, Wikipedia), "projecting the slide onto the canvas or ... traditional grid techniques" (same); hyperrealists "make allowances for some mechanical means of transferring images" including "photographic slide projections or multi media projectors" and gridding (Wikipedia, Hyperrealism). Grid: matching grids of **equal ratio** on photo and sheet, copied square by square, drawn "very lightly" and erased later ([Art is Fun, the grid method](https://www.art-is-fun.com/grid-method)). Tracing with a graphite-backed print is also used (Creative Bloq).
- **Form is tone, not line.** No contour lines survive: edges are where two tones meet, "crisp and lost edges as in a photo" (task brief; Wikipedia's "range of focus"). **Judgement:** the transferred outline is light enough to vanish under the tone.

## 7. Composition
- **The photograph's composition:** close crops, extreme close-ups, objects and faces filling the frame, shallow focus (Wikipedia, Hyperrealism: depth of field, range of focus). Classic studio tests: an egg or sphere under one light, crumpled paper, an eye, metal or glass (**judgement**, common practice; the test drawing uses an egg and crumpled paper).
- Perspective is the lens's, not constructed (Wikipedia: "perspective" among the photographic limitations used).

## 8. Marks and edges
- **Marks invisible at viewing distance:** many light layers, crossed in different directions, then blended. Build tone "gradually ... two or three light passes instead of one heavy pass" (Vitruvian Studio); treat blending as a supplement and blend "in stages" (Wildlife Art Store, blending).
- **Hard over soft:** "Always hard over soft"; a harder grade's clay "can get into those pits", smoothing and solidifying a soft layer (2H over 2B: two grades harder) ([Sibley, layer and burnish](https://sibleyfineart.com/draw-with-mike-videos/ds005-layer-burnish.htm)). Soft grades leave pits open; soft over hard "often impossible" ([Sibley, soft over hard](https://sibleyfineart.com/_blog/2012/05/)).
- **Blenders** ([Wildlife Art Store, blending tools](https://www.wildlifeartstore.com/drawing-tools-for-blending-pencil/)): stumps and tortillons (point for small areas, side for large; light pressure, since pressing forces graphite into the paper where it is harder to erase); makeup and fan brushes ("soften graphite with very little contact", "smooth tonal transitions"); chamois (smooths broad areas and picks graphite up; less control). A soft brush applies powder and softens hatching (Creative Bloq). Overblending "can flatten a drawing" (Wildlife Art Store).
- **Lights are lifted and reserved:** kneaded and mechanical erasers lift (Creative Bloq; Met: graphite "can be lifted with erasers"). An eraser pen gives very thin lines from a freshly cut edge; a battery eraser, sharpened, makes a line "as thin as human hair" and removes graphite "almost perfectly" ([Wildlife Art Store, white lines](https://www.wildlifeartstore.com/white-lines-in-a-pencil-drawing/)).
- **Indented white lines** (whiskers, hairs, stitches): grooves pressed in before any shading, which "later drawing skips over and leaves as pristine white lines" with "clean, sharp edges", where an erased line is never pure white ([Sibley, indenting explained](https://sibleyfineart.com/_blog/?p=780)); a smooth rounded stylus, even pressure (more pressure, wider), smooth paper on a hard surface; indent before shading; the line stays white "even under heavier shading" if well made, but cannot be erased ([Sibley, indenting tutorial](https://sibleyfineart.com/tutorial--indenting.htm)).
- **Edges:** sharp in the focal plane, lost and soft out of focus (Wikipedia: depth of field); hard-edge share of a photograph 0.17 ± 0.07, against 0.03 in the finished 19th-century drawings (measured, section 13).

## 9. Process (the order of work)
1. **Photograph** the subject (or choose one); the drawing is made from it (artnet: "I always draw from a photograph"). [the schema: `ref` a photograph or a rendered scene]
2. **Transfer** the outline by grid, projection or tracing, lightly (Wikipedia; Art is Fun; Creative Bloq). [`transfer` (graphite: a 2H pencil tracing)]
3. **Indent** the white lines before any tone (Sibley). [`strokes` with `stylus`]
4. **The darks first, in charcoal,** built in cycles: powder brushed on as a base, compressed charcoal, matte workable fixative between layers, "the darker you need it the more layers you will build up" (Desdain Art); the fixative gives "an artificial tooth that will accept additional shading" (Sibley). [`charcoal_powder`, `compressed_charcoal`, a soft `brush` inside the passage, `finish fixative {"kind": "workable"}`; repeat]
5. **Lay the rest of the tone in layers** ("there are no rules", though the darks are best decided early: Sibley), hard over soft to fill the tooth (Sibley), powder and brush for broad areas (Creative Bloq). [`hatch` with mechanical pencils per value band, darkest first, clipped off the charcoal darks; `glaze` for powder]
6. **Blend in stages** (Wildlife Art Store), masking edges. [`hatch` with `brush`, `tortillon`, `chamois`, `clip: true`]
7. **Correct against the photograph,** layer and blend again, until it "looks like the photograph" (artnet). [rounds of `match` (value-seeking) and blending; `report`]
8. **Lift the lights** with kneaded, pen and battery erasers (Creative Bloq; Wildlife Art Store). [`match` with `kneaded`, `precision_eraser`, `electric_eraser` and `"seek": true`]
9. **Last darkest accents** (charcoal or carbon), then optional fixative (graphite needs little; spray coatings can darken values: Vitruvian Studio). [`compressed_charcoal`, `carbon_pencil`, `finish accents`, `finish fixative`]
- **Time:** about 200 hours over 25 days for one large drawing (My Modern Met); "two to three weeks to two to three months" per work (artnet).

## 10. Finishing
- **Finished means:** it looks like the photograph (artnet): no visible strokes at viewing distance, smooth gradients, the full value range the media allow, crisp edges in the focal plane and lost edges out of it, photographic texture. **Measured stand-ins** (section 13): flat passages with fine grain about 0.8 ± 0.3 L* and 0.5-5 mm mottle 1.6 ± 0.4 L* in the finished Met drawings (a smooth graphite surface can be smoother still); a photograph's key, spread and edges as above.
- **Last stage:** highlights lifted last, darkest accents, edges checked, margins cleaned; fixative optional (it dulls the shine and can darken: Vitruvian Studio; `docs/FINISHING.md`).

## 11. Pitfalls for Teodor
- **The airbrushed plastic look:** overblended, flattened tone with no texture (Wildlife Art Store warns overblending flattens a drawing). Keep photographic texture where the photo has it (paper fibre, skin).
- **Graphite glare in the darks** from forcing soft grades (Vitruvian Studio): past about L* 40 the darks are charcoal, laid first (charcoal won't take on unfixed soft graphite: Wildlife Art Store; it covers fixed graphite: Desdain Art).
- **Outline left showing**, or hatching visible at viewing distance: the hand must disappear.
- **Copying instead of seeing:** the critic's question is not "is it accurate" but whether the photograph's feeling survives (**judgement**). Nearest wrong feelings: clinical coldness, a technical demonstration with no subject.
- **Engine:** see section 12 and `docs/calibration/graphite-hyperrealism.md`.

## 12. Engine mapping
- **Medium** `graphite`, support `vellum` (or `plate`, `hot_press`), ground `#f7f6f2`, size 2400 x 3200 or larger (48 x 64 cm at 5 px/mm). Schema: a photograph or a rendered scene, greyscale.
- **Tools** (`docs/manual/graphite.md`): `mech_03`, `mech_05`, `mech_07` (fixed width, grade capped by lead), pencils `grade` 2H-8B, `compressed_charcoal`, `charcoal_powder`, `carbon_pencil`, `brush`, `tortillon`, `stump`, `chamois`, `kneaded`, `precision_eraser`, `electric_eraser`, `stylus`.
- **Intentions:** `transfer`; `hatch` in value bands (one `hatch` per band with `layers: [band]` and the band's value as colour, darkest first, `clip` per part); `hatch` with a blender (`value: 0`, `layers: [1.0]`, spacing a third of the size) as an even blending pass; `match` with a pencil for corrections and with an eraser plus `"seek": true` to lift lights to the schema's value.
- **Felt bias:** arousal -0.2, tension 0.0, care +0.4 (**judgement**: slow, steady, deliberate).
- **Gaps:** value bands step at their edges (core `hatch` has no feathering at a layer's boundary); `match` leaves dashes that need blending; per-part masks have to be written as polygons; no movable light for the sheen; graphite alone cannot reach a photograph's blacks (charcoal laid first reaches about L* 19; a measured modern charcoal sheet is still missing); a canvas larger than 2400 x 3200 is slow (timings in the calibration note).
