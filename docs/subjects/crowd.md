# Crowd (layer 1)

Groups and crowds as they really are: people in small social groups, spaced by relationship and density, sharing a ground plane and an eye level, read by the viewer as a whole before any one face. Researched 2026-11. Every value names its source; values marked **judgement** have no published source and are editorial choices for Teodor. Read with `figure`, `face` and `space`.

## 1. What it is, structurally

**A crowd is made of groups, not individuals.** In video studies of pedestrians, about 55 % of people at a low-density site and 70 % at a busier one walked with others; groups of two to four are the most frequent and larger groups are rare (sizes fit a zero-truncated Poisson distribution) ([Moussaïd et al. 2010, *PLoS ONE*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1003.3894)). So a believable crowd is mostly pairs and threes with some singles, not a field of separate people.

**Group shapes.**
- **Walking:** at low density group members walk side by side, abreast; as density rises the line bends forward: a group of three forms a V with the middle person a step back, four form a U; groups walk slower than individuals, and speed falls as group size grows (Moussaïd et al. 2010; densities studied 0.03 and 0.25 people/m²).
- **Standing and talking:** people form an F-formation around a shared empty space (the o-space) to which they have "equal, direct, and exclusive access": two people vis-à-vis, in an L at right angles, or side by side; three or more in a rough circle ([Setti et al., F-formation detection, after Kendon](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.2702)). Bodies turn toward the shared space; outsiders stand outside the ring.
- **An audience** has a shared focus instead: bodies and faces turn toward one thing, and the F-formations dissolve or shrink to pairs (**judgement**).

**Spacing by relationship** (Hall's proxemic zones, [Wikipedia, Proxemics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxemics)): intimate 0–0.46 m (embracing, whispering), personal 0.46–1.22 m (friends, family), social 1.2–3.7 m (acquaintances), public 3.7 m and beyond. In a painting, the gap between two figures tells their relationship before their faces do (**judgement**).

**Density** (people per m², [G. Keith Still, static crowd density](https://gkstill.com/Support/crowd-density/CrowdDensity-1.html)): about 1/m² is a loose crowd; event safety limits are "typically assessed at 2 people per square metre"; 4/m² for a moving queue; 4.7/m² the UK Green Guide figure for standing viewing; 5/m² "should be the UPPER limit for standing/viewing spaces"; 6–10/m² is crush territory. Still notes "crowds rarely pack in regular formation" and that density looks higher from lower viewpoints. Rough visual equivalents (**judgement**): below 1/m², ground visible around everyone; 2/m², people at arm's length, much overlap at eye level; 4–5/m², shoulders touching, no ground visible; above 5/m², bodies pressed, swaying as one.

**Heads on the eye level.** On flat ground, when the viewer stands, "all the heads align on the same horizontal line", the viewer's eye level, at any distance ([Stephanie Bower](https://stephaniebower.blogspot.com/2018/07/310-tips-heads-align-at-your-eye-level.html); also [Sketchaway](https://sketchaway.substack.com/p/sketching-crowds) and [Outdoor Painter](https://www.outdoorpainter.com/tips-painting-figures-landscape/)). The exceptions are what make it alive: children, seated, stooping or bending people, and real height differences (Sketchaway). If the viewer sits, the eye level falls: "the people closest to me have heads that appear the farthest above my eye level" (Bower). The general rule (**judgement**, follows from linear perspective): the horizon cuts every standing figure on the same ground at the same body height, the viewer's eye height; nearer figures are simply larger.

**Scale.** Distant figures are smaller in proportion to distance (Outdoor Painter); a figure must fit its surroundings: "a figure near a house should not be so large that it cannot fit through the door" (same source).

## 2. Seen from different viewpoints

- **Eye level:** heads line up; bodies overlap heavily below; only the nearest row shows whole figures; the crowd reads as a band of heads and shoulders over a tangle of bodies and legs (Bower; **judgement** for the description).
- **High view:** the ground shows between people, so groups, gaps and paths become visible as a pattern; each figure is a separate shape seen partly from above (head and shoulders large, legs foreshortened). The Kunsthistorisches Museum notes that seeing *Children's Games* from above was "the only way Bruegel could render visible the impressive crowd" of over 230 children playing 83 games ([KHM](https://khm.at/en/object/321)); Bruegel here is an example of observation, not a rule.
- **Low view:** the nearest heads rise above the horizon and against the sky; distant heads sit near the horizon; overlap is total and the crowd becomes a wall (Bower; Still on lower angles looking denser).
- **Distance:** near figures carry individual faces and gestures; middle figures carry head shapes, posture and clothing colour; far figures are simplified silhouettes and finally a broken band of head shapes (Sketchaway: "more detail on those closer to you"; background figures can be simple silhouettes).

## 3. Under light

- **The crowd is one value mass first.** Squinted, a crowd reads as one or two shapes with broken top edges (the heads) and a dark base (bodies and their shadows); individual figures emerge only where light picks them out (**judgement**, consistent with the value-first teaching in `light-shadow`).
- **Lights on tops.** Light from above catches heads, shoulders and hats; a touch of light on heads and shoulders helps figures stand out, especially silhouettes against light (Outdoor Painter).
- **Cast shadows** tie figures to the ground and to each other; in low sun they become a striped rhythm across the ground (**judgement**). Without contact shadows the figures float.
- **Backlight** turns a crowd into overlapping silhouettes with rim-lit heads and shoulders; front light flattens it into a colourful pattern (**judgement**).
- **Depth.** Rows farther back lose contrast and saturation and move toward the air's colour (atmospheric perspective, `light-shadow`), and edges between figures soften (**judgement** for crowd scale; the principle is sourced in `light-shadow`).
- **Local colour.** Clothing breaks the crowd into many local colours; keep them inside the value plan so the mass still reads (**judgement**).

## 4. Variation

- **Kind of crowd** (**judgement**, from the structure above): strollers (sparse, groups of 2–4 abreast, mixed directions); a market (milling, many directions, F-formations around stalls); a queue (a line at personal distance, everyone facing one way); an audience (shared focus, dense, faces turned one way); a march or procession (shared direction and rhythm of steps, banners); a celebration (dense, arms up, groups dissolving); panic or crush (above 5/m², bodies pressed, faces strained, no free space: Still's limits).
- **People.** Ages, heights, builds, clothing, hair and posture all differ; children's heads fall below the eye line (Sketchaway). Include a few distinctive characters: "I always draw in at least a few characters that either speak to me or speak to the place" (Sketchaway).
- **Weather and time.** Umbrellas, coats and hunched shoulders in rain; shade-seeking clusters in heat; long shadows in evening light (**judgement**).
- **Motion.** In a moving crowd most people share a direction, with some cross-flow; walking groups bend into V and U shapes as density rises (Moussaïd et al.).

## 5. How painters observe and construct it

- **Gesture first, heads and shoulders carry it.** Start with a stick figure and add structure once the gesture is right; heads and shoulders, correctly placed and sized, let the rest of the body stay loose; "a couple of quick drybrush strokes for the legs are often enough to indicate movement" ([Outdoor Painter](https://www.outdoorpainter.com/tips-painting-figures-landscape/)).
- **Overlap and depth.** Avoid neat rows of static figures: "lots of overlap and dynamic action help"; place people at varying depths ([Sketchaway](https://sketchaway.substack.com/p/sketching-crowds)). First sketches look emptier than the scene felt: keep adding people until it matches the energy (same).
- **Integration.** Paint figures into their surroundings wet-into-wet, letting surrounding colour run into them; soft edges avoid a pasted-on look (Outdoor Painter).
- **How the viewer reads a crowd.** People extract the average emotion of a group of faces quickly and automatically, even when they cannot report individual identities, and summarise gaze direction, gender and average identity the same way ([Whitney Lab, Ensemble perception](https://whitneylab.berkeley.edu/research/ensemble.html)). So the crowd's mood comes from what most of its members do (posture, direction, expression), and individuality comes from a few characters who differ from the average (**judgement** for the painting consequence).
- **Order of work** (**judgement**, consistent with the above):
  1. Fix the eye level and the ground plane; decide density and kind of crowd.
  2. Mass the crowd as value shapes, with the top edge broken by heads.
  3. Place groups (mostly 2–4, with F-formations or walking shapes), gaps and paths; vary spacing by relationship.
  4. Paint the near characters with individual faces, hands and gestures; the middle with heads, posture and colour; the far as silhouettes and head shapes.
  5. Tie them down with contact and cast shadows; push the far rows back with lower contrast.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Clones:** the same height, build, pose, face and clothing repeated; Teodor's "generic mannequin people" habit (`docs/habits.md`). Viewers read the average of a crowd, so clones don't add up to a crowd; they read as a pattern (**judgement**, from ensemble perception).
- **Grid or row spacing:** people evenly spaced like plants in a bed, or in neat rows (Sketchaway; Still: crowds rarely pack regularly).
- **Everyone facing the viewer:** a crowd that poses for the picture instead of facing each other or a shared focus; Teodor's "frontal, stiff bodies" habit (**judgement** with F-formations as the corrective).
- **Floating heads:** heads above or below the eye level with no reason (child, seated, stooped), so figures float or sink (Bower).
- **Wrong scale:** a distant figure too big for the doorway beside it (Outdoor Painter).
- **Pasted figures:** hard cut edges, no colour shared with the surroundings, no cast shadows (Outdoor Painter).
- **Detail everywhere:** every face resolved to the back row; or the opposite, a crowd painted as an empty block of one colour (Teodor's "big empty block" habit).
- **A single expression:** every face with the same mask; real crowds share a mood with variation (**judgement**).

## 7. Checks for Teodor

1. The horizon (eye level) is fixed, and the heads of standing adults on the same ground sit on it, or at a constant body height relative to it; exceptions are explained (children, seated, stooping).
2. The crowd is built of groups: mostly 2–4, spaced by relationship (Hall's zones), facing each other or a shared focus; not a grid, not all frontal.
3. Figure size falls with distance consistently; check one figure against a door or another known size.
4. Detail falls off with depth: individual faces and hands only in front; head shapes and posture in the middle; silhouettes behind.
5. At least a few characters differ clearly from the average (age, build, gesture, colour); no two neighbours share a pose.
6. Contact and cast shadows tie every figure to the ground; edges between figures are partly lost.
7. Squint: the crowd reads as one designed value mass with a broken top edge, not as scattered dots or one flat block.
