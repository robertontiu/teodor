# Flowers and fruit (layer 1: the subject as it really is)

Flowers and fruit as painted subjects: how a bloom is built, how it turns in space, how petals and skins take the light, and how flower painters have actually worked. Trees, leaves and grass in the landscape are in `trees-plants.md`; vases, cloth and tabletops in `objects.md`.

## 1. What it is, structurally
- **A flower is whorls of modified leaves.** From the outside in: sepals (calyx), petals (corolla), stamens, carpels (pistil). Calyx and corolla together are the perianth ([LibreTexts, Botany lab manual, anatomy of a flower](https://bio.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Botany/Botany_Lab_Manual_(Morrow)/23%3A_Angiosperms_I_-_Flowers/23.05%3A_Anatomy_of_a_Flower)).
- **Count the parts.** Flowers with parts in threes (3 or 6 petals: lily, tulip, iris) are generally monocots; parts in fours or fives (rose, buttercup, poppy's 4, apple blossom's 5) are generally eudicots (same source). A painted flower with the wrong petal number reads as invented to anyone who knows it.
- **Symmetry.** Radially symmetric (actinomorphic) flowers have several lines of symmetry (daisy, rose, tulip); bilaterally symmetric (zygomorphic) flowers have one (orchid, pea, snapdragon, foxglove) (same source).
- **Spirals.** Leaves, seeds and florets are usually placed at about 137.5° (the golden angle) from one another; about 92% of plants with spiral phyllotaxis show Fibonacci numbers ([Smith College, Phyllotaxis](https://www.science.smith.edu/phyllo/About/FiboPhyllo.html)). A sunflower head shows, for example, 34 spirals one way and 55 the other; in a citizen-science count, 74% of 768 spiral families were Fibonacci numbers and two-thirds of clockwise/anticlockwise pairs were consecutive Fibonacci numbers, so not every head obeys ([Plus Magazine, Turing's sunflowers](https://plus.maths.org/sunflowers)).
- **Composite flowers** (daisy, sunflower, aster) are many small florets: a disc of tubular florets in a spiral, ringed by ray florets that look like petals (**judgement**, standard botany; the LibreTexts page defers it to a later section).
- **Basic solids** (common teaching): disc (daisy, open rose seen from the front), cup or bowl (tulip, poppy), cone or trumpet (lily, daffodil trumpet, morning glory), sphere or ball (peony bud, hydrangea head), spike (lupin, foxglove) (**judgement**, construction practice; the circle and cone are named in the [California Native Plant Society handout](https://northinlet.sc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/cnps_drawing-flowers.pdf)).
- **Stem, leaf and bloom are one system:** the stem enters the flower at the centre of the receptacle, on the axis of the cup; leaves attach at nodes and follow the plant's phyllotaxis (**judgement**).
- **Fruit** are simple solids (sphere, ovoid, cylinder) with a stem cavity, a blossom end, and skins that are glossy (apple, cherry, tomato), matte (peach fuzz), waxy (grape, plum, blueberry bloom) or pitted (citrus). Plum and grape bloom is epicuticular wax, "often visible to the naked eye as a white, dull, or glossy coating" ([Trivedi et al., Frontiers in Plant Science 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpls.2019.00431/full)).

## 2. Seen from different viewpoints
- **Circles become ellipses.** "If you foreshorten a circle, you see an ellipse. Similarly, if you foreshorten a flower that is based on a circle, the flower makes an ellipse" (CNPS handout). The ring of petal tips, the rim of a cup, the disc of a daisy all follow ellipses whose minor axis lies along the flower's axis.
- **Where the centre goes.** "The center of the flower stays in the middle of the flower" for flat flowers; "in a cone-shaped flower, the center of the flower drops as the flower rotates" (CNPS handout).
- **Draw front to back:** overlapping petals and leaves are drawn from the nearest one back; a flat-looking leaf or petal improves by strengthening "the line's edge that is coming toward you" (CNPS handout).
- **Profile views** show the calyx, the curve of the stem into the bloom and the depth of the cup; front views show the symmetry and the centre. A bunch where every bloom faces the viewer looks like wallpaper (**judgement**).
- **Petals curl:** a petal is a curved sheet; where it turns, its edge shows as a thin rim, often lighter, and its two faces differ in value (**judgement**).

## 3. Under light
- **Translucency.** Petals and thin leaves are translucent: lit from behind they glow with a more saturated colour than from the front, and petals cast coloured shadows into each other (**judgement**; the same transmitted-light principle as leaves).
- **Each bloom is one form.** Model the whole cup, sphere or cone first (lit side, shadow side, core), then the individual petals within it; petals inside a cup are in its shadow (**judgement**).
- **White flowers are never white everywhere:** their shadows take colour from the surroundings and the light; their lightest light is reserved for a few planes facing the source (**judgement**).
- **Fruit:** glossy skins show a sharp specular highlight (the light source's shape) and a reflected light on the shadow side; waxy bloom scatters light and lowers the highlight into a soft, bluish sheen; velvety skins (peach) have a soft light rim at the silhouette and no sharp highlight (**judgement**).
- **Directional light from one side** is the still-life convention because it models every bloom: in a Dutch bouquet, "light shining from the left both accents individual blossoms and models their forms" ([National Gallery of Art, *From botany to bouquets*](https://www.nga.gov/content/dam/ngaweb/research/publications/pdfs/from-botany-to-bouquets.pdf)).

## 4. Variation
- **Life cycle:** bud (closed, a sphere or spindle with the sepals tight), opening (cup), full bloom (open, petals relaxed and spreading), past (petals drop, curl, brown at the edges, stamen dusting pollen), seed head. Cut flowers droop: the heaviest heads bend their stems first (**judgement**).
- **Species** change petal count, symmetry and solid (see section 1).
- **Arrangement:** a natural plant grows from one root with all blooms on one phyllotactic plan; a bouquet mixes species and directions. Seventeenth-century Dutch painters built bouquets from separate studies, sometimes reusing the same flower reversed in different paintings, and combined flowers that "did not blossom during the same season"; "few Dutch still lifes were painted from life," and they sought "a diverse array of differently shaped and colored flowers" for interest (NGA, *From botany to bouquets*). Those are conventions of the genre, not of nature (see layer 2).
- **Fruit ripeness:** colour shifts (green to yellow or red), softening, wrinkling and bruising; bloom rubs off where fruit is handled (**judgement**).
- **Weather:** rain beads on waxy petals and leaves; wind turns blooms away and shows their backs (**judgement**).

## 5. How painters observe and construct it
1. **Gesture of the whole arrangement** (the big shape of the bouquet or plant), then the direction of each bloom's axis as a line.
2. **Each bloom as its solid:** disc, cup, cone, sphere, with the ellipse of its opening on the axis line (CNPS handout).
3. **Count and place the petals** on that ellipse; overlapping petals front to back.
4. **Stems into the centres,** leaves at the nodes.
5. **Mass the values** (light, shadow, and the cast shadows between blooms) before any petal detail.
6. **Detail at the focal bloom;** others are simplified, and some petals lose their edges into the background (**judgement**).
7. **Study single flowers from life.** Dutch painters worked from individual drawings and studies (Bosschaert "composed his paintings with the aid of such drawings"; Brueghel painted rare flowers "directly from nature": NGA). Dürer's watercolour plant studies and Redouté's botanical plates are examples of close observation.

## 6. Common mistakes
- **Clip-art flowers:** five identical rounded petals around a dot, all facing the viewer; the wrong petal count for the species.
- **No ellipses:** turned flowers drawn as circles; stems that miss the centre of the bloom.
- **Petals as flat cut-outs** without curl, thickness at the turn, or a shadow inside the cup.
- **Every bloom equally detailed and equally sharp;** no lost edges.
- **White flowers painted with pure white** throughout, and shadows grey.
- **Fruit as billiard balls:** perfect spheres, the same highlight on every skin, no stem cavity or blossom end, no cast and contact shadows.
- **Leaves as identical tear-drops** stuck on; no turning, no undersides.

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. Each flower's petal count and symmetry fits its species (threes for lilies and tulips; fours or fives for most others; one mirror line for orchids and peas).
2. Every turned bloom sits on an ellipse whose minor axis lies along the stem's direction; stems enter the centre.
3. Blooms face several directions; at least one is seen in profile or from behind.
4. Each bloom is modelled as one form first (lit side, shadow side), with petals inside a cup in shadow.
5. The skins differ: glossy fruit has a sharp highlight; waxy fruit a soft bloom; velvet a soft rim.
6. Detail and hard edges are concentrated at the focal bloom; some edges are lost.
