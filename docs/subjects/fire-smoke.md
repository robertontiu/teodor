# Fire and smoke (layer 1: the subject as it really is)

Fire, smoke, steam and candlelight as subjects: the structure of a flame, its colours and motion, the plume above it, smoke's changing colour against light and dark, the visible and invisible parts of steam, and fire as the light source of a scene. How firelight falls on other things is also in `light-shadow.md`.

## 1. What it is, structurally

**A flame is glowing gas and soot, shaped by buoyancy.**
- **Candle flame zones:** unburned wax vapour near the wick (a dark core), a bright yellow middle of incandescent soot, a blue base where soot is sparse and excited CH and C₂ radicals emit, and water vapour above. Most of the flame is about 1,100 °C, with hot spots of 1,300–1,400 °C. The yellow is "incandescence of very fine soot particles," radiating like a blackbody ([Wikipedia, Flame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flame)).
- **Shape comes from gravity:** hot gas rises, so a flame is drawn up into a teardrop; without convection (in microgravity) it becomes spherical and bluer (same source).
- **Colour scale of hot things** (glowing embers, coals, metal) ([Wikipedia, Red heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_heat), from 19th–20th-century engineering tables):

| appearance | approx. °C |
|---|---|
| just visible red | ~525 |
| dull red | ~700 |
| cherry red | 800–900 |
| orange | 1,000–1,200 |
| yellow | 1,100–1,250 |
| white | 1,300 and above |

So the deep, dull reds of a fire are its coolest glowing parts (embers at the edge, dying coals); the hottest centre of a bed of coals and the base of a big fire are yellow to near white. Cooler, oxygen-starved house-fire flames are red, 600–850 °C (Wikipedia, Flame).

**A fire has three zones** above the fuel (McCaffrey's buoyant-flame measurements, [NIST NBSIR 79-1910](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nbsir79-1910.pdf)):
1. **continuous flame** at the bottom, of constant temperature, up to about 0.08 Q^(2/5) m (Q, heat release in kW);
2. **intermittent flame** above, where tongues of flame break off and the flame "is intermittent in appearance," up to about 0.20 Q^(2/5) m;
3. **buoyant plume,** non-luminous hot gas and smoke above that.
For a 33 kW fire (a small campfire) these boundaries are about 0.3 m and 0.8 m (same source). The visible flame's top reaches about 2.5 times the height of its steady base, so the flickering part is about 1.5 times as tall as the steady part (arithmetic from 0.20/0.08).

**Flames flicker at a predictable rate.** Buoyancy rolls the hot gas into toroidal vortices that pinch the flame off and release a puff; the frequency is f ≈ 0.48 √(g/d), about 1.5/√d Hz with d the fire's diameter in metres: about 10 Hz for a 2 cm flame, about 1.5 Hz for a 1 m fire ([Xia & Zhang, vortex-dynamical interpretation of flickering flames](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.10411)). A big fire's tongues rise and break off slowly; a small flame quivers.

**Smoke** is "the cloud-like product of burning" ([eclecticlight, *There's no smoke without painting*](https://eclecticlight.co/2019/11/23/theres-no-smoke-without-painting-1/)).
- **Plume:** near the source the stream is smooth (laminar); it speeds up as it rises and breaks into turbulent eddies ([University of Illinois, Q&A: cigar smoke and fluid dynamics](https://van.physics.illinois.edu/qa/listing.php?id=2179)). The plume widens as it rises and mixes with air; wind bends it over and strings it out downwind (**judgement**).
- **Colour depends on viewing direction.** Fine smoke particles "scatter blue light very efficiently": with the light behind you, smoke looks blue; looking toward the sun or a bright sky you see light that has lost its blue, and smoke looks yellowish, "a dirty, yellowish brown"; when moisture swells the particles they scatter all colours and the smoke turns white ([Irish Times, *Several shades of smoke*](https://www.irishtimes.com/news/several-shades-of-smoke-1.169228); [SDSU, Atmospheric scattering](https://aty.sdsu.edu/explain/optics/scatter.html)). Soot-laden smoke absorbs as well as scatters, so heavy smoke is grey to black (SDSU; [WMO, smoke](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/smoke.html)).
- **At a distance:** smoke from nearby cities can be brown, dark grey or black; thick forest-fire smoke turns the sky greenish-yellow; smoke from far away is light grey or bluish; through smoke the sun is "very red" at sunrise and sunset and orange when high (WMO, smoke).

**Steam.** The clear gap just above a kettle spout "is the actual steam," invisible; the white cloud beyond it is what steam turns into when it meets cooler air: tiny droplets ([Indiana Public Media, *A Moment of Science: Clouds in the kitchen*](https://www.ipm.org/show/amomentofscience/2019-09-19/clouds-in-the-kitchen)). Steam and breath are white like clouds, they dissipate quickly in dry air and linger in cold, humid air (**judgement**).

## 2. Seen from different viewpoints
- **Fire is the same from any side** in shape, but its value against the background is not: against night it is the brightest thing in view; in daylight a flame is pale and almost invisible, and only the heat shimmer and smoke show (**judgement**).
- **From above** you see the bed of coals, its colour gradient from yellow-white centre to dull red edge; **from the side** the flames dominate (**judgement**).
- **Smoke columns** in the distance lean with the wind and flatten where they meet a stable layer, spreading as a level sheet (**judgement**; the WMO notes smoke in the free atmosphere has no discernible outline).
- **Candle at close range** shows the zones; at room distance only the yellow teardrop and a halo (**judgement**).

## 3. Under light
- **Fire is a light source,** not a lit object: it has no form shadow. Its colour temperature is low: candle flame about 1,800–2,000 K ([Engineering ToolBox](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/color-temperature-d_1776.html); [MFA CAMEO](https://cameo.mfa.org/wiki/Color_temperature) gives 1,800–2,900 K), far warmer than daylight (about 6,500 K).
- **Firelight falls off fast.** Light from a small source drops with the square of the distance (inverse-square law): an object twice as far from the candle gets a quarter of the light (physics). So a candle-lit scene has a bright pool around the flame and darkness a short distance away; faces near it are lit from below or the side, with the shadows cast upward (**judgement** for the staging consequences).
- **Firelight flickers:** shadows in a fire-lit room waver at the flame's flicker rate, and their edges are soft for a large fire and sharp for a candle (source size) (**judgement**).
- **The flame's own values:** the brightest part is near white and should be the lightest value in a night picture; the colour saturates toward the edges and the tips (orange, then red), and the core near the wick is darker (Wikipedia, Flame). A flame painted all one yellow reads as a sticker.
- **Glow:** around a bright flame the eye and the air spread a halo, warm-coloured and fading outward (**judgement**).
- **Smoke takes the light around it:** lit from below by fire it glows orange; under moonlight it is cool grey; against a bright sky it is darker than the sky; against dark trees lighter and bluer (see the colour rule above; [eclecticlight](https://eclecticlight.co/2019/11/23/theres-no-smoke-without-painting-1/) for paintings of furnace smoke lit from below and smoke softened by moonlight).

## 4. Variation
- **Fuel:** candle (steady, small, yellow, a single teardrop), wood fire (many tongues, intermittent, orange-yellow with sparks), coal and embers (glow with little flame, red to yellow-white), oil and tyre fires (sooty, red, heavy black smoke), gas (blue, little soot) (Wikipedia, Flame; fuel list **judgement**).
- **Size:** larger fires flicker more slowly and break into bigger tongues (flicker law above).
- **Wind:** flames lean and stretch downwind; smoke streams flat; sparks trail (**judgement**).
- **Stage:** kindling (much white smoke, little flame), full burn (tall flames, less smoke), embers (glow, no flame, thin blue smoke) (**judgement**).
- **Smoke moisture:** damp fuel gives white, billowing smoke; dry hot fires give thin, bluish smoke (Irish Times on moisture swelling the particles; application **judgement**).
- **Steam:** cold air makes long, dense plumes; warm dry air makes short ones that vanish (**judgement**).

## 5. How painters observe and construct it
1. **Fix the light role first:** in a fire-lit scene, decide where the flame is, how far its light reaches (inverse square), and keep everything outside that pool dark.
2. **Paint the flame from the darkest to the lightest:** the red-orange outer envelope, then yellow, then the near-white core last, with the darker zone near a candle's wick (Wikipedia, Flame).
3. **Shape the flame as tongues** of the continuous base plus detached pieces above it; vary the lengths; tips curl and break off (McCaffrey's zones).
4. **Smoke as a mass with soft edges,** smooth near the source and broken into eddies higher up; decide its colour by what is behind it and where the light comes from.
5. **Edges:** flames have soft outer edges but a sharper inner contrast between core and envelope; smoke edges are lost in places and found in others (**judgement**).
6. **Observe from life:** a candle in a dark room, a campfire at dusk, a kettle. Paintings named as examples of observation: Georges de La Tour's candlelit interiors (light pool and fall-off), Joseph Wright of Derby's firelit scenes, de Loutherbourg's *Coalbrookdale by Night* (furnace smoke lit from below), Turner's *Burning of the Houses of Lords and Commons*.

## 6. Common mistakes
- **Fire as a stylised icon:** a symmetrical teardrop with three points, uniform orange with a yellow middle, hard outline.
- **Wrong colour order:** dark red at the hottest centre, or a flame with no near-white core in a night scene.
- **Firelight that reaches too far:** the whole room evenly lit by one candle; no fall-off; shadows cast in the wrong direction.
- **Smoke as grey cotton or a solid tube:** uniform colour, hard edges, no change from laminar to turbulent, the same tone against light and dark backgrounds.
- **Steam starting right at the spout,** with no clear gap.
- **Flames and smoke that disagree with the wind** in the rest of the picture.
- **Sparks everywhere:** a uniform sprinkle of dots instead of a few rising, trailing streaks (**judgement**).

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. The flame's colour runs from near-white core through yellow and orange to red at the tips, with a darker zone near a candle's wick; nothing in the flame is darker red than its tips.
2. Fire lights its surroundings by the inverse square: the lit pool is small, and brightness halves twice when distance doubles.
3. Flames have a continuous base and broken, detached tongues above; for a campfire the flames' overall height is a believable 0.5–1 m.
4. Smoke is smooth near its source and turbulent higher up; it is bluish where seen against dark with the light behind the viewer, yellowish-brown against bright sky, white when damp, grey-black when sooty.
5. Steam has a clear gap at the spout and fades in the air.
6. Flame, smoke and steam lean with the same wind as clouds, water and trees.
