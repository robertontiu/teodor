# Landscape (layer 1: the subject as it really is)

Terrain, rock, mountains, fields and paths: the ground as a set of planes that take the light differently, built by geology and worn by weather, and receding into the distance by perspective and atmosphere. Trees are in `trees-plants.md`, water in `water.md`, sky in `sky.md`; general perspective in `space.md` and light on form in `light-shadow.md`.

## 1. What it is, structurally
- **Four planes.** Carlson's theory of angles sorts the landscape by its angle to the sky: the sky (the light source) is lightest; flat ground is second ("the half-light plane"); slopes, hills and low mountains third, "receiving less light than the flat ground but more light than the vertical elements"; upright elements such as trees and cliffs darkest ([The Artist's Road, Carlson's theory of angles](https://theartistsroad.net/articles/thecarlsontheoryofangles)).
- **Rock is layered and fractured.** "Most rock is layered"; "rock layers usually maintain the same thickness, color, and texture across an outcrop, cliff face, or hillside"; fractures "create lines and linear depressions between intact blocks of rock" and "control the angle of tops and sides of cliffs and hillsides." "Fracture surfaces form the angular edges, while weathering creates the rounded edges." "Smooth, vertical cliffs are rare" ([Outdoor Painter, *Getting rock right in a landscape painting*](https://www.outdoorpainter.com/?p=17569), written by a geologist-painter). Ruskin calls these "the lines of force with which the real rocks have risen" and "the lines of couch in which they repose" ([*The Elements of Drawing*](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.264111/2015.264111.The-Elements_djvu.txt)).
- **Loose slopes have a maximum angle.** The angle of repose is about 34° for dry sand, 45° for wet sand and crushed stone, 25–30° for natural sandy gravel, 38° for snow ([Wikipedia, Angle of repose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_repose)). Scree is usually less steep than that, concave (steeper at the top than at the foot), and sorted with the largest blocks at the base ([Wikipedia, Scree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scree)). A painted talus fan steeper than about 40° reads as wrong (arithmetic from these values).
- **Mountains** are big rock forms: ridges, faces and gullies governed by the same layers and fractures, with scree at the foot of cliffs and snow lying on slopes gentler than its angle of repose (**judgement**, combining the above).
- **Rolling ground** is a sequence of overlapping convex and concave masses; Ruskin: "strive to get the retirement and succession of masses in irregular ground," helped by "the perspective diminutions of its herbage" and by shadows.
- **Fields and paths** are flat planes in perspective: crop rows, furrows, fences and the two edges of a road converge to vanishing points on the horizon (on level ground) or above or below it (on slopes) (**judgement**, linear perspective; see `space.md`). Paths and roads follow the contours: they wind around hills and disappear behind crests rather than going straight up them (**judgement**).
- **Texture gradient.** A textured ground "gets finer and appears smoother" as it recedes; the cobblestones in Caillebotte's *Paris Street; Rainy Day* shrink until they blur into roughness ([Hanover College, texture gradient](https://psychology.hanover.edu/Krantz/art/texture.html)).

## 2. Seen from different viewpoints
- **Eye level sets everything.** A low eye compresses the ground into thin horizontal bands and makes near objects tower; a high eye (from a hill) opens the ground as a map and shows the pattern of fields and roads (**judgement**).
- **Recession in bands:** foreground (detail, full colour and contrast), middle ground, distance (flat, pale shapes). Planes overlap; a far hill's silhouette is simpler than a near one (**judgement**, the usual three-plane teaching).
- **Shore and river curves** are the hardest perspective in nature: "every intelligent spectator will feel the difference between a rightly drawn bend of shore or shingle, and a false one"; there is no rule, only careful looking (Ruskin).
- **Aerial perspective** makes distant ground lighter, bluer, lower in contrast and less detailed (section 3).

## 3. Under light
- **Plane values** follow Carlson's order in sunlight. On a grey or misty day the four values draw much closer; snow can make the ground lighter than the sky (The Artist's Road).
- **Aerial perspective:** Leonardo: "if one is to be five times as distant, make it five times bluer" ([Notebooks, §295](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/Richter-NotebooksOfLeonardo/section-6/item-295.html)). Physically, scattered skylight adds a veiling luminance between object and eye, so contrast falls, fine detail goes first, colours lose saturation and shift toward the colour of the air: bluish in sunlight, reddish at sunrise and sunset ([Wikipedia, Aerial perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerial_perspective)). Distant rock "take[s] on a pale and bluish tinge" with softer edges ([University of Alberta, atmospheric perspective](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~mhirji/atmospheric-perspective.html)). Bright distant surfaces (sunlit snow, white cliffs) do not get lighter: they get duller and, in haze, warmer (**judgement**).
- **Rock colour:** weathering "usually lightens the color of dark rock and darkens the color of light rock," in proportion to exposure (Outdoor Painter). Each block has a top plane (light, takes sky colour), side planes (half-tone or shadow) and a cast shadow; cracks are the darkest accents (**judgement**).
- **Cloud shadows** move across the ground as large shapes that follow the terrain; they are the painter's main tool for alternating light and dark bands in depth (**judgement**). Overhead cloud shadows cover most of the visible ground, while "distant cloud shadows are more linear and horizontal" ([John Muir Laws, clouds in perspective](https://johnmuirlaws.com/how-to-draw-clouds-in-perspective/)).
- **Cast shadows on ground** describe its shape: Ruskin's note on how shadows of trunks on undulating ground "explain the form and distance of the earth on which they fall."
- **Shadow colour** outdoors takes the blue of the sky on the flat ground and warmer bounced light in vertical recesses (**judgement**; see `light-shadow.md`).

## 4. Variation
- **Rock type:** layered sedimentary rock (bands, ledges, blocky joints), massive granite (rounded domes, few large joints), volcanic (dark, columnar or rough) (**judgement**, broad geology; the layer and fracture rules above apply to all).
- **Season:** green growth, harvest gold and stubble, ploughed browns, snow that flattens forms and fills hollows; snow reverses the usual ground-sky value relationship (The Artist's Road).
- **Weather and hour:** haze compresses values; low sun rakes the ground and shows every undulation with long shadows; noon sun flattens it; after rain, roads and fields reflect the sky (**judgement**; see `sky.md` for the light colour).
- **Climate:** arid land shows bare rock and the geology; wet land hides it under vegetation (**judgement**).
- **Use:** fields are geometric and human; paths wear into the ground, with bare tracks, puddles in the ruts and grass in the middle strip (**judgement**).

## 5. How painters observe and construct it
1. **Place the horizon and eye level** first.
2. **Block the big planes** by angle (sky, flat, slope, upright) and give each its value group (Carlson); squint to check.
3. **Overlap the masses** front to back; keep far silhouettes simple.
4. **Apply aerial perspective** in steps: each band back gets lighter, bluer, lower contrast, softer.
5. **Draw the structure lines:** rock layers and fractures, field rows and path edges in perspective, shore curves by eye.
6. **Detail and texture only in the foreground,** diminishing with the texture gradient.
7. **Study from life.** Ruskin's advice: before mountains, "draw a piece of rounded rock, with its variegated lichens, quite rightly"; then "the forms of distant hills will be comparatively easy." Constable's oil sketches of fields and Cézanne's repeated studies of one mountain's planes are examples of sustained observation.

## 6. Common mistakes
- **Equal detail everywhere;** distant hills as sharp and saturated as the foreground; no aerial perspective.
- **Ground as a vertical wall:** foreground and distance painted the same size and texture, so the land doesn't lie down; fields with parallel rows that don't converge.
- **Rocks as lumpy potatoes or identical bricks:** no layers, no fracture planes, the same block size repeated, perfectly vertical smooth cliffs (Outdoor Painter).
- **Cartoon mountains:** symmetric cones or zigzags; scree steeper than about 40°; snow lying on vertical faces.
- **Ruskin's "rugged fragments":** a mountainside broken into random pieces, "wholly unconscious of the lines of force with which the real rocks have risen."
- **Paths drawn as ribbons on top of the land,** going straight up hills, the same width all the way.
- **Plane values out of order** without a reason (tree lighter than the ground in sun).

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. Squint view: sky lightest, flat ground next, slopes next, uprights darkest (unless snow, backlight or mist explain otherwise).
2. Each step back in depth is lighter (for dark things), bluer, lower in contrast and less detailed; the farthest plane has the softest edges.
3. Converging lines (rows, furrows, path edges, fences) on level ground meet on the horizon line.
4. Rock shows layering and fracture planes with varied block sizes; loose slopes are no steeper than about 35–40°, concave, with big blocks at the foot.
5. Texture size decreases steadily with distance; no foreground texture in the distance.
6. Cast and cloud shadows follow the ground's undulations.
