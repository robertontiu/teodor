# Drapery (layer 1)

Cloth and clothing as they really are: a flexible sheet that hangs from where it is held, folds where it is pushed, wraps the body and answers to gravity, wind and water. Researched 2026-11. Every value names its source; values marked **judgement** have no published source and are editorial choices for Teodor. Read with `figure` (the body under the cloth) and `light-shadow`.

## 1. What it is, structurally

**Support points and gravity.** Cloth hangs from tension points: on the body, the skeletal landmarks that press against it (shoulders, collarbones, chest, hips, elbows, knees, buttocks), plus seams, waistbands and garment edges. "Drapery clings to these areas. It pushes and grabs around them", and between them "gravity is a profound force that creates a straight downward gesture" ([Proko, Intro to Drapery](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/intro-to-drapery)). Leonardo's rules for a fold held at two points: the part farthest from the fixing points "will fall most nearly in its natural form", and the fold is widest midway between them ([Notebooks §390](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/R.bak/section-7/item-390.html)). So: every fold has a cause, and it starts at a support point and dies away from it.

**The fold types.** The drawing-school tradition names seven: pipe, zigzag, spiral, half-lock, diaper, drop and inert ([Bridgman, *Drawing the Draped Figure*, Dover synopsis](https://test.blackwells.co.uk/bookshop/product/Drawing-the-Draped-Figure-by-George-B-Bridgman/9780486418025); a modern course lists pipe, diaper, half-lock, spiral, flag, zigzag/compression and inert, and says complex folds are "usually just a combination of the six active folds", [Proko](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/intro-to-drapery)). Descriptions after [Pluralsight's fold guide](https://www.pluralsight.com/blog/creative-professional/the-6-fold-types-every-illustrator-should-know-for-drawing-clothing):

| fold | what makes it | where on clothes |
|---|---|---|
| **pipe** | cloth hangs from one gathered or compressed point and falls in tubes | skirts, curtains, a cloak from the shoulder, a sleeve from a raised arm |
| **diaper** | cloth sags between two support points; alternating diamond-shaped planes | scarves, a sagging neckline, cloth between the shoulders, a sling |
| **zigzag (compression)** | a tube is compressed along its length and buckles in alternating folds | inside a bent elbow or knee, trousers bunched over the shoe |
| **spiral** | cloth is pulled around a cylinder under tension | sleeves pushed up the forearm, trouser legs around a striding leg |
| **half-lock** | a tube changes direction abruptly and the cloth locks in a curved fold | the side of a bent knee when sitting, the elbow, a trouser at the ankle |
| **drop** | cloth falls freely off a form | a towel on a hook, a cloak off the shoulder, a tablecloth off a table edge |
| **flag** | cloth fixed at one edge is moved by air | a cloak or scarf in wind, a hem when running |
| **inert** | cloth at rest on the ground, folded by nothing but its own weight | a train, a dropped coat, cloth pooled on the floor |

**Material.** "The thickness and pliability (softness) of the cloth determines the size and shape of the folds" ([Hagan, Painting clothing](https://www.oocities.org/~jlhagan/advanced/cloth1.htm)): fur and wool show large gentle contours; stiff heavy linen or damask resists folding, with straight edges and multiple creases; light linen and cotton make thinner folds closer together with squarer outlines; velvet is heavy but pliable, with slight curves and single folds; silk and satin are thin, fold narrowly in many directions and "cling to the shape they cover"; gauze depends on starch. Leonardo: study real wool, silk, fine and coarse cloth, linen and crape, and vary the folds for each; paper or leather stand-ins mislead ([§392](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/R.bak/section-7/item-392.html)). In physics terms, folds are set by the cloth's bending stiffness against gravity and tension: stiffer, heavier cloth makes fewer, larger folds (Cerda, Mahadevan & Passini, *Elements of Draping*, PNAS 2004, [lab page](https://softmath.seas.harvard.edu/publication/elements-of-draping/); the paper's scaling laws were not read, so this is the qualitative result only).

**The anatomy of one fold** (**judgement**, standard atelier teaching): a fold is a small cylinder or prism with a lit top plane, a turning halftone, a shadow side and a valley; where cloth turns sharply (a crease, a half-lock) the edge is hard, where it rolls (a pipe) the edge is soft. Fold ridges usually run from the support point toward the free end, and they fan out and get wider as they go.

**Hierarchy.** One or two dominant fold systems per garment section, a few secondary folds, and small wrinkles only where the cloth is compressed (**judgement**, consistent with Leonardo's warning against "a great confusion of many folds", §392, and Proko's "it is easy to get caught up in the minutia of small wrinkles").

**Cloth and the body.** The cloth both hides and reveals. A cloak should not show the limbs as if it lay against the skin, because other garments lie beneath (paraphrase); only thin cloth "pressed and clinging to the limbs … by the action of the wind" shows their true shape (§391). The classical "wet drapery" convention is "cloth that appears to cling to the body in animated folds while it reveals the contours of the form beneath" ([Met, Classical Art and Modern Dress](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/classical-art-and-modern-dress)); it is a sculptural convention, not ordinary dress (same essay). The body shows through cloth at tension points: where cloth is pulled tight over a form, the form reads; between them, the cloth reads (**judgement**, follows from the tension-point principle).

## 2. Seen from different viewpoints

- **Wrapping.** Fold lines, seams, hems and cuffs follow cross-contours of the body: around a forearm they are curves, not straight lines across (Proko: wrap fabric around the mannequin, overlap lines for depth).
- **Hems and cuffs are ellipses** in perspective, as cross-sections of the cylinder the garment makes: seen from above the hem curves down at the front, from below it curves up (**judgement**, follows from perspective of cylinders).
- **Foreshortening.** Folds on a limb pointing toward the viewer overlap like stacked rings; the near ones hide the far ones (**judgement**).
- **Silhouette.** At the contour, folds show as notches and bulges; a smooth contour on a folded garment looks pasted on (**judgement**).
- **Distance.** At figure distance, only the big shapes (the main folds, the hem, the shadow under the chest or between the legs) and the cloth's colour and value read; wrinkles vanish first (**judgement**).

## 3. Under light

- Each fold is a small form with its own light, halftone, core shadow, reflected light and, if deep, an occlusion shadow in the valley; the valleys facing away from the light hold cast shadows from the next ridge (see `light-shadow`; applying it to folds is **judgement**).
- **Material shows in the highlight and the halftone** (**judgement** unless noted): matte wool and cotton have broad soft halftones and no highlight (Proko: a highlight is "not present on matte materials", [Elements of Light and Shadow](https://www.proko.com/course-lesson/elements-of-light-and-shadow/notes)); silk and satin have narrow, bright highlights along fold ridges and a large jump from light to dark; velvet looks lightest at its turning edges and darkest where it faces you (the pile); linen shows sharp crease lines.
- **Colour.** Shadows in deep folds take the colour of the cloth next to them by reflection: Leonardo, "every opaque and colourless body assumes the hue of the colour reflected on it" ([§281](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/R.bak/section-5/item-281.html)). A red cloak bounces red into its own folds, so its shadows stay saturated rather than grey (**judgement** for the saturation).
- **Thin cloth against light** transmits: a backlit linen or muslin glows and shows the silhouette of the body (**judgement**).
- Renaissance painters studied exactly this: "Many renaissance artists made drapery studies by drawing with a brush on linen", and Leonardo's drapery studies use a pale blue mid-tone ground with black shading and white highlights ([Royal Collection Trust](https://www.rct.uk/collection/exhibitions/leonardo-da-vinci-a-life-in-drawing/the-drapery-of-a-kneeling-figure)).

## 4. Variation

- **Material:** see section 1 (Hagan; Leonardo §392).
- **Tailored vs draped.** Tailored garments carry structure from seams, darts and padding (a jacket keeps its shoulder line, a collar holds its shape); draped garments (cloaks, saris, togas, sheets) get all their form from tension points and gravity (**judgement**).
- **Pose.** Sitting: compression (zigzag) at the waist and lap, half-locks at the knees, the skirt or trouser pulled taut over the thighs and dropping from the knee. Raising an arm: the sleeve slides down, pipes from the shoulder. Striding: spirals around the leading leg, the cloth pulled taut from the back knee (**judgement**, by applying the fold table).
- **Motion.** Cloth lags behind the body (inertia): when a figure turns or runs, the free cloth trails and then swings past on the stop; hems flare out on a turn (**judgement**).
- **Wind.** On the windward side cloth presses against the body and reveals it (Leonardo §391; Met's "wet drapery"); on the leeward side it billows and streams downwind as flag folds; free edges flutter in smaller, faster waves than the main body of the cloth (Proko names the flag fold; the rest is **judgement**). Gravity still acts: wind lifts cloth only as far as its weight allows, so heavy wool barely lifts and silk streams (**judgement**). Wind direction must agree across cloth, hair, smoke, trees and water.
- **Wet.** Wet cloth darkens (water lowers surface reflection and traps light: [Lekner & Dorf 1988](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/scps/staff/pdf/darkerwhenwet.pdf)), gets heavier, clings, makes fewer and deeper folds, and thin cloth turns translucent (darkening is sourced; the rest is **judgement**).
- **Wear and age.** Worn clothes keep memory creases at the elbows, behind the knees and at the waist; old cloth sags and loses its sheen (**judgement**).
- **Pattern and stripes** follow every fold and are the clearest proof of wrapping: a stripe bends over a ridge and disappears into a valley (**judgement**).

## 5. How painters observe and construct it

- **From real cloth.** Vasari tells that Leonardo made clay figures, laid soft cloths over them ("on those he put soft rags buried, and then with patience he put himself to portray them") and worked "in black and white with the tip of the brush" on fine linen; the article adds that the cloths were wet and earth-covered (quoted in [Finestre sull'Arte](https://www.finestresullarte.info/en/works-and-artists/leonardo-da-vinci-draughtsman-the-studies-on-canvas)). The Louvre's drapery studies for *Saint Anne* are in black chalk and grey wash heightened with white ([Louvre](https://mini-site.louvre.fr/trimestriel/2019/l%C3%A9onard_de_vinciGB/105/)). Leonardo and Dürer are examples of this kind of observation (Hagan notes Dürer's deeper creases in shadow to suggest the body), not models of manner.
- **Order of work** (Proko): a "map" of the whole silhouette first, then shape, gesture and perspective; a simple mannequin under the cloth. Hagan: observe the broadest and narrowest folds first and simplify complicated folds at the drawing stage.
- **For a painting** (**judgement**, consistent with the above):
  1. Draw the body first, then mark the tension points.
  2. Draw the few main folds as lines of force from those points, with gravity pulling the free cloth straight down.
  3. Block the shadows of the big folds as connected shapes; keep the light side simple.
  4. Model the main folds as forms (light, halftone, core, reflected light); state the hard edges at creases and the soft edges at rolls.
  5. Add secondary folds only where the cloth is compressed or pulled; leave the rest quiet.
  6. Check the hem, cuffs and patterns wrap the form.
- Composition: arrange folds so they suggest the form beneath and lead the eye; reveal some forms, only suggest others (Hagan, after Virgil Elliott).

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Wrinkles everywhere:** a uniform texture of small folds; Leonardo's "great confusion of many folds" (§392), Proko's "minutia of small wrinkles".
- **Folds without a cause:** lines that don't start at a support point, a compression or a pull (Proko on tension points).
- **Cloth that doesn't wrap:** folds and hems drawn as straight lines across a round body (Proko).
- **Folds as drawn lines:** dark lines laid over flat colour instead of modelled forms with lit planes; reads as cartoon (**judgement**; Teodor's craft note that lines drawn over paint read as cartoon, `docs/habits.md`).
- **Shrink-wrap or sack:** the body shown as if through skin (Leonardo §391) or lost altogether under a shapeless mass (**judgement**).
- **No gravity:** folds drifting sideways with no wind, or hanging cloth that doesn't fall plumb (Proko on gravity).
- **One fabric for everything:** silk, wool and linen folding alike (Leonardo §392; Hagan).
- **Even, parallel, equally spaced folds** like a radiator (**judgement**).
- **Stiff mannequin dress:** clothes that don't respond to the pose, so the figure looks like a doll in a painted costume (connects to Teodor's "frontal, stiff bodies" habit; **judgement**).
- **Wind that disagrees** with hair, smoke or trees in the same picture (**judgement**).

## 7. Checks for Teodor

1. Every main fold starts at a support point, a compression or a pull; gravity pulls the free cloth straight down (or wind visibly redirects it).
2. Fold size matches the material: few large folds for heavy cloth, many narrow ones for silk.
3. Hems, cuffs, seams and stripes curve around the body as ellipses or cross-contours.
4. Folds are painted as forms with lit and shadow planes, not as lines; creases hard, rolls soft.
5. The body shows at tension points and is hidden between them.
6. Small wrinkles only at compressions; large quiet areas elsewhere.
7. Wind, hair and cloth agree in direction and strength.
