# Animals (layer 1: the subject as it really is)

Mammals, birds, fish and insects as a painter meets them: the skeleton that sets the proportions, the gesture of a living body, the covering (fur, feathers, scales) that sits over it, and how it moves. The horse gets the most numbers because it has the longest literature of artists' canons; other animals are given in the same terms where a source exists, and marked as judgement where it doesn't.

## 1. What it is, structurally

**One skeleton plan, many proportions.** Four-legged mammals share the human bone plan, stretched differently. The joints that confuse painters are the ones that have moved off the ground:

| animal term | human equivalent | source |
|---|---|---|
| stifle (hind leg, femur to tibia) | knee | [LibreTexts, Lawson](https://med.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Veterinary_Medicine/Anatomy_and_Physiology_of_Animals_(Lawson)/06%3A_The_Skeleton/6.15%3A_Common_Names_of_Joints) |
| hock (tarsals to metatarsals) | ankle | same |
| horse's "knee" (radius to metacarpals) | wrist | same |
| fetlock (metacarpals/metatarsals to phalanges) | knuckle | same |

- **Three stances.** Plantigrade animals (humans, bears, raccoons, monkeys) walk on the whole sole; digitigrade animals (dogs, cats, most birds) walk on their toes with the raised metacarpus/metatarsus adding to leg length; unguligrade animals (horse, deer, cattle, pigs) stand on the hoof, with the foot bones raised and lengthened even further ([UMN veterinary anatomy, plate 6](https://vanat.ahc.umn.edu/run/plate6.html); [Line of Action](https://www.line-of-action.com/article/animal-leg-anatomy-cheat-sheet)). The joint that looks like a "backward knee" on a dog or a bird is the ankle; no animal has a backward knee ([Line of Action](https://line-of-action.com/digitrade-animals-dont-have-backward-knees)). In birds the upper leg is hidden inside the body contour (Line of Action).
- **The head as the unit.** Animal painters measure the body in head lengths, as figure painters do.

**Horse** (the best-documented canon):
- Height at the withers ≈ body length (point of shoulder to point of buttock) ≈ **2.5 head lengths**; full height from forelock to ground ≈ **3 head lengths** (Bourgelat's canon, reported in Cuyer, *The Artistic Anatomy of Animals*, [Project Gutenberg #38315](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/38315)). Duhousset's "two heads and a half in height and length" was found in only 14 of the 50 horses he surveyed (same source): use the canon as a check, never as a mould.
- Lengths about equal to the head: the neck; the depth of the girth; elbow to fetlock on the foreleg; point of shoulder to withers; ground to hock; hock to stifle. Body length is 2.5 to 3 heads; the hock is level with the chestnuts (just above the fore "knee"); elbow and stifle are roughly level; the point of the buttock sits slightly higher than the shoulder joint. In the head, the eye sits about a third of the way down the face ([Art Nerds, horse proportions](https://artnerds.substack.com/p/drawing-horses-general-proportions), secondary).

**Cattle:** built like a horse at first sight but with shorter legs and neck; a hump over the shoulder made by the spinal processes; a very wide chest; a rounded belly; a big, long skull; loose skin under the throat (the dewlap) that makes the neck look wider than it is; cloven hooves; horns with a round cross-section that become ellipses as they turn away; large dark eyes with horizontal pupils ([Envato Tuts+, drawing bovines](https://design.tutsplus.com/tutorials/how-to-draw-animals-cows-and-other-bovines--cms-21683), secondary). The hip bones (hooks and pins) and the spine show as hard landmarks under the hide of a lean cow (**judgement**, from the same tutorial's note that hips "are well visible under the skin" on buffalo).

**Dog and cat:** proportions vary so much by breed that no canon holds; puppies and kittens are not small adults, their heads and paws are larger relative to the body ([*Dogs and Puppies*, Walter Foster](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/dogs-and-puppies/9781600580277/xhtml/ch05.html), preview). Both are digitigrade: the wrist and heel are well off the ground, and the paw pads are the toes. As starting checks (**judgement**): a medium dog's body (chest to buttock) is about as long as it is tall at the withers, a box rather than a sausage; a cat's body is longer than tall, its shoulder blades rise above the spine when it walks, and its tail is about as long as its body.

**Birds:**
- Wing feathers in groups: primaries on the "hand" (usually 9 to 11; most passerines 10), secondaries on the forearm (from 6 in hummingbirds to about 40 in some albatrosses), tertials covering the folded flight feathers, coverts in overlapping rows above and below, and the alula on the "thumb". The tail almost always has 12 feathers (six pairs) ([Wikipedia, Flight feather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_feather)).
- At rest, the wing folds into the body contour; the tertials cover the folded primaries and secondaries, and the "primary extension" (how far the primaries project past the tertials) is a species mark (Flight feather).
- Most birds are digitigrade, and the visible "backward knee" is the ankle (Line of Action).

**Fish:** a fusiform body with fins: pectoral (turning and braking), pelvic (steadying), anal, dorsal (balance), caudal (propulsion), and on trout, salmon and catfish a small fleshy adipose fin; most have scales; a lateral line runs along the flank ([SC Department of Natural Resources, fish anatomy](https://www.dnr.sc.gov/fish/anatomy.html)).

**Insects:** three body regions (head, thorax, abdomen); three pairs of legs, one on each thoracic segment; in most winged insects two pairs of wings on the second and third thoracic segments; each leg in segments (coxa, trochanter, femur, tibia, tarsus); antennae and compound eyes on the head ([Wikipedia, Insect morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect_morphology)). The legs always come from the thorax, never from the abdomen.

## 2. Seen from different viewpoints
- **Side view** is where the canons work: the horse's 2.5-head box, the level of elbow and stifle, the leg lengths.
- **Front and three-quarter views** foreshorten the barrel into a near cylinder; the chest becomes the widest shape and the legs overlap. In a front view of a horse or cow the legs sit under a wide chest and the far legs hide behind the near ones (**judgement**).
- **Horns, ears and wings** are circles or flat planes in perspective: a horn's round section becomes an ellipse as it turns away (Envato Tuts+); a wing seen from below or edge-on becomes a thin line with a thickened leading edge (**judgement**).
- **Birds in flight seen from below** show the wing's underside, the coverts pale against darker flight feathers in many species; seen from a distance only the silhouette and wingbeat rhythm identify the bird (**judgement**).
- **Distance:** at a distance, an animal reads by silhouette and gesture alone. Fur and feather texture is only visible close up (Ruskin's general rule that detail disappears with distance, [*The Elements of Drawing*](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.264111/2015.264111.The-Elements_djvu.txt)).

## 3. Under light
- **Countershading:** many mammals, birds, fish and insects are darker on the back and lighter underneath, which cancels the form shadow under top light and flattens the animal ("Thayer's law", [Wikipedia, Countershading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countershading)). A painter has to model the form *against* this pattern: the belly is lighter in local colour but in shadow, the back darker in local colour but in light. Thayer overstated how universal it is (same source).
- **Fur** reads as masses of value and colour, not as strands: "what we think is thousands of individual strands end up looking like bigger masses of colors and values" ([Trekell](https://www.trekell.com/blogs/experience/how-to-paint-fur-without-painting-every-hair), secondary). The form shadow on fur is soft-edged; the silhouette against light is broken and often rim-lit, because light passes through the outer hairs (**judgement**).
- **Short coats** (horse, short-haired cow) show the muscles under the skin; leaving them out makes the body flat (Envato Tuts+).
- **Feathers** are layered plates: each tract catches light as one plane, with sheen on dark plumage (**judgement**). **Fish scales** are small mirrors: highlights appear in bands along the flank, and the belly is often silvery (**judgement**).
- **Eyes** are wet: a single small specular highlight sits on the cornea, and in prey animals with large dark eyes it gives the eye its life (**judgement**).

## 4. Variation
- **Species and breed** change proportion more than anything else (dogs especially).
- **Age:** young animals have big heads, big joints and paws, and short muzzles (Walter Foster preview; details **judgement**).
- **Condition:** a lean animal shows ribs, hip bones and shoulder blade; a fat or long-coated one hides them (Envato Tuts+ on yak legs "very short under the fur").
- **Coat and season:** winter coats thicken and soften the silhouette; moulting breaks the coat into patches (**judgement**).
- **Gait** (horse) ([Wikipedia, Horse gait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_gait)):

| gait | beats | footfall | speed | airborne? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| walk | 4 | left hind, left fore, right hind, right fore | ~7 km/h | never: alternates three and two feet on the ground |
| trot | 2 | diagonal pairs together | ~13 km/h | two feet always off the ground; a brief suspension between diagonals at speed (**judgement**) |
| canter | 3 | one hind; other hind with opposite fore; leading fore | 16–27 km/h | brief pause after each cycle |
| gallop | 4 | the canter's middle beat splits in two | 40–48 km/h | yes, with the legs **gathered under the body**, not extended |

- **The flying gallop is wrong.** Muybridge's 1878 photographs showed a galloping horse is airborne when its legs are beneath the body, just before the hind legs land; the pose with front and hind legs stretched out while airborne "is never actually exhibited by a galloping horse" ([Wikipedia, *The 1821 Derby at Epsom*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_1821_Derby_at_Epsom)).
- **Wingbeat:** the downstroke gives most of the thrust; on the upstroke the wing is partly folded inward and the primaries rotate open to let air through; gliding uses no propulsion, soaring uses rising air, flap-bounding alternates flaps with folded wings; hummingbirds beat at about 43 per second ([Wikipedia, Bird flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_flight)). A painted bird is usually best caught at the top or bottom of the stroke or in a glide, when the wing shape is clearest (**judgement**).
- **Fish** swim by flexing the body and caudal fin; pectorals steer and brake (SC DNR).

## 5. How painters observe and construct it
1. **Gesture first:** one line for the spine from poll to tail, then the tilt of shoulders and hips; the head's angle sets the mood (**judgement**, carried over from figure drawing).
2. **Boxes and cylinders:** chest and hindquarters as two masses joined by the barrel; the head as a block with a muzzle; build from "simple chunks" (Envato Tuts+).
3. **Check with head lengths:** withers height, body length, neck, leg segments (Bourgelat/Duhousset via Cuyer; Art Nerds).
4. **Place the joints:** elbow and stifle level, hock level with the chestnut; draw the leg as bones and joints, not tubes.
5. **Mass the covering:** fur and feathers as large value groups following the growth direction; texture only at the light-dark transitions and the silhouette: "a few smaller shapes where light meets dark creates the illusion that there is more detail" (Trekell).
6. **Observe from life and from sequence photographs** for motion; painters known for close animal observation include Stubbs (dissection-based horse anatomy) and Dürer (the *Young Hare*), named only as examples of observation.

## 6. Common mistakes
- **The flying gallop** and other invented gaits: legs in impossible phase (e.g. both forelegs forward at a trot). Check the footfall table.
- **Backward knees** on dogs, cats and birds, or bears walking on tiptoe (Line of Action).
- **Tube legs** with no joints, no hoof angle, no fetlock; legs all the same width.
- **Toy proportions:** head too big, legs too short, body a sausage; adult animals painted with puppy proportions or the reverse.
- **Fur as hair-by-hair hatching** everywhere, or as a smooth plastic shell; feathers drawn as scales everywhere, or a wing as a single paddle with no primaries.
- **Flat bodies**: countershading copied as value without modelling the form under it; short-coated animals with no muscle.
- **Insect legs from the abdomen,** wrong leg count, fish fins in the wrong place.
- **Symmetry and stiffness:** all four legs vertical, head straight on, tail as a rope.

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. Measure the main animal in head lengths: horse withers height and body length about 2.5 heads (±0.3); flag anything outside 2–3.
2. Each moving leg matches a real gait phase; a galloping horse in the air has its legs gathered under the body.
3. Joints are where they belong: the visible "backward" joint on dogs, cats and birds is the ankle, high off the ground; elbow and stifle are level on a horse.
4. Fur and feathers are painted as value masses following the growth direction, with texture only at light-dark edges and the silhouette.
5. Form is modelled through countershading: back and belly still read as lit and shadowed planes.
6. A bird in flight shows separated primaries at the wingtip and a believable phase of the stroke; a bird at rest has its wings folded into the body line.
7. At a distance, the animal reads from its silhouette alone (check in the squint or notan view).
