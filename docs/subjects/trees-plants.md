# Trees and plants (layer 1: the subject as it really is)

Trees, foliage, grass and vegetation: how a tree is built by growth, how it reads as a few large masses at painting distance, how it takes the light, and how it changes with species, season and weather. Flowers and fruit have their own file (`flowers.md`).

## 1. What it is, structurally
- **Branching conserves thickness.** Leonardo's rule: "all the branches of a tree at every stage of its height when put together are equal in thickness to the trunk." Written as d^Δ = Σ dᵢ^Δ, Leonardo's Δ is 2 (cross-sectional area is conserved); measurements across many species put Δ between 1.8 and 2.3, and wind-induced stress is one explanation ([Eloy 2011, *Leonardo's rule, self-similarity and wind-induced stresses in trees*](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1105.2591)). In practice: when a limb forks into two equal branches, each is about 0.7 of its diameter (1/√2); a trunk never splits into branches that together look thicker or much thinner than itself. (The 0.7 is arithmetic from Δ = 2.) Leonardo himself excepted pollarded trees (below).
- **Many more small branches than large.** With Δ = 2, a tenfold drop in branch radius means about 100 times as many branches; "a two-fold increase in resolution reveals four times more branches" ([Bentley et al., *Fractal scaling and the aesthetics of trees*](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2402.13520)). Each fork leads to twice as many downstream branches (same source).
- **Two crown plans.** Strong apical dominance gives an *excurrent* tree: one leader, a conical crown, trunk growing faster than branches, branches faster than their side shoots (pines and most conifers). Weak apical dominance gives a *decurrent* (deliquescent) tree: many shoots growing at similar rates, the main stem hard to follow, a large spreading crown (most broadleaf trees) ([Iowa State Extension, Tree anatomy 101](https://naturalresources.extension.iastate.edu/forestry/tree_biology/101.html); [Wikipedia, Crown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crown_(botany))).
- **The fork is the key joint.** Ruskin: "When once you have mastered the tree at its armpits, you will have little more trouble with it" ([*The Elements of Drawing*](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.264111/2015.264111.The-Elements_djvu.txt)).
- **Foliage is a fractal surface,** not a solid: measured fractal dimension of foliage between 2.2 and 2.8 (Eloy 2011). It reads as clumps on the ends of branches with gaps between them.
- **Trunk:** flares at the base into the roots and tapers upward; bark pattern follows the growth (vertical furrows, horizontal lenticels on birch and cherry). (**judgement**)
- **Grass and low vegetation** grow as a carpet of near-vertical blades; at any distance they read as a plane with a top-lit colour and a texture that shrinks with distance (texture gradient, see `landscape.md`).

## 2. Seen from different viewpoints
- **Profile vs above or below.** "All masses of foliage have an upper and under surface, and the side view of them, or profile, shows a wholly different organization of branches from that seen in the view from above." Trees are usually seen more or less in profile, "and Nature puts her best composition into the profile arrangement" (Ruskin).
- **Branches come toward and go away from the viewer.** Branches pointing at the viewer are foreshortened and overlap the trunk; a tree drawn only with branches in the picture plane looks like a flat cut-out (**judgement**).
- **Distance simplifies.** Ruskin's exercise: put a drawn bough one, one and a half and two yards away and see "how the thinner stalks and leaves gradually disappear, leaving only a vague and slight darkness where they were." The twigs become "a confused network or mist" around the main branches, to be scratched in as a shape, not drawn twig by twig.
- **Far trees** become flat shapes coloured by aerial perspective (bluer, lighter, lower contrast: see `landscape.md`). A wood seen across a field is one mass with a ragged top edge.

## 3. Under light
- **Trees are the darkest plane in a sunny landscape.** Carlson's theory of angles: sky lightest, flat ground second, slopes third, upright planes such as trees darkest; there may be many lights and shadows within a tree, but they stay inside its dark range; on a grey or misty day the four values draw much closer together ([The Artist's Road, Carlson's theory of angles](https://theartistsroad.net/articles/thecarlsontheoryofangles)).
- **A crown is a rough sphere or cone made of clumps.** Each clump has a lit top, a shadow side and a cast shadow on the clumps below; the whole crown has its own form shadow on the side away from the sun (**judgement**, the standard form-shadow logic of `light-shadow`).
- **Sky holes:** gaps in the foliage where the sky shows through. Ruskin tells the student to draw the white interstices between branches "with as much scrupulousness as if they were little estates which you had to survey." Sky holes are slightly darker than the open sky around the tree, because the glare of the surrounding foliage and the edge irradiation make them look small and dim (**judgement**).
- **Transmitted light:** a leaf lit from behind glows yellow-green, warmer and more saturated than the same leaf lit from the front (**judgement**; leaves are thin and translucent).
- **The underside of a crown** is darker and often cooler, lit only by light bounced from the ground; on a broadleaf tree the undersides of leaves are paler than the tops and show when wind turns them (**judgement**).
- **Cast shadows of trees** on undulating ground explain the ground's form: "If you draw the shadows of leaves and tree trunks on any undulating ground with entire carefulness, you will be surprised to find how much they explain of the form and distance of the earth on which they fall" (Ruskin).

## 4. Variation
- **Species:** excurrent conifers (cone, single leader) vs decurrent broadleaves (spreading dome). Each species has its own bough and leaf pattern; Ruskin asks for "one bough of every common tree: oak, ash, elm, birch, beech" and complains of painters who give "not even so much as the species of tree."
- **Season** ([Harvard Forest, the process of leaf colour change](https://harvardforest.fas.harvard.edu/leaf-process)):
  - green from chlorophyll, which all leaves lose gradually through the season;
  - yellow from carotenoids that remain when chlorophyll is gone;
  - red from anthocyanins actively made just before leaf fall;
  - orange from carotenoids plus anthocyanins; brownish from anthocyanin plus chlorophyll.
  - Winter broadleaves show the full branch structure; the twig mist reads as a warm grey-violet haze around the crown (**judgement**). Spring foliage is light, yellow-green and sparse; summer foliage dark and dense (**judgement**).
- **Age and history:** young trees are slim and regular; old trees have broken limbs, hollows and asymmetric crowns. Pollarded trees break Leonardo's rule (Leonardo, quoted in Bentley et al.).
- **Wind:** branches and grasses bend downwind together; leaves turn and show their pale undersides; a tree in a constant wind grows leaning (**judgement**).
- **Exposure:** isolated trees grow broad, low crowns; trees in a forest grow tall with crowns only at the top (**judgement**).

## 5. How painters observe and construct it
1. **Silhouette first.** Draw the outer shape of the whole tree and its big sky holes; Ruskin's beginner draws the branches as "a flat ramification on a white ground" and the interstices as exactly as the branches.
2. **Trunk and main limbs,** with the forks right, obeying the thickness rule.
3. **Group the foliage into three to five big masses,** each with a light, a halftone and a shadow; then break the edges of the masses (**judgement**, common teaching practice).
4. **Value check against the sky and the ground** (Carlson's order).
5. **Detail only at the edges and in the light,** leaving twigs as a mist and interiors as masses. Ruskin's warning applies both ways: a bad sketcher "puts his leaves on the trees as if they were moss tied to sticks"; the good draughtsman shows the "law of radiation" that rules the masses and the individual liberty of each leaf within it.
6. **Study single boughs and leaves close up** to learn the species' pattern, then simplify. Constable's tree studies and the Barbizon painters' forest interiors are examples of this kind of close observation.

## 6. Common mistakes
- **Lollipop trees:** a smooth ball of green on a straight stick; no sky holes, no clumps, no branches through the foliage.
- **Branches that thicken outward,** or forks where the two branches together are much thicker than the parent; all branches the same thickness; branches only in the picture plane.
- **Symmetrical "Y" forks** repeated at every level; regular spacing.
- **Every leaf painted** at middle distance, or uniform dab texture across the whole crown (a "digital filter" surface).
- **Trees lighter than the ground or sky** in full sun (breaks Carlson's order without a reason such as backlight or snow).
- **Sky holes painted as bright as the open sky,** or as the same shape repeated.
- **One green** for all trees; no species, no season; foliage edges uniformly hard or uniformly soft.
- **Trees that don't sit in the ground:** no root flare, no cast shadow, no contact shadow.

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. At every visible fork, the daughter branches together look as thick as the parent (each about 0.7 of its diameter for an equal split); branches taper outward.
2. The crown has three to five value masses with a lit side and a shadow side, and its outline is broken by clumps and sky holes.
3. Sky holes exist, vary in size, and are a touch darker than the open sky.
4. In sunlight, trees are the darkest plane (darker than ground and sky), unless the scene gives a reason (snow, backlight, mist).
5. The species reads: conical with one leader, or spreading with no clear leader; leaf colour fits the season.
6. Detail is reserved for silhouettes and the light; the interior and distant trees are masses.
7. The tree has a cast shadow on the ground, and its shadow follows the ground's undulations.
