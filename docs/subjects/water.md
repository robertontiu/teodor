# Water (layer 1: the subject as it really is)

Still and moving water, waves, spray, reflections, transparency and rain. Water has almost no shape of its own: what a painter paints is a mixture of three things, the sky and objects reflected in the surface, what is seen through it, and the surface's own disturbance. Getting the proportions of those three right, and the geometry of the reflections, is most of the subject.

## 1. What it is, structurally
**The reflection law.** Ruskin: "Suppose all the objects above the water actually reversed (not in appearance, but in fact) beneath the water, and, precisely the same in form and in relative position, only all topsy-turvy. Then whatever you can see, from the place in which you stand, of the solid objects so reversed under the water, you will see in the reflection always in the true perspective of the solid objects so reversed" ([*The Elements of Drawing*](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.264111/2015.264111.The-Elements_djvu.txt)). Consequences (geometry of a horizontal mirror):
- A vertical object's reflection hangs straight down, and its length measured from the point where the object meets the water equals the object's height.
- The reflection is not a flipped copy of the picture: it shows the object as seen from below (the underside of a bridge, the soffit of eaves), and a set-back object's reflection starts below the waterline at the bank and may be partly or entirely hidden by the bank.
- Reflections of objects lying back from the shore are shorter than you expect, and distant hills reflect only their tops.
- Leaning objects reflect at the mirror angle: a post leaning toward the viewer has a reflection leaning toward the viewer too, so in the picture the two lines meet at the waterline and look longer below than above (**judgement**, from the geometry).

**How much the surface reflects (Fresnel).** Water reflects about 2% of light at normal incidence (98% enters), rising to almost 100% at grazing angles ([scubageek, External reflection from a water surface](https://scubageek.com/articles/wwwatw)). So:
- Looking steeply down (near your feet) you see into the water: the bottom, its colour, or darkness. Looking along it toward the distance, you see a near-perfect mirror of the sky. "The more you look down into the water, the better you see objects through it; the more you look along it, the eye being low, the more you see the reflection of objects above it" (Ruskin). Water "approaches the reflectivity of a perfect mirror only when you're looking straight across it at a very shallow angle" ([Gurney, water reflections part 1](https://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2007/12/water-reflections-part-1.html)).
- "Wherever the reflection is darkest, you will see through the water best, and vice versa" (Ruskin).
- **Values in reflection:** light tones above the water appear "a little darker in the reflection" (Gurney); dark objects reflect a touch lighter, because some sky glare is added to the weak reflected image (**judgement**, follows from Fresnel and the bright sky).

**Ripples and waves.**
- A disturbed surface is many small tilted mirrors. Distant wavelets tilt toward and away from you, so each reflects a strip above or below, and the image is **stretched vertically**; near ripples become visible individually and **break the reflection horizontally** ([Gurney, reflections on gently rippled water](https://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2013/08/reflections-on-gently-rippled-water.html), after Pollock's *Light and Water*, 1903).
- Ruskin: with "the least motion on the water, the horizontal lines of the images will be diffused and broken, while the vertical ones will remain decisive, and the oblique ones decisive in proportion to their steepness." With large ripples the reflections "must be drawn with bold undulatory descending lines."
- **Glitter path:** the sun or moon on rippled water makes a roughly elliptical pattern; for a high source its angular length is four times the maximum wave slope; its width-to-length ratio equals the sine of the source's elevation, so a low sun gives a long, narrow path; "a perfectly smooth surface would contain only one glint" ([NOAA PSL, glittering light on water](https://psl.noaa.gov/outreach/education/science/glitter)).
- **Breaking:** a wave crest can't get sharper than about 120° (Stokes' limiting wave); breaking begins when the front face passes vertical ([Geometric and kinematic indicators of breaking inception, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.14230v1)). The crest leans forward as the wave grows (same source).
- **Sea state by wind** (Beaufort, [NWS Juneau](https://www.weather.gov/ajk/beaufort)): 0 "sea like a mirror"; 1 ripples like scales, no foam; 2 glassy crests that don't break; 3 crests begin to break; 4 "fairly frequent white horses"; 5 many white horses; 6 whitecaps common, more spray (8–13 ft waves); 7 foam blown in streaks along the wind; 8 spindrift off the crests, well-marked streaks; 9 dense streaks, spray affects visibility; 10–12 the sea white with foam, air filled with spray.

**Colour and transparency.**
- Pure water is blue because it absorbs red; the blue shows only through several metres of water, and in thin layers water is nearly colourless ([WebExhibits, Causes of colour: water](https://www.webexhibits.org/causesofcolor/5B.html)). Green water usually comes from algae; brown from dissolved tannins; the sky reflection adds to, but does not cause, the blue ([Wikipedia, Color of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_of_water)).
- In coloured water the darker reflections take the water's colour: "the reflection of a black gondola, for instance, at Venice, is never black, but pure dark green." Water's own colour is of three kinds: a milky bloom on the surface, the colour of light transmitted through wave edges (often clear green), and the tint it gives objects seen through it (Ruskin).
- **Refraction** bends straight lines where they enter the water; stones under clear water are displaced and distorted (Ruskin).
- **Foam and spray** are white because they are many air-water interfaces scattering all wavelengths (**judgement**; same principle as clouds).

**Rain.** Large drops (about 6 mm) fall at about 10 m/s; small raindrops much slower ([UW–Madison CIMSS, How fast do raindrops fall?](https://wxguys.ssec.wisc.edu/2018/07/16/raindrop-speed)). At normal exposure times a falling drop reads as a short streak, not a teardrop; streaks are visible mainly against a dark background and when lit from the side or behind (**judgement**). Rain on still water makes expanding rings and small splashes; rain veils distance like haze (**judgement**).

## 2. Seen from different viewpoints
- **Low eye:** mostly reflection, long stretched images, the far water as bright as the sky near the horizon.
- **High eye (from a bridge, a cliff):** mostly transparency and the water's own colour; reflections short and faint; waves seen as a pattern.
- **Into the light:** the surface is a bright sheet with dark wave silhouettes and a glitter path. **With the light behind you:** the water shows its body colour and the reflected sky, which is darker and bluer opposite the sun (see `sky.md`) (**judgement**).
- **Distance:** ripples are invisible far away; the far water reads as a smooth plane reflecting the sky near the horizon; waves diminish in size and spacing with the texture gradient (**judgement**; see `landscape.md`).
- **Wind patches:** a calm patch reflects; a breeze-ruffled patch (cat's paw) scatters the sky into a lighter or darker band. Ruskin tells the painter to watch "the lines of disturbance on the surface, as when a bird swims across it, or a fish rises, or the current plays round a stone."

## 3. Under light
- **Water has no form shadow of its own;** its values come from what it reflects and transmits. A wave's face reflects a lower, often darker part of the sky (and transmits light through its thin crest); its back reflects the higher sky (**judgement**, small-mirror reasoning).
- **Cast shadows on water** appear only where there is something in or under the water to receive them (suspended matter, a shallow bottom): in clear deep water, shadows barely show; in muddy or shallow water they are clear. "Where that shadow falls it makes the water more reflective, and where the sunshine falls you will see more of the surface of the water, and of any dust or motes" (Ruskin).
- **Transmitted light** through a thin wave crest is green or turquoise and the most saturated colour in a seascape (Ruskin's "pure emerald green by transmitted sunshine through its edges").
- **Specular highlights** of the sun on ripples are the brightest values in the picture and the colour of the sun (warm at a low sun).

## 4. Variation
- **Still:** pond, lake at dawn: a mirror, the sky's gradient continued below the horizon line, reflections with sharp edges.
- **Moving water:** a river's surface shows the flow by elongated reflections and V-shaped wakes behind obstacles; rapids show standing waves, foam and pale broken water (**judgement**).
- **Waterfalls:** smooth glassy tongues at the lip, breaking into white streaks, with mist at the base (**judgement**).
- **Sea:** the Beaufort table above; swell (long, rounded, regular) vs wind sea (short, steep, choppy) (**judgement**).
- **Shallow and clear:** the bottom dominates. Ruskin's mountain stream with reddish-orange stones under blue sky reflection yields purple.
- **Weather and hour:** grey day, water grey and flat; sunset, glitter path and warm reflections; rain, rings and a veiled distance; wind, darker ruffled bands.

## 5. How painters observe and construct it
1. **Decide what each area shows:** reflection, transparency or the disturbed surface, from the eye level and angle.
2. **Draw the reflections as carefully as the objects.** "The picture in the pool needs nearly as much delicate drawing as the picture above the pool"; "if you have not patience to draw the reflections, no expedient will give you a true effect" (Ruskin).
3. **Construct them geometrically:** drop verticals from each point where an object meets the water level; measure reflected heights from there.
4. **Then disturb them** as the wind and current do: stretch vertically, break horizontally, keep verticals decisive.
5. **Mark** with horizontal touches for calm elongated reflections, or vertical touches to indicate them quickly; bold wavy descending lines for big ripples (Ruskin).
6. **Get the shore curves right;** they carry the perspective of the water (see `landscape.md`).
7. **Study from life:** Ruskin's exercise of a mirror laid flat on a table with books on it, or a basin of blue water with floating objects, raising and lowering the head to see reflection change to transparency. Monet's river surfaces and Turner's sea studies are examples of long observation of the effect.

## 6. Common mistakes
- **Water as horizontal white dashes** or bars of light instead of reflections; Ruskin calls such expedients "vain and absurd."
- **Reflections as a flipped copy of the image** (shows the object's top, wrong length, starting at the wrong place); reflections slanting away from verticals; reflections of a set-back object starting at the object's base instead of at the water level below it.
- **Reflections as bright as the objects,** or reflections of dark things painted black.
- **Uniform water colour** from foreground to horizon; no shift from transparency at the feet to mirror in the distance.
- **Waves as rows of identical humps or scallops;** foam as white outlines along every crest; breaking crests sharper than 120° or curling everywhere at once.
- **Glitter path too wide for a low sun,** or a single round sun reflection on choppy water.
- **Rain as teardrops,** or rain streaks everywhere at the same density with no light behind them.
- **A single false ripple curve** ruins careful reflections: "your admitting a single false curve of ripple from a wild duck's breast" (Ruskin).

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. Every vertical's reflection hangs straight down; reflected lengths are measured from the water level under the object, and set-back objects' reflections are shorter or hidden.
2. Reflections of light things are slightly darker, of dark things slightly lighter; none is pure black or pure white.
3. The water shifts from showing its body colour or bottom near the viewer to mirroring the sky in the distance; near the horizon it matches the sky's value.
4. Ripples stretch reflections vertically in the distance and break them horizontally up close; verticals stay decisive.
5. A glitter path is narrow and long for a low sun, wider for a high one; it lies on the line from the sun to the viewer.
6. Wave scale, whitecaps and spray agree with one Beaufort force across the whole picture, and with the wind shown in sky, trees and smoke.
7. Transmitted light at wave crests is the most saturated colour in the water; foam is broken, never an outline.
