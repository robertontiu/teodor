# Sky (layer 1: the subject as it really is)

The sky as a light source and a subject: its gradients, the clouds by type and height, weather, the atmosphere between the viewer and the land, the sun and moon, and how all of these change through the day. The sky sets the light for everything else in a landscape, so its colour and value have to agree with the ground, water and shadows (`landscape.md`, `water.md`, `light-shadow.md`).

## 1. What it is, structurally

**A clear sky gradates in two directions at once.** One system is tied to the sun, the other to height above the horizon ([Gurney, *Sky blue*](https://gurneyjourney.blogspot.com/2008/04/sky-blue.html)):
- Near the sun: a region of warm glare that lowers the blue's chroma toward "a dull grey-green"; away from the sun the blue is more saturated, tending toward violet, and darker. A weaker lightening appears at the antisolar point, 180° from the sun.
- Toward the horizon the sky gets lighter, usually whiter, because the line of sight passes through more atmosphere; near the sun its value stays about constant from the horizon upward.
- The darkest blue (the "well of the sky") is at the zenith only at sunrise and sunset; at other times it lies about 65° from the sun.
- So a patch of clear sky needs "at least four separate starting colors" in opaque paint.

**Why.** Air molecules scatter short wavelengths more strongly (Rayleigh scattering; violet three to four times more than red), which makes the daytime sky blue; along the long path at sunrise and sunset the direct light is reddened ("sunrises and sunsets are red because the daytime sky is blue"); haze and pollution aerosols "do not enhance sky colors, they subdue them," turning the sky bluish-grey or white ([NOAA SPC, Corfidi, *The colors of sunset and twilight*](https://spc.noaa.gov/publications/corfidi/sunset.html)).

**Clouds by height (WMO *International Cloud Atlas*):**

| level | genera | temperate height | polar | tropical |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| high | cirrus, cirrocumulus, cirrostratus | 5–13 km | 3–8 km | 6–18 km |
| middle | altocumulus, altostratus, nimbostratus | 2–7 km | 2–4 km | 2–8 km |
| low | stratus, stratocumulus, cumulus, cumulonimbus | surface–2 km | same | same |

([WMO, cloud definitions, table 6](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/clouds-definitions.html); cumulus and cumulonimbus bases are low but their tops can reach far higher.)

**What each looks like** (WMO definitions):
- **Cumulus:** "detached clouds, generally dense and with sharp outlines," rising in mounds, domes or towers; the top "often resembles a cauliflower"; sunlit parts "mostly brilliant white"; the base "relatively dark and nearly horizontal" ([WMO, cumulus](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/cumulus-cu.html)).
- **Cumulonimbus:** a "heavy and dense cloud, with a considerable vertical extent, in the form of a mountain or huge towers"; the top smooth, fibrous or striated, nearly always flattened, often spreading as an anvil; the base often very dark with ragged low cloud beneath; precipitation sometimes as virga ([WMO, cumulonimbus](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/cumulonimbus-cb.html)).
- **Cirrus:** "white, delicate filaments or white or mostly white patches or narrow bands," with "a fibrous (hair-like) appearance, or a silky sheen, or both" ([WMO, cirrus](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/cirrus-ci.html)).
- **Cirrocumulus:** thin, white, "without shading," of very small grains or ripples, elements under 1° wide ([WMO, cirrocumulus](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/cirrocumulus-cc.html)).
- **Altocumulus:** white or grey patches, sheets or layers "generally with shading," of rounded masses or rolls, elements 1° to 5° wide ([WMO, altocumulus](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/altocumulus-ac.html)).
- **Stratocumulus:** grey or white sheets with dark parts, of rounded masses or rolls, elements more than 5° wide ([WMO, stratocumulus](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/stratocumulus-sc.html)).
- **Stratus:** "generally grey cloud layer with a fairly uniform base," with drizzle or snow grains; the sun's outline is "clearly discernible" through it ([WMO, stratus](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/stratus-st.html)).
- The element widths (under 1°, 1–5°, over 5°) are the WMO's way to tell high, middle and low patterned clouds apart: an outstretched little finger covers about 1° (**judgement**, common rule of thumb).

**Cloud form.** Ruskin: clouds are "neither spongy nor flat"; they are "definite and very beautiful forms of sculptured mist," carved by the surrounding warm air, so their outlines strike "an inexpressible mean between roundness and squareness" and are neither "as solid and hard-edged as a heap of stones tied up in a sack" nor "vague wreaths of mist" ([*The Elements of Drawing*](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.264111/2015.264111.The-Elements_djvu.txt)). Every cloud in a sky follows the same "sweeps of associated curves"; a sky is a single system moved by one wind.

**Optical landmarks** (angles fixed by physics; use them to place things correctly):
- Sun and moon: about 0.5° across (sun 31′–32′; moon 29′–34′) ([Wikipedia, Angular diameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_diameter)); the moon is no larger near the horizon, "your fingernail and the Moon are about the same size" ([NASA, moon illusion](https://science.nasa.gov/science-news/science-at-nasa/2007/27jun_moonillusion)). A painted sun or moon far larger than 1° of the picture's field of view is a deliberate choice, not observation.
- Rainbow: centred on the antisolar point (opposite the sun, below the horizon when the sun is up), radius about 42°, red outside and violet inside; "the sky is darker outside the bow than inside" ([WMO, primary rainbow](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/primary-rainbow.html)). So a rainbow appears only with the sun behind the viewer and lower than 42°.
- 22° halo in cirrostratus, red inner edge; sun dogs about 22° either side of the sun at its elevation; a spread hand at arm's length (thumb on the sun, little finger tip on the halo) measures it ([RMetS, haloes and sun dogs](https://www.rmets.org/metmatters/haloes-and-sun-dogs)).
- Crepuscular rays are parallel; they appear to fan out from the sun only by perspective, and converge again toward the antisolar point (anticrepuscular rays) ([Atmospheric Optics, crepuscular rays](https://old.atoptics.co.uk/atoptics/rayform.htm)).
- The moon's lit side faces the sun; the line joining the horns of a crescent is perpendicular to the direction of the sun (**judgement**, geometry of illumination; the eye often misjudges it, the "moon tilt illusion"). Earthshine dimly lights the dark part of a crescent ([NASA Science, earthshine](https://science.nasa.gov/science-news/science-at-nasa/2005/04oct_leonardo)).

## 2. Seen from different viewpoints
- **Clouds in perspective.** Cumulus bases lie at a common height, so they form a ceiling that recedes toward a vanishing point; distant clouds are not small copies of near ones: they are "more squashed," become "thin horizontal elements," and are spaced closer together toward the horizon; a distant cumulus's base becomes a horizontal ellipse ([John Muir Laws, how to draw clouds in perspective](https://johnmuirlaws.com/how-to-draw-clouds-in-perspective/)).
- **Overhead** you see the dark bases of the nearest clouds; near the horizon you see their sunlit sides and tops (**judgement**, from the ceiling geometry).
- **Into the sun:** clouds are dark masses with brilliant silver rims (thin edges forward-scatter light) (**judgement**). **Away from the sun:** clouds are front-lit and brightest, against the deepest blue.
- **Wide views** compress the upper sky; the horizon band is where most clouds, haze and colour change happen (**judgement**).

## 3. Under light
- **Clouds are white** because droplets scatter all colours about equally; tops and sides are brighter because light is scattered back up and sideways; "cloud bases are often grey as a result of the same scattering that makes them white"; rain clouds are darker because their droplets are bigger ([Met Office, why are clouds white?](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/types-of-weather/clouds/why-are-clouds-white)).
- **Cloud value can't match nature.** "The force of light in clouds" is "wholly unattainable by art," so if a painter puts in as much shade as nature to model them, they become "painfully too dark on the dark sides" (Ruskin). Model clouds with small value steps, and keep the shadow sides lighter than they measure.
- **Cloud shadows are coloured:** blue-grey reflecting the sky on the side away from the sun, warm where lit from below by sunlit ground or by a low sun (**judgement**).
- **Coloured clouds at sunrise and sunset** are mostly the high and middle ones ("spectacular shades of scarlet, orange, and red most often grace cirrus and altocumulus layers"); low clouds are often already in shadow (NOAA SPC, Corfidi).
- **The sky is a light source:** the colour of the open sky goes into every shadow on the ground (see `light-shadow.md`).

## 4. Variation
**Times of day:**
- **Night:** moon and stars; moonlight is sunlight reflected, but it reads blue-grey to the dark-adapted eye (**judgement**).
- **Twilight:** civil (sun 0–6° below the horizon), nautical (6–12°), astronomical (12–18°) ([Wikipedia, Twilight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twilight)); painters call it the "blue hour," with no shadows and objects in silhouette against the lit sky (same source). Opposite the sun: the pink Belt of Venus, about 10–20° above the horizon, above the dark blue-grey band of Earth's shadow, which rises as twilight deepens ([Wikipedia, Belt of Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belt_of_Venus)).
- **Golden hour:** shortly after sunrise or before sunset; light redder and softer, contrast lower, shadows long ([Wikipedia, Golden hour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_hour_(photography))). Low sun is about 1,850–2,000 K, overcast daylight about 7,000 K, clear blue sky 12,000 K and above ([Engineering ToolBox, colour temperature](https://www.engineeringtoolbox.com/color-temperature-d_1776.html)).
- **Midday:** highest contrast, bluest sky, short shadows, the most neutral sunlight (**judgement**).

**Weather:**
- **Clear:** the two-way gradient above. **Haze:** whiter, lower in chroma, horizon dissolved (NOAA SPC).
- **Fair-weather cumulus:** scattered, flat-based, on a common ceiling. **Building cumulus to cumulonimbus:** towers, an anvil, a dark base, rain shafts or virga ([UCAR, clouds in art](https://scied.ucar.edu/sites/default/files/media/documents/art_clouds_scied.pdf), on Constable's *Seascape Study with Rain Cloud*).
- **Overcast stratus:** a nearly uniform grey dome, slightly lighter toward the zenith or the sun's position, soft shadowless light on the ground (**judgement**).
- **Smoke-laden sky:** a red sun at the horizon and orange when high; greenish-yellow sky under forest-fire smoke ([WMO, smoke](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/smoke.html)).
- **Wind:** clouds stretched, streaked and aligned; cirrus "mares' tails" (**judgement**).

## 5. How painters observe and construct it
1. **Decide the sun's position** (even off-canvas) and the hour; all gradients, cloud lighting and ground shadows follow from it.
2. **Lay the clear sky's two gradients** first (toward the horizon and toward the sun), with at least four mixtures (Gurney).
3. **Choose the cloud types and their levels;** keep low cumulus on one receding ceiling, high cloud as thin texture above.
4. **Mass each cloud** with a lit side, a shadow side and a base; small value steps (Ruskin's warning).
5. **Edges:** firm on sunlit cumulus tops against blue, soft at the bases and in cirrus; Ruskin: "the lighter wreaths and flakes of the upper sky cannot be outlined; they can only be sketched, like locks of hair."
6. **Work fast and whole.** Clouds move: "if you are not able to recollect … precisely the form and position of all the clouds at a given moment, you cannot draw the sky at all"; sketch the whole range at speed, or draw a single cloud, which may stay steady five or six minutes; practise on lumps of cotton (Ruskin).
7. **Constable's cloud studies** (dated and annotated with time and wind) are the classic example of systematic sky observation ([UCAR, clouds in art](https://scied.ucar.edu/sites/default/files/media/documents/art_clouds_scied.pdf)).

## 6. Common mistakes
- **Flat blue sky,** one colour from zenith to horizon; or a gradient in only one direction; or a sky darker at the horizon on a clear day.
- **Clouds as cotton balls:** round, hard-edged, the same size everywhere, no flat bases, no perspective (Ruskin's "flour sacks"), or the opposite fault, vague smears with "a little white paper between dashes of blue."
- **Clouds too dark on their shadow side** for a sunny day.
- **Mixed-up physics:** clouds lit from a different side than the ground; a rainbow with the sun in front of the viewer, or with the colours reversed; crepuscular rays fanning from a point other than the sun; a crescent moon whose lit side faces away from the sun, or a crescent rising at midnight.
- **Giant sun or moon** painted as observation.
- **Every cloud a different type at random,** with no common wind or level.
- **The sky not agreeing with the land:** blue-sky shadows under an overcast sky, or warm sunset light on the ground with a midday-blue sky.

## 7. Checks for Teodor
1. The clear sky darkens and saturates away from the sun and lightens toward the horizon; near the sun it is lighter, warmer and duller.
2. Low clouds share a base height and shrink, flatten and crowd toward the horizon; sunlit tops are brightest and bases grey; shadow sides are lighter than nature would measure.
3. Cloud types sit at their WMO levels and agree with one wind and one weather.
4. Sun and moon are about 0.5° of the field of view; the moon's lit side faces the sun.
5. Any rainbow, halo or ray pattern obeys its geometry: rainbow 42° around the antisolar point with red outside; halo 22° around the sun; rays converging on the sun or the antisolar point.
6. The ground's light colour, shadow colour and contrast match the sky's hour and weather.
