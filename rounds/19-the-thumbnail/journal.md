# Journal, round 19: Man and the Moon (graphite hyperrealism, vellum)

## Phase 1, planning

**Habits read, past work looked at.** The contact sheet is a row of small people seen from behind in dark rooms and dusks, then a turn to loud colour with mask faces. This drawing breaks, on purpose:
1. *The lone figure from behind, in the middle distance*: the man's face is a few centimetres from the lens, near three-quarter, and his expression (one eye clenched, a closed-mouth grin) carries the feeling.
2. *Eye-level, middle-distance, centred*: the camera lies in the grass beside his head, close and low, with a wide lens; the head is cropped hard by the lower-left corner and the picture runs on a diagonal.
3. *A hushed, melancholy, bittersweet mood; one warm light in a dark room*: a bright afternoon in the open, a playful feeling (sly delight), mid-to-high key; no dusk, no lamp, no doorway.
4. *Mannequin faces and tube hands*: the hand is the focal plane, built from a tonal study with knuckles, creases and nail; the face an individual man of about 45 with stubble and eye-corner lines, from a tonal study.
5. *The radial burst / the centre*: no central light; the moon is a small hard white shape at the end of a diagonal, off centre.
Risk I keep: the raised arm (rounds 12, 13, 16, 17). Here it is not a fling but a precise sighting arm, straight and still; the hand, not the arm, is the gesture. Watch it.

**Research.**
- Style doc and calibration: the hyperrealist works from a photograph, and the photograph's devices carry the feeling: depth of field, one light, a close crop, lens perspective. Graphite's darks stop near L* 36 (carbon near 32), so a low-key night picture would sit in the medium's weakest range; a daylight picture with a mid-grey sky and a white moon sits where graphite is strongest. A drawing of a moon at night would also be the postcard.
- emotion.md: posture first, then key. Joy is muted by the art frame, so paint it bigger than seems needed: the clenched eye and bunched cheek must be unmistakable, with the mouth closed (my open-red-laugh habit). Viewers name the loudest half of a mixed feeling, so the quiet half (the size of the moon, the moon not gone) gets its own form: the deep sky and the one hard white shape. Awe wants vastness and something that does not fit: the moon is visibly there where he believes he has hidden it.
- Other currents (web): the Japanese woodblock moon series (One Hundred Aspects of the Moon, nineteenth century) shows the moon through a window, reflected in water, or with something near placed in front of it (autumn flowers before the full moon): the near object overlapping the far moon. I take that near/far device into the photograph's lens. The Romantic moon-gazer seen from behind is what I refuse.

**Thumbnails** (thumbs.png, eight structures): A side view at grass level, B over the shoulder, C face filling the frame with the moon only in the eye, D the moon in his glasses, E his face over a water barrel with the moon below, F the hand alone, G a small man under a big sky, H from the crown. C and D lose the moon as a speck; E is the reflection postcard and dark; F has no person and is the thumbs-up sign; G is my old habit; H turns the face upside down (faces read poorly inverted). **Chosen: A**, the face low left, the arm on the diagonal, the moon high right.

**Value studies** (colour_studies.png, graphite: values only): 1 sun from the right front, filtered sky; 2 backlit, face in shade; 3 deeper zenith with cirrus and a lit face. 2 is lower key and the moon drowns in a light sky: rejected. **3 chosen**: the darker zenith (as a yellow-filtered black-and-white photograph renders blue sky) makes the white moon the strongest small contrast; thin cirrus keep the sky from being an empty block. Lesson from the studies: drawn as a stick, the thumb reads as a pointing finger or a thumbs-up; the fist and the nail must be real, and the face larger.

**The reference (geometry found by testing the 3D scene).** Physics decides where the moon can be: seen from anywhere but his own eye, the moon does not sit beside his thumb; the parallax is the camera's offset from his eye over the 60 cm to the thumb. What is true instead: the moon lies on the vanishing point of every line parallel to his line of sight, so in the photograph the line from his open eye through the thumb, extended, runs straight to the moon. That is the composition: eye, thumb and moon on one diagonal, the thumb a short way along it, the moon at its end, small and white and plainly not hidden. A test render from behind his crown (plan/r/v1.png) puts the moon there but turns the face upside down and hides the arm; a side view at 40-50 degrees cannot hold the face and the moon at once. The plan: a wide lens (about 65-75 degrees, a close 24-28 mm photograph: lens perspective is part of the current) beside his head on the face side, slightly above; the moon lower in the sky (late afternoon, about 35-40 degrees up, toward his feet) with his neck flexed to look along his body. The renderer has no one-eyed wink, so the face is built from the render's planes plus a tonal study with the near eye clenched.

**Focal point:** the thumbnail and the moon beyond it on the same diagonal (upper right third); the hardest edges are the thumbnail's edge and the moon's lit limb. Secondary: the clenched eye and bunched cheek (lower left), slightly soft.

**Expectation ledger (planned).** The drawing teaches: soft photographic tone everywhere, mid-grey sky, a diagonal from lower left to upper right, everything near the lens blurred. The one break: the moon's lit edge, the only pure paper-white (electric eraser, or reserved) with the hardest edge, small, far up the diagonal; the thing he thinks he has put out is the sharpest thing in the picture.

**Reference geometry, settled (to build in the next session, when the studio is refreshed).** Three more test renders and a camera search (plan/solve_cam.py, plan/wide_search.py; plan/r/v1.png, v2.png) give the rule: a face turned to the camera and the moon it sights cannot share an ordinary lens, because the face looks along the line to the moon; the camera must look between the two. The working answer: (1) his head turned about 35 degrees toward the camera off the sightline, the open far eye rolled to sight past the thumb; (2) camera low beside his right shoulder, on the face side, about 0.4-0.5 m from the head, never behind the crown (that view turns the face upside down: v1, v2); (3) a wide lens, about 75-80 degrees vertical; (4) the moon about 35-40 degrees up, toward his feet, so his neck flexes a little to look along his body. In the frame the face lies on its side, crown toward the left edge, in the lower left; the arm rises on the diagonal; the moon sits up and right at the end of the eye-thumb line. A sideways face reads far better than an inverted one. Render final at render_scale 1.5-2 for the face and hand, then make tonal studies for the clenched near eye (the renderer has no one-eyed wink), the grin and the fist-and-thumb, and set them as the references for those regions. Sheet 2400 x 3200 vellum, as the calibration advises.

## Phase 1, continued (after planning)

**Read before drawing.** feeling.md, this journal, subjects.json; layer 1 and layer 2 pages for face, hands-feet, figure, sex-age, hair, drapery, trees-plants, sky, moon, light-shadow, space; the MANUAL (scene people, detail pass, references, layers), graphite.md, the style doc.

**Research flags weighed (the research wins, and here it agrees with me once I look):**
- *Daytime moon brightness.* A day moon is sunlit asphalt, 1.1-1.7x the sky beside it. Under the orange filter the sky drops and the moon keeps its value, so the gap opens to about 15-30 L*. The order stays lit skin >= moon > sky. Schema: sky by the moon L* 43, moon highlands 74, the lit thumb up to 86. The moon is the brightest thing in the sky, not on the sheet. My planning ledger said "the only pure paper-white": that was wrong and is withdrawn. Its break is now its hardness and its smallness, not its whiteness.
- *Aperture.* f/16, not a shallow focus: at f/2.8 the moon's blur is larger than the moon. At f/16 the zone of focus is 0.4-1.2 m: the fist (0.68 m) and a few small blades on the ground beyond the arm are sharp; the face at 0.25 m is softened by about 1 mm on the sheet (computed for the 19 mm-equivalent lens of the solved camera); the blades a few cm from the lens are broad blurred bands. No sharp blade among the near ones.
- *Cast shadows.* The low sun (18 degrees, behind his head and to his right) gives sharp cast shadows on the hand; softness comes from round forms and blur, never from soft penumbras on the hand.
- *Sky.* Top of the frame L* 43-45 (orange filter, not red: a red-filter near-black field is beyond graphite over a large area), horizon L* 70, carbon over graphite only where it is darkest. Cirrus are lifted, not drawn, 4-6 L* above the sky.
- *Face texture.* Hierarchy: crow's feet and the bunched cheek as soft forms, stubble as a zone; no pores (and the face is behind a 1 mm blur anyway); grey sclera; the far eye's catchlight reserved; no indents in the face, because it is out of the focal plane and an indent is crisp. The fist gets the sharpest edges, planned: thumbnail's free edge and the lit rim of the thumb, reserved before tone.
- *Darks stop at L* 36-41 (graphite) and 32 (carbon).* The schema's darkest masses (hair, near grass, the gaps of the fist) are set at L* 22-30 and will land at the carbon floor; nothing large is planned below L* 33.

**References** (refs/refs.json, refs/sheet.jpg; Met and Cleveland open access, CC0/public domain). Hand fist thumb up: six drawn studies, six sources; the most useful is a 19th-century clenched fist seen from the knuckles (the arc of the four knuckle heads, the dark occlusion under the curl, the cords at the wrist) and an 18th-century loosely curled hand rising out of a cuff (creases pointing to the knuckle, the cuff's folds). Face: a 19th-century photograph of natural laughter (crow's feet fanning, the eye narrowed from below by the cheek, the nasolabial fold curving round the lifted cheek), a photographed laughing cast (the cheek balled under the eye), two drawn men's heads (the brow ridge over a deep socket; the far socket and the nose overlapping the far cheek in three-quarter). Studied for structure only: my man is invented, from the mesh and these structures, no one's face.

**The reference, rebuilt on the human mesh.** The near eye could not be shut alone on the mesh, so in my own copy of the studio I let an action unit take a side ("43_r": one eye closed), additively. Then a camera solver (plan/m/solve.py): his open (far, left) eye and the moon placed at chosen points in the frame, the sightline at 38 degrees up, 20 degrees to his left of his body; for each camera position it finds the field of view, reports how frontally the face is seen and where the crown points. The geometry is unforgiving: face-view angle plus the eye's roll is fixed near 100 degrees by the lens, so I took a 3/4-to-profile face (about 60 degrees from frontal, eyes rolled about 45 degrees toward the moon), crown toward the left edge, the camera 0.28 m from his face at grass level, a 86-degree vertical field (about a 19 mm lens). The left arm (the far one) raises the fist; the right arm would have hidden the face. Sun at azimuth -150, 18 degrees: behind his head and to his right, so the moon (elongation 123 degrees) is a waxing gibbous, 77% lit, lit limb up toward the sun, and the hand is lit from behind: we see its shadow side and a lit rim. The value schema (plan/m/schema.py, schema1.png) translates the render into an orange-filtered black-and-white photograph at f/16: sky remapped to L* 43-70 with three faint cirrus, a true-size moon (27 px across) with its phase and two faint maria, a distant treeline as a soft dark band, a chambray shirt (mid-grey, so the face and fist are the lights), near grass as blurred bands by distance and small sharp blades at the hand's distance. Eleven scene versions and one schema.

**Habits, checked again against the new composition.** A face rotated onto its side recalls the "tilted top-down view with the figure rotated across the frame" (13, 14, 18). It is not a top-down view: the camera lies in the grass beside him with the horizon in frame, so the rotation is the lens's, not a design tilt. Watch it.

**Focal point** (1318, 1172): the thumbnail and the lit rim of the thumb. Elements declared: fist and thumb, face, moon, raised arm.

## Phase 2, development

**Read before drawing:** feeling.md, this journal, handoff.md, critique_1.md and the studio manager's note.

**Critique 1, recorded.** The critic felt "a foggy, dreamlike loneliness", "someone hitching a ride toward something far away": one of my nearest wrong feelings (the generic thumbs-up, here a hitchhiker) with the dusk mood I refused. What tipped it: the hand was a bare-paper cutout with no structure (so only the gesture's outline spoke, and that outline says "thumb out"); the face did not read (the clenched eye read as open, the oval rim read as a visor); no darks; airbrush softness everywhere. The painting is wrong, not the critic. The plan, as the studio manager advises: work passage by passage to near-finish, the fist first, then the face, then the arm, then the darks.
