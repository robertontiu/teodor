# Critique 2 (a fresh critic; knew only the commission "The Orange Buffoon: the vanity of power", satire; read crit_2.png)

**1) First three seconds:** A small, pompous man in front of a giant golden frame. It reads as a theatrical, slightly melancholy joke, more pathetic than biting.

**2) What makes me feel that:**
- The tiny round figure. He has a toy crown, a red robe, and an arm raised in triumph. He is dwarfed by a heroic shadow and an empty gilt frame, which is the vanity gag.
- The spotlight and the silhouetted audience make it a stage.
- The warm brown-gold glow makes it feel nostalgic rather than angry.

**3) Weaknesses:**
- **Composition:** The audience band takes up the bottom third. The heads are flat, repetitive cut-outs that draw attention but add nothing. The frame is nearly symmetrical and static.
- **Values:** The shadow is muddy mid-dark rather than dramatic. It doesn't separate from the brown wall, so the main idea (shadow larger than the man) is underplayed. The figure's reds sit too close in value to the warm background.
- **Edges:** The square-dab texture is the same everywhere: frame, wall, shadow. There's no hierarchy between hard and soft edges. The figure's outline is clean while everything else is mush.
- **Drawing/gesture:** The figure is stiff and doll-like. The arm is a tube, the head is an egg with a nub, and the hand on the hip barely reads. The bravado lacks swagger and weight.
- **Color:** The "orange" theme is missing. He's crimson, not orange. The palette is all one warm family, so nothing pops.
- **Finish:** The audience looks pasted on and stylistically foreign, with a vector feel against the impasto.

**4) The one change:** Make the shadow the star. Give it a crisp, dark, near-black silhouette that is clearly grander than the man: taller, puffed chest, bigger crown. Then turn the figure orange against it. That contrast is the satire. Shrink or darken the audience so they don't compete.

(Studio manager's note: the craft points stand, above all the shadow: crisp, near-black, grander than the man. As before, keep orange off his skin and hair; if you want more orange, it can go into his robe or sash and the glare on him, so that he still reads as your own invented ruler, not a likeness of anyone. Weigh the critic's feeling against yours: they felt pathos, not yet the laugh with the cold under it.)
